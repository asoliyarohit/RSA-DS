Start Here: Reasoning game plan

In one line: Reasoning is 30 questions worth 25 marks, a wrong answer costs a quarter mark, and your job is to bank the easy marks first and guess only when the odds are on your side.

Lesson 1 · The numbers and the plan

1Picture this

You are at a mela stall with 30 rings to throw. Each ring that lands scores a little, and each ring that misses costs you a small coin. Smart players throw first at the near pegs, and only gamble on the far pegs when the prize is worth it. The Reasoning section is that stall.

Reasoning 25English 25 · Quant 25 · GA 25 (25 each)

Reasoning is one quarter of Paper 1's 100 marks, packed into 30 questions.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The Phase I online exam is on 15 October 2026 (Thursday). Paper 1 is for all streams: 90 questions, 100 marks.
Paper 1 has four parts: English 20 Q (25 marks), Quantitative Aptitude 20 Q (25), Test of Reasoning 30 Q (25), General Awareness 20 Q (25).
All four parts share one 60-minute timer (the table shows 60 minutes against Paper 1 as a whole). Average: 60 ÷ 90 = about 40 seconds per question across the paper. That average is our calculation.
Marks per Reasoning question = 25 ÷ 30 = 0.8333. The notification does not print this; it is our calculation from the table.
A wrong answer loses marks. In this guide we use a flat −0.25 per wrong answer (your direction for this build).
Official wording: "1/4th of marks assigned to the question". Taken literally that is 0.8333 ÷ 4 = 0.2083 per wrong answer. The call letter settles which one applies. We use −0.25 everywhere, which is the safer (slightly harsher) reading.
There is a separate cut-off for each paper plus an aggregate cut-off. Phase I marks only shortlist you for Phase II.
Reported in third-party and memory-based pages: through 2025 Paper 1 had 80 questions with 20 in Reasoning. In 2026 Reasoning rose to 30. So it carries more weight than before.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Right = +0.8333. Wrong = −0.25. Skipped = 0.
Break-even chance = 0.25 ÷ (0.8333 + 0.25) = 0.2308, about 23%.
Guess only if your chance of being right is above about 23%. Under the 0.2083 reading the break-even is 20%, so 23% is safe under both.

Exam structure and "1/4th of marks" wording: PFRDA 2026 notification, page 6 (fetched, see Sources). Break-even and marks table: derived, Python-checked. Meaning of probability as "ways it can happen ÷ total outcomes": Math Is Fun "Probability" (fetched).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

You attempt 20 questions: 16 right, 4 wrong. Your Reasoning marks?

Step 1. Right: 16 × 0.8333 = 13.33.
Step 2. Wrong: 4 × 0.25 = 1.00.
Step 3. Marks = 13.33 − 1.00 = 12.33. Skipped 10 questions cost nothing.

You can remove 2 of 4 options. Should you guess between the other 2?

Step 1. Chance of being right = 1 in 2 = 0.5, which is above 23%.
Step 2. Expected marks of one guess (the long-run average) = 0.5 × 0.8333 − 0.5 × 0.25 = 0.4167 − 0.125 = +0.29.
Step 3. Positive, so guess. Compare: with all 4 options open it is 0.25 × 0.8333 − 0.75 × 0.25 = +0.02, barely above zero, and not worth the risk of a bad streak.

5The trap

Thought: "Wrong answers only cost a quarter mark, so I should answer everything."
Check: one wrong answer cancels 0.25 ÷ 0.8333 = 0.3 of a right answer. So 3 wrong answers wipe out about 1 right answer (3 × 0.25 = 0.75, nearly the 0.83 you earned).
Blind guessing on 4 options gets about 1 right in 4. Expected result is about +0.02 per question, so almost zero gain with big swings.
Right move: guess only after you have crossed out at least one option you can prove wrong, and only when your chance is above about 23%.

Coding-Decoding

In one line: a code is one rule applied to every letter or word. Find the rule from a short example, test it on two letters, then apply it. Three lessons: shifts and positions, opposite letters and word substitution, conditional and chained rules.

Lesson 1 · Letter shift and position values

In one line: coding means one fixed rule is applied to every letter. Find the rule on one or two letters, then apply it to the rest.

1Picture this

A friend texts you in a “secret” way: every letter is moved one step ahead in the alphabet. You see IFMMP. You step each letter back one: H E L L O. The message was HELLO. That is all coding-decoding is: a rule to code (write the secret form) and the reverse to decode (read it).

H (8)I (9)

One step forward: position 8 becomes position 9.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The English alphabet has 26 letters, A to Z. Position value means the place of a letter: A = 1, B = 2, ... Z = 26. (Convention used in this chapter.)
Learn five anchors: E = 5, J = 10, O = 15, T = 20, Y = 25 (every 5th letter). To find a letter, count from the nearest anchor (for example R: T is 20, so R is two before it, 18).
A coding rule replaces each letter by another using one fixed rule. Decoding means undoing it.
Forward shift +k: move k steps toward Z. CAT with +2: C→E, A→C, T→V, so ECV.
Backward shift −k: move k steps toward A. ECV with −2 gives back CAT.
Wrap-around: after Z comes A again, like a clock after 12 comes 1. So X, Y, Z with +3 give A, B, C. In numbers: new position = old + k, and if that is above 26, subtract 26.
Position-value coding: replace each letter by its number. CAB becomes 3, 1, 2. The “value of a word” usually means the sum: 3 + 1 + 2 = 6.
Changing shifts: the shift can change by place: +1 for the 1st letter, +2 for the 2nd, +3 for the 3rd, and so on.
Finding the rule: compare the first coded letter with its original, then CHECK the second letter. A rule that fits only one letter is just a guess.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Position value: A = 1 ... Z = 26. Anchors: E 5, J 10, O 15, T 20, Y 25.
Shift +k: new position = old + k; if above 26, subtract 26. Shift −k: new = old − k; if below 1, add 26.
Decode a shift +k by applying −k (the opposite move).
A coding rule must send different letters to different letters, otherwise nobody could decode it (one-to-one).

Shift and wrap-around, decoding as the reverse shift: Wikipedia “Caesar cipher”; clock wrap idea: Wikipedia “Modular arithmetic”; 26 letters and vowels: Wikipedia “English alphabet”. Position formula and wrap rule: derived, Python-checked (all 26 letters, shifts 1 to 25).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

In a code, PLANE is written RNCPG. How is TRAIN written?

Step 1. Compare first letters: P (16) → R (18). That is +2.
Step 2. Check the second pair: L (12) → N (14). Also +2. A,N,E: A→C, N→P, E→G. Same rule fits all, so the rule is +2.
Step 3. Apply to TRAIN: T→V, R→T, A→C, I→K, N→P. Answer: VTCKP.

Each letter of BREAD moves +1, +2, +3, +4, +5 in order. Code?

Step 1. B+1 = C. R+2 = T.
Step 2. E+3 = H. A+4 = E.
Step 3. D+5 = I. Answer: CTHEI.

5The trap

Fact: in a code, each letter was moved +2 and the word CAT became ECV.
Wrong move: someone asked to decode ECV applies +2 again and gets GEX.
Right: decoding goes the OTHER way. ECV with −2 gives CAT.
Second trap, wrap-around: Y + 3 is not “28”. Position 25 + 3 = 28, minus 26 = 2, so B.

6Speed method: test the first letter only, then check one more

Elimination routine. (1) Find the shift from the first letter pair. (2) Look at the options: any option whose first letter does not match is out. (3) Confirm the last letter of the survivors. Two letters checked often leaves one option.

Drill: apply the shift on the letter. Say the answer aloud before you tap Show.

Lesson 2 · Opposite letters and word substitution

In one line: the opposite letter is the mirror letter (A↔Z, B↔Y ...), and its position is always 27 minus the original position.

1Picture this

Imagine the 26 letters printed on a strip of paper, then you fold the strip in the middle. A lands on Z, B lands on Y, and M lands on N. That folded partner is the opposite letter. Reading the alphabet backwards from Z gives the same partners.

A24 letters in betweenZ

The fold sits between M (13) and N (14).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Write the alphabet forward on top and backward below: A B C ... Z over Z Y X ... A. Each column is a pair.
Each pair adds up to 27 in position: A 1 + Z 26, B 2 + Y 25, M 13 + N 14.
So the opposite letter of the letter at position p is the letter at position 27 − p. Also called “reverse alphabet” or “mirror letter”.
Opposite is its own undo: the opposite of the opposite is the original letter. So coding and decoding are the same move.
Example: DELHI. D (4) → 23 = W. E (5) → 22 = V. L (12) → 15 = O. H (8) → 19 = S. I (9) → 18 = R. Result WVOSR.
“Position from the right end” of the alphabet: the 7th letter from Z is at position 27 − 7 = 20, which is T.
Careful, two different “reverses”: reversing the ALPHABET swaps each letter with its partner (CAT → XZG). Reversing the WORD only changes the order of the same letters (CAT → TAC).
Word substitution: a made-up language gives each word a code word. Each word has exactly one code word and vice versa (one-to-one). Find a word that is common to two statements: its code is the code word that is common to both.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Opposite letter: position p ↔ 27 − p. A↔Z, B↔Y, ..., M↔N.
Opposite twice = original letter (self-inverse).
Reverse alphabet ≠ reverse word. Do exactly what the question says, in order.
Word substitution: common word → common code word (assuming one word, one code word).

Mirror-alphabet substitution and its self-inverse property: Wikipedia “Atbash”; one-to-one substitution needed for decoding: Wikipedia “Substitution cipher”. Pair sum 27 and the shift/opposite link: derived, Python-checked on all 26 letters. The word-substitution puzzle was solved by checking all 120 ways of matching 5 words to 5 code words.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Write DELHI using the opposite letter for each letter.

Step 1. Positions: D 4, E 5, L 12, H 8, I 9.
Step 2. 27 minus each: 23, 22, 15, 19, 18.
Step 3. Letters: W, V, O, S, R. Answer: WVOSR. Check: opposite of WVOSR gives DELHI back.

In a language, “red hot tea” = “ku pa mo”, “hot green tea” = “mo ra pa”, “tea red cup” = “ku ni mo”. What is the code for “tea”?

Step 1. “tea” appears in all three statements. Which code word appears in all three? Statement 1 has ku, pa, mo. Statement 2 has mo, ra, pa. Statement 3 has ku, ni, mo. Only mo is in all three.
Step 2. So tea = mo. Then “hot” (in statements 1 and 2, besides tea) = pa, and “red” (in 1 and 3, besides tea) = ku.
Step 3. Answer: tea = mo. Checked by trying all 120 matchings: exactly one fits.

5The trap

Rule: “write the opposite letter of each letter”. Word: CAT.
Wrong move: write the letters backwards, TAC. That reverses the WORD, not the alphabet.
Another wrong move: use 26 − p instead of 27 − p. A (1) would give 25 = Y, but A↔Z.
Right: C (3) → 24 = X, A (1) → 26 = Z, T (20) → 7 = G. Answer: XZG. Test: A must go to Z.

6Speed method: the pair sum 27 and the A to M / N to Z trick

Fast way. Letters A to M (1 to 13) pair with N to Z (14 to 26). Learn the pairs in one breath: AZ BY CX DW EV FU GT HS IR JQ KP LO MN. Or use 27 − p. The check that never fails: the two positions add to 27.

Lesson 3 · Conditional and chained codes

In one line: a conditional or chained code is just several simple rules in a row. Write the word, do step 1 under it, step 2 under that, and never skip the order.

1Picture this

At a railway counter the clerk says: “If the ticket is for a sleeper coach, stamp it red. Otherwise, stamp it blue.” You first CHECK the ticket, then pick the stamp. A conditional code works the same way: check the word (or letter), then apply the matching rule. A chain is like a two-stage assembly line: parcel goes through stage 1, then stage 2.

WordStep 1Step 2

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A conditional rule has the form “if (test) then (rule A), otherwise (rule B)”. A vowel is one of A, E, I, O, U; every other letter is a consonant.
Tests can be about the whole word (starts with a vowel? even or odd number of letters?) or about each letter (vowel or consonant? even or odd position?).
Always test the ORIGINAL word, not the half-coded one, unless the question says otherwise.
Chain: apply step 1 to every letter, write the result, then apply step 2 to that result. This is like function composition: do one rule, then the next.
Order matters. Take A with “+2 then opposite”: A→C→X. Take “opposite then +2”: A→Z→B. Different answers (checked for all 26 letters).
Handy link: opposite after a +k shift equals the shift −k after the opposite. So the pair “+2 then opposite” is the same as “opposite then −2”.
Example: BAT with “+2 then opposite”: B→D→W, A→C→X, T→V→E. Result WXE.
Example with a test: rule “starts with a vowel: +1 each letter; otherwise reverse the word”. ORANGE starts with O (vowel) so +1: PSBOHF. MANGO starts with M (consonant) so reverse: OGNAM.
Write the working in rows under the word. Short, tidy rows beat doing it in your head.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Read the test first, decide the branch, then apply only that branch.
Chain: result of step 1 is the input of step 2. Do not swap the steps.
Test the original word unless told to test something else.
+k then opposite = opposite then −k (derived).

Doing one rule and then another (composition) and that the order matters: Wikipedia “Function composition”; vowels A E I O U: Wikipedia “English alphabet”; letter-by-letter substitution: Wikipedia “Substitution cipher”. The +k/opposite identity and order effect: derived, Python-checked (all 26 letters, k from −30 to 30).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Rule: “if the word starts with a vowel, move every letter +1; otherwise write the letters in reverse order.” Code ORANGE.

Step 1. Test: first letter O is a vowel. Branch: +1.
Step 2. O→P, R→S, A→B, N→O, G→H, E→F.
Step 3. Answer: PSBOHF.

Rule: “each letter moves +2 then is changed to its opposite letter.” Code BAT.

Step 1. After +2: B→D, A→C, T→V. Row: DCV.
Step 2. Opposite of each: D(4)→23 W, C(3)→24 X, V(22)→5 E. Row: WXE.
Step 3. Answer: WXE. If you swapped the steps you would get ABI, a wrong option.

5The trap

Rule: “+2, then opposite letter”. Word: BAT.
Wrong move: do the opposite first (B→Y, A→Z, T→G), then +2 (Y→A, Z→B, G→I). You write ABI.
Right: +2 first (DCV), then opposite: WXE.
Second trap: testing the coded word for the condition. Test the word you were GIVEN.

6Speed method: two rows and one-letter elimination

Routine. (1) Write the word. (2) Row 1 = after step 1. (3) Row 2 = after step 2. (4) Compute only the FIRST letter all the way through, then strike out options that do not start with it. (5) Check the last letter on the survivors. Then check the last letter on the survivors.

Drill: do it in your head or on paper, then Show.

Direction and Distance

In one line: draw the walk as (East, North) numbers, count turns clockwise and anticlockwise, and use Pythagoras for any straight-line distance.

Lesson 1 · Compass and turns

In one line: a right turn walks you one step clockwise round the compass (N → E → S → W), a left turn one step back, so count net turns and you know where you face.

1Picture this

You walk out of your school gate and the gate faces East. The main road is to your right, so you turn right and walk down it. Which way are you heading now? South. You just did a compass turn without thinking.

Facing EastRight → South

Left and right belong to the person, not to the map.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A compass has four main directions, called cardinal directions: North, East, South, West. They are 90° apart and go clockwise in that order (N → E → S → W).
Between them sit four more, the intercardinal directions: North-East, South-East, South-West, North-West. Each is 45° from its neighbours.
Clockwise means the way a clock’s hands go. A right turn is clockwise. A left turn is anticlockwise (the opposite way).
Stand facing North. Your right hand points East, your left hand points West, and behind you is South.
Each 90° right turn moves your heading one step along N → E → S → W → N. Each left turn moves one step back.
Two right turns (or two left turns) make an about-turn: you face the opposite way. A right turn then a left turn cancel out.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Order clockwise: N → E → S → W → N (90° each). Eight-point compass: add NE, SE, SW, NW (45° between).
Right turn = clockwise = +1 step of 90°. Left turn = anticlockwise = −1 step.
Net turns = (rights) − (lefts). Net 1 = right, net 2 = about-turn, net 3 = left, net 0 or 4 = same heading.
Angles: heading in degrees clockwise from North (N 0°, E 90°, S 180°, W 270°). Add for a right turn, subtract for left, then wrap around 360°.

Compass points and angles, clockwise order: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Net-turn rule: derived, Python-checked (all sequences of up to 6 turns, from every start heading, compared with step-by-step turning and with a rotation-matrix check).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: Facing South, you turn left, left, right, left. Where do you face?
Step 1. Score each turn: right = +1, left = −1. Turns: −1, −1, +1, −1.
Step 2. Add: −1 −1 +1 −1 = −2. Two net left turns = an about-turn.
Step 3. South reversed is North. Check one step at a time: S → left → E → left → N → right → E → left → N. Same answer.

5The trap

Facing South. You turn right. Which way are you facing?
Wrong thought: “right means East, always”. That gives East.
Test with your own body: if you face the door and the door faces South, your right shoulder points the way the sun sets, West.
Right: a right turn moves one step clockwise from your current heading: S → W. Always start from where you face, never from the map.

6Speed method: net-turn count

Do not redraw the compass for every turn. Score right +1, left −1, add them, take the remainder after dividing by 4, then step that many places clockwise from the start.

Elimination. If the net is 0 or 4 you face the start direction. If the net is 2 the answer is the opposite direction. That alone removes two options before you do anything else.

Say the direction aloud before you tap Show.

Lesson 2 · Walking, coordinates and shortest distance

In one line: keep a running (East, North) score for every leg, then Pythagoras on the final pair gives the straight-line distance.

1Picture this

An auto driver in a grid-planned city goes 3 blocks North and then 4 blocks East. He paid for 7 blocks of road. But a crow flying straight from the start to the end covers only 5 blocks. Road distance and straight-line distance are two different numbers.

Walked: 3 + 4 = 7
Straight: 5

Exams usually ask for the straight-line one (“how far from the starting point?”).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw a plus sign and put the start in the middle: that is the point (0, 0). Call it (East, North).
Moving East adds to the first number, West takes away. Moving North adds to the second number, South takes away.
So every leg changes just ONE of the two numbers. Keep a running pair after each leg.
At the end, the pair (x, y) says where you are. x positive = East of start, y positive = North of start. Both non-zero? Name it by both, for example North-East.
Distance from the start in a straight line: make a right-angled triangle with legs |x| and |y|. Pythagoras: distance = √(x² + y²). (|x| means x with its sign ignored.)
Distance WALKED is different: just add every leg. It is never smaller than the straight-line distance.
To say where the START is from the END, flip both signs: North-East becomes South-West.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Position = (East, North). E: x + · W: x − · N: y + · S: y −.
Straight-line distance = √(x² + y²) (Pythagoras: a² + b² = c², c = hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle).
Distance walked = sum of all legs. Straight-line ≤ walked, equal only if the whole path is a straight line.
Direction of end from start: by signs of x and y (both non-zero = a quadrant name such as North-East). Start from end = flip both signs.
Convention in this book: when both x and y are non-zero we name the quadrant (North-East) even if the angle is not exactly 45°.

Pythagoras theorem, hypotenuse, 3-4-5 / 5-12-13 / 8-15-17, and distance formula: pages in Sources. Triples 6-8-10, 9-12-15, 7-24-25, 20-21-29, 12-16-20, 15-20-25, 10-24-26: derived, Python-checked. Coordinate method: derived, Python-checked (turn and walk simulator).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: Facing North, you walk 15 km, turn right and walk 24 km, turn right and walk 8 km. Distance and direction from the start?
Step 1. Leg 1: facing North, 15 km. Position (East, North) = (0, 15).
Step 2. Right turn from North = East. Leg 2: 24 km East. Position (24, 15).
Step 3. Right turn from East = South. Leg 3: 8 km South. Position (24, 7).
Step 4. East 24 and North 7: you are North-East of the start. Distance √(24² + 7²) = √(576 + 49) = √625 = 25 km. Total walked: 47 km (not the answer).

5The trap

Question: 5 km North then 12 km East. “How far from the start?”
Wrong move: add the legs. 5 + 12 = 17. This is a tempting option in the list.
Why it fails: 17 km is the road you walked, but the start and end are joined by the diagonal of a right-angled triangle.
Right: 5² + 12² = 169, √169 = 13 km. Always ask: walked, or straight-line? Second trap: a “right turn” means right of the way you FACE now, not East.

6Speed method: known triples and cancelling

Lesson 3 · Shadows and two travellers

In one line: a shadow points away from the Sun (morning West, evening East), and two people leaving one point either add, subtract or use Pythagoras for their gap.

1Picture this

It is early morning on the school ground and the Sun is just coming up over the buildings in the East. Look at the flagpole’s shadow: it stretches away from the Sun, towards the West, across the ground.

Sun EastShadow → West

Light on one side, shadow on the other side.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A shadow always falls on the side AWAY from the light. Light from the East gives a shadow pointing West.
Exam convention: at sunrise the Sun is in the East, at sunset in the West. (In real life this is exact only at the equinoxes; questions ignore that.)
So morning shadows point West, evening shadows point East.
Now place the shadow on the person: facing North, right = East and left = West. Facing South, right = West and left = East. Facing East, right = South and left = North. Facing West, right = North and left = South.
Work backwards: shadow on your right in the evening means East is on your right, and East is on the right only when you face North.
Two travellers from one point: opposite directions add the distances, same direction subtract, right angles use Pythagoras.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Shadow points directly away from the Sun.
Convention: sunrise Sun = East, so shadow = West. Sunset Sun = West, so shadow = East.
Facing N: R = E, L = W · Facing S: R = W, L = E · Facing E: R = S, L = N · Facing W: R = N, L = S.
Two travellers from one point: opposite directions → add · same direction → subtract · at right angles → √(a² + b²).

Shadow definition (blocked light) and sunrise near due East: Wikipedia pages in Sources; “shadow points away from the Sun” is a geometric consequence, derived. Facing table and traveller rules: derived, Python-checked (facing table for all headings and sides; traveller rules on 2000 random cases). Morning/evening East/West is an exam convention, not exact for most dates.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: One morning Sita’s shadow falls on her left. Which way is she facing?
Step 1. Morning: Sun East, so shadow points West.
Step 2. The shadow is on her left, so West is on her left.
Step 3. Left = West when you face North (check: facing North, left hand points West). Answer: North.

5The trap

Question: in the morning a pole’s shadow falls towards the East. Which side is the Sun?
Wrong thought: “Sun in the East, so shadow in the East”. That puts the shadow on the Sun’s side.
Shadows point AWAY from the light. The picture only works if the shadow and the Sun are on opposite sides.
Right: a shadow pointing East means the Sun is West, so this is the evening (sunset). Always flip the Sun to get the shadow.

6Speed method: shadow-to-facing in one hop

Shortcut: shadow in front of you means you face the shadow’s direction; shadow behind means you face the Sun’s direction; shadow on your right means you face the direction one step ANTICLOCKWISE from the shadow; on your left means one step CLOCKWISE from the shadow.

Check: morning, shadow West, on your left: one step clockwise from West is North. That matches the worked example.

Safety Net: what to do when you blank

In one line: when a question freezes you, you do not fight it. You run a fixed routine: cross out, decide, move on.

Label: the arithmetic here is checked in Python. The advice (skip rules, time plans, reset steps) is our suggested strategy, not an official fact. Marks used: +0.8333 per right answer, −0.25 per wrong answer (flat, as this guide assumes; the call letter settles the real rule).

Lesson 1 · Elimination and the guess rule

In one line: a guess is only worth making when your chance of being right is above about 23%.

1Picture this

You are in a Pune chai shop. The menu has four snacks and you cannot taste them first. You already know two are stale, so you cross them out. Now you pick between two. That is a fair bet, much better than picking from four. Exam options work the same way: every option you can prove wrong makes the remaining guess better.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Only one option is right. Your job is to cross out options, not to "find" the answer by force.
Process of elimination means finding the answer by excluding the options that cannot be it.
Chance of being right if you guess among n options left = 1 out of n. Four left: 1/4 = 25%. Three left: 1/3 = 33%. Two left: 1/2 = 50%.
Expected value (EV) is the average result if you could repeat the same bet many times: multiply each outcome by its probability and add. Here: EV = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25, where p is your chance of being right.
If EV is above 0, guessing gains marks on average. If it is below 0, it loses marks on average. EV = 0 happens at p = 0.25 ÷ (0.8333 + 0.25), which is about 23%.
So: blank with 4 options, EV is just above zero (+0.02). Cross out even one option and it jumps to +0.11.

3The rule (now that you get it)

EV of a guess = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25
Answer if p is above about 23%. Skip if p is below it.
23% break-even
4 left: 25%
3 left: 33%
2 left: 50%

Expected value as a probability-weighted sum: Wikipedia "Expected value". Elimination definition: Wikipedia "Process of elimination". Penalty-scoring context: Wikipedia "Multiple choice" (SAT Subject tests took a quarter-point off for a wrong answer; random guessing on four options gives about a 25% chance). The 23% break-even and every EV number: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked example, one tap per step

You have crossed out one of four options. Should you guess among the other three?

Step 1. Three options left, so p = 1/3 = 33%.
Step 2. Gain part: 1/3 × 0.8333 = 0.2778.
Step 3. Loss part: 2/3 × 0.25 = 0.1667.
Step 4. EV = 0.2778 − 0.1667 = +0.11. And 33% is above 23%. Answer it.

5The trap

Trap 1: "I will guess everything, it is free." It is not free. A blind guess from 4 options is only +0.02 on average, and from 5 options it is −0.03. One question gains nothing; 20 of them is a coin toss on your score.
Trap 2: "I crossed one out, so I am 50% sure." No. Crossing out one of four leaves three, so 33%.
Trap 3: crossing out an option because it "looks odd" is not elimination. Cross out only what you can prove wrong, for example a number that breaks a rule you have checked.

6Speed method + drill

The 3-second routine: (1) read the question again, (2) cross out every option that breaks a fact you checked, (3) count what is left, then apply the rule: 3 or 2 left, answer; 4 left, only if you feel a pull toward one.

Quick EV table (4-option paper, derived, Python-checked). 4 left: +0.02. 3 left: +0.11. 2 left: +0.29. 5 left (if a paper has 5 options): −0.03.

Lesson 2 · Time: skip rules and the last 10 minutes

In one line: 60 minutes is one shared pot for 90 questions, so a stuck question steals time from easy marks elsewhere.

1Picture this

You are at a railway ticket queue with 40 seconds for each person behind you. One passenger starts arguing about a fare for 3 minutes. Four or five people behind him wait for nothing. A hard question is that passenger: it eats the time of the easy questions behind it.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Paper 1 has 90 questions and one shared 60-minute timer (official notification).
Average time = 60 min × 60 s ÷ 90 = 40 seconds per question.
Reasoning has 30 questions. At the average pace that is 30 × 40 s = 20 minutes of the pot.
Some questions take 15 seconds, some 2 minutes. The average only works if you stop the 2-minute ones from becoming 5-minute ones.
Time-boxing means giving a task a fixed block of time and stopping when it ends. Suggested strategy: after about 90 seconds on one question with no progress, mark it, take your best guess only if the rule from Lesson 1 allows, and move on.
One stalled 3-minute question costs 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5 average questions of time.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Seconds per question = total seconds ÷ questions. Paper 1: 3600 ÷ 90 = 40 s.
Suggested skip rule (strategy): no progress by about 90 seconds, then mark and move on.
Suggested last-10-minutes plan (strategy): (1) go back to marked questions, (2) finish those with 2 or 3 options left, (3) leave 4-option blanks only if you have no pull toward any option.

Paper timing: PFRDA 2026 notification. Time-boxing idea (fixed blocks of focused time): Wikipedia "Time management" (Pomodoro technique). 40 s and 4.5 questions: derived, Python-checked. The 90-second cap and the 10-minute plan are our suggestions, not tested facts.

4Worked example, one tap per step

With 10 minutes left you have 12 unattempted questions. How much time can each get?

Step 1. 10 minutes = 600 seconds.
Step 2. 600 ÷ 12 = 50 seconds each.
Step 3. Split them: if 6 are easy (25 s) and 6 are hard (75 s) it still fits: 150 + 450 = 600.
Step 4. Do the 25-second ones first. They are the same marks for half the time.

5The trap

Trap: "I have already spent 4 minutes, so I must finish this puzzle." That is the sunk cost fallacy: letting time already spent decide a future choice.
The 4 minutes are gone either way. Ask only: what will the next 2 minutes buy here, versus on other questions?
Second trap: skipping and never returning. Mark skipped questions on your rough sheet by number so the last-10-minutes plan can find them.

6Speed method + drill

Three passes (suggested): Pass 1 take every question that starts moving within 10 seconds. Pass 2 return to the marked ones. Pass 3 in the final minutes apply the Lesson 1 rule to what is left.

Lesson 3 · Resetting after a hard puzzle

In one line: a bad question is one question. Reset in 20 seconds and the next one is worth full marks again.

1Picture this

A batter in a local cricket match gets bowled for a duck in the first innings. The second innings still starts at zero. An exam works like that: the next question does not know that the last one went badly, and it is worth the same.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Assumption (stated): every question in a section carries equal marks, as the notification's table suggests. Then a hard puzzle is worth 0.8333, the same as an easy one.
Hard questions cost more minutes for the same marks. A 5-question puzzle set that needs 8 minutes pays 5 × 0.8333 ÷ 8 = 0.52 marks per minute. Ten short questions at 36 s each pay 8.333 ÷ 6 = 1.39 marks per minute.
So when you are stuck, the best move is almost always to go where the marks per minute are higher.
One right answer wipes out a lot: +0.8333 covers three wrong answers (−0.75) with 0.083 to spare. A bad patch is recoverable.
Reset routine (suggested, about 20 seconds): put the pen down, take three slow breaths, say "next question", read it fresh. Slow breathing is described as helping with relaxation, though evidence varies.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Marks per minute = marks available ÷ minutes needed. Spend time where it is higher.
After a stall: pen down, 3 slow breaths, "next question". Strategy, not a fact.
3 wrong answers = −0.75. One right answer = +0.8333. One right puts you back above zero.

Sunk cost: Wikipedia "Sunk cost". Slow breathing and relaxation (with mixed evidence in some uses): Wikipedia "Diaphragmatic breathing". Marks per minute and the −0.75 versus +0.8333 comparison: derived, Python-checked. The 20-second routine is our suggestion, not a tested method.

4Worked example, one tap per step

A 5-question puzzle set needs about 8 minutes. Is it a good use of time early on?

Step 1. Marks available: 5 × 0.8333 = 4.167.
Step 2. Marks per minute: 4.167 ÷ 8 = 0.52.
Step 3. Short questions at 40 s each give 0.8333 ÷ (40 ÷ 60) = 1.25 marks per minute.
Step 4. So take the short ones first, then come back to the set with whatever time is left. (This changes if you are very fast at puzzles. Use your own mock times.)

5The trap

Trap: carrying the last question's mood into the next one. Two wrong in a row make people read slowly and doubt correct answers.
Fix: the marks maths. Two wrong answers cost only −0.50, and one right answer gives back +0.8333.
Second trap: "changing" answers out of panic. Change an answer only if you can name the specific step you got wrong.

6Speed method + drill

When you blank: (1) pen down, (2) three slow breaths, (3) re-read the question word by word, (4) cross out what you can prove wrong, (5) apply the 23% rule, (6) mark it and go. Six steps, about 30 seconds.
