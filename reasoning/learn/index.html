Lesson 1 · The numbers and the plan

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The Phase I online exam is on 15 October 2026 (Thursday). Paper 1 is for all streams: 90 questions, 100 marks.
Paper 1 has four parts: English 20 Q (25 marks), Quantitative Aptitude 20 Q (25), Test of Reasoning 30 Q (25), General Awareness 20 Q (25).
All four parts share one 60-minute timer (the table shows 60 minutes against Paper 1 as a whole). Average: 60 ÷ 90 = about 40 seconds per question across the paper. That average is our calculation.
Marks per Reasoning question = 25 ÷ 30 = 0.8333. The notification does not print this; it is our calculation from the table.
A wrong answer loses marks. In this guide we use a flat −0.25 per wrong answer (your direction for this build).
Official wording: "1/4th of marks assigned to the question". Taken literally that is 0.8333 ÷ 4 = 0.2083 per wrong answer. The call letter settles which one applies. We use −0.25 everywhere, which is the safer (slightly harsher) reading.
There is a separate cut-off for each paper plus an aggregate cut-off. Phase I marks only shortlist you for Phase II.
Reported in third-party and memory-based pages: through 2025 Paper 1 had 80 questions with 20 in Reasoning. In 2026 Reasoning rose to 30. So it carries more weight than before.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Right = +0.8333. Wrong = −0.25. Skipped = 0.
Break-even chance = 0.25 ÷ (0.8333 + 0.25) = 0.2308, about 23%.
Guess only if your chance of being right is above about 23%. Under the 0.2083 reading the break-even is 20%, so 23% is safe under both.

Exam structure and "1/4th of marks" wording: PFRDA 2026 notification, page 6 (fetched, see Sources). Break-even and marks table: derived, Python-checked. Meaning of probability as "ways it can happen ÷ total outcomes": Math Is Fun "Probability" (fetched).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

You attempt 20 questions: 16 right, 4 wrong. Your Reasoning marks?

Step 1. Right: 16 × 0.8333 = 13.33.
Step 2. Wrong: 4 × 0.25 = 1.00.
Step 3. Marks = 13.33 − 1.00 = 12.33. Skipped 10 questions cost nothing.

You can remove 2 of 4 options. Should you guess between the other 2?

Step 1. Chance of being right = 1 in 2 = 0.5, which is above 23%.
Step 2. Expected marks of one guess (the long-run average) = 0.5 × 0.8333 − 0.5 × 0.25 = 0.4167 − 0.125 = +0.29.
Step 3. Positive, so guess. Compare: with all 4 options open it is 0.25 × 0.8333 − 0.75 × 0.25 = +0.02, barely above zero, and not worth the risk of a bad streak.

5The trap

Thought: "Wrong answers only cost a quarter mark, so I should answer everything."
Check: one wrong answer cancels 0.25 ÷ 0.8333 = 0.3 of a right answer. So 3 wrong answers wipe out about 1 right answer (3 × 0.25 = 0.75, nearly the 0.83 you earned).
Blind guessing on 4 options gets about 1 right in 4. Expected result is about +0.02 per question, so almost zero gain with big swings.
Right move: guess only after you have crossed out at least one option you can prove wrong, and only when your chance is above about 23%.

Inequalities (direct and coded)

In one line: letters stand for numbers, and you only ever ask "is this true in every possible case?" Three lessons: chains, either-or, coded symbols.

Lesson 1 · Direct chains: can I walk from P to R?

In one line: letters stand for numbers, and a chain like P > Q ≥ R lets you walk from the left end to the right end only while the arrows all point the same way.

1Picture this

Four friends in a Pune hostel compare heights. Ravi is taller than Sunil. Sunil is at least as tall as Tara. Is Ravi taller than Tara? Yes: you walked down two steps, and one step was a strict "taller".

Ravi
Sunil
Tara

Ravi › Sunil ≥ Tara. Tara could equal Sunil, but never beat Ravi.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Each letter is a hidden number (marks, heights, ages). We do not know the numbers, only how they compare.
Symbols: > means bigger than, < means smaller than, = means same, ≥ means bigger or equal ("at least"), ≤ means smaller or equal ("at most"). The small end of the symbol points at the smaller number.
A chain is a row like P > Q ≥ R. It is two facts glued together: P > Q and Q ≥ R.
Same direction: you can walk the whole chain. P > Q ≥ R means P is above R, so P > R.
One strict link makes the whole walk strict. P ≥ Q ≥ R only gives P ≥ R. P > Q ≥ R gives P > R.
An = link is invisible: P > Q = R gives P > R, as if Q were not there.
Direction change stops the walk. P < Q > R: Q is the biggest, but P and R can be in any order. No relation.
A conclusion definitely follows only if it is true for every possible set of numbers that fit the statements. One counter-example kills it.
Strong implies weak: P > R also means P ≥ R and P ≠ R. But P ≥ R does not give P > R.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Same direction chain: the ends are related. Direction change: the ends are NOT related (not definite).
Strict wins: if any link is > (or <), the ends are strict. If all links are ≥ (or all ≤), the ends are ≥ (or ≤). If all links are =, the ends are =.
= links are invisible: they take on the neighbour's relation.
Sign-combine table (walking left to right): > then > = >; > then ≥ = >; ≥ then ≥ = ≥; = then X = X.

Why it works: < and ≤ are transitive, and ≠ is not (Wikipedia: Transitive relation); the symbol meanings are on the Math Is Fun and Wikipedia inequality pages. The sign-combine table and the 'direction change gives no relation' rule: derived, Python-checked (all 25 symbol pairs, every possible ordering of 3 letters).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

P > Q ≥ R = S. Is P > S definitely true?

Step 1. Walk from P: P > Q (down). Q ≥ R (down). R = S (flat). All the same direction, so we can walk.
Step 2. The = is invisible, so ignore it: P > Q ≥ S.
Step 3. One strict link (P > Q) makes the walk strict. P > S. Yes, definitely true.
Check: P ≥ S is also true (strong implies weak), but S ≥ P is definitely false.

H ≥ J; J > K; L ≤ K. Is H > L definitely true?

Step 1. The statements are separate. Find the shared letters: J and K link them.
Step 2. Flip L ≤ K so it points the same way: K ≥ L.
Step 3. Line up: H ≥ J > K ≥ L. All point down.
Step 4. One strict link (J > K) so H > L. Yes, definitely true.

5The trap

The chain is P ≤ Q ≥ R. Wrong thought: "Q is big, so P ≥ R, or at least P ≤ R."
Try numbers. P = 0, Q = 2, R = 1: everything fits, and P is smaller than R.
Now P = 1, Q = 2, R = 0: everything fits, and P is bigger than R.
So neither P ≤ R nor P ≥ R is certain. Both arrows point towards Q (or both away): no walk. Mark it not definite.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Rewrite every statement so all arrows point the same way (flip the ‹ and ≤ ones).
  2. Stack them into one line using the shared letters.
  3. For each conclusion: are its two letters on one same-direction stretch? If not, cross it out.
  4. If yes: does it claim strict (›) but the stretch has only ≥? Cross out. Does it claim = but a link is not =? Cross out. Opposite direction? Cross out.

Drill: the relation of P to R after walking the chain.

Lesson 2 · Two conclusions: only I, only II, either-or, neither, both

In one line: test each conclusion alone for "always true"; if neither is, check whether together they cover every case (either-or).

1Picture this

Two cricketers, Asha and Bela, have unknown scores. Someone offers two claims: I, "Asha scored more than Bela"; II, "Asha scored at most what Bela scored". You do not know the scores. Is one of the two claims certainly true? Yes: either Asha is ahead, or she is not. One of the two must hold.

Asha
Bela

I: Asha › Bela. II: Asha ≤ Bela. Between them they cover every case.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Take conclusion I alone. Is it true in every case? If yes, I follows. Do the same for II. Use the chain walk from Lesson 1.
If I and II both follow: both. If only one does: only I or only II.
If neither follows, do not stop. Look at the letters: do I and II talk about the same two letters?
If yes, ask: could both be false at once? The three possible cases for A and B are A > B, A = B, A < B.
I: A > B covers one case. II: A ≤ B covers the other two. Together all three cases are covered, so one is always true: either I or II follows.
If some case makes both false (A = B makes both A > B and A < B false), the answer is neither.
Order of checks: definite first, either-or second, neither last. Either-or is only for when neither is definite on its own.

3The rules (now that you get it)

A conclusion follows only if it is true in every case.
Either-or: neither conclusion follows alone, both talk about the same two letters, and in every case at least one of them is true.
Complementary pairs (checked, with no statement linking the two letters): < with ≥, > with ≤, and ≤ with ≥.
NOT either-or when the letters are unlinked: > with < (both fail when equal), > with = (both fail when A < B), > with ≥ (both fail when A < B).
Linked case: if the statements give A ≥ B, then I. A > B and II. A = B is either-or (≥ leaves only those two cases). Always test "can both be false in some case that fits the statements?"

4Worked examples, one tap per step

P ≥ Q; R > Q. I. P > R II. P ≤ R

Step 1. P ≥ Q and R > Q: both P and R sit above Q, so P and R are not linked.
Step 2. I alone: P > R can fail (P = R). II alone: P ≤ R can fail (P > R). Neither follows.
Step 3. Same letters P and R. I is > and II is ≤: a complementary pair.
Step 4. Either I or II follows.

A > B = C ≥ D. I. A > D II. B ≥ D

Step 1. Walk from A: A > B, B = C, C ≥ D. All the same direction. A > D: I follows.
Step 2. B = C ≥ D, so B ≥ D: II follows.
Step 3. Both follow. Do not use either-or here: it is only for when neither follows alone.

5The trap

Statements: F > G ≥ H. I. F > H II. H ≥ F. Wrong move: "same letters, looks like a pair, so either-or".
Test I alone: F > G ≥ H, so F > H. I follows.
Test II: H ≥ F is the opposite of what the chain says. False in every case.
Answer: only I. Either-or is the last test, never the first.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Chain-walk each conclusion first. Mark it definite, false or not definite.
  2. One or both definite: the answer is only I, only II or both.
  3. Neither definite: are the letters the same two, and is the pair ‹ and ≥, › and ≤, or ≤ and ≥? Then either-or. Also either-or: the statements give A ≥ B and the pair is A › B with A = B.
  4. Otherwise: neither.

Lesson 3 · Coded inequalities: decode first, then solve

In one line: a coded question swaps the usual symbols for @, #, $, % and &; the question gives a key, you translate every symbol, then it is Lessons 1 and 2 again.

1Picture this

Your friend texts in code: "Ravi @ Sunil". Before you can read it you need the code book: say @ means "taller than". Once decoded it is plain English. Coded inequalities are a code book plus a chain.

@ means >
# means ≥

Your job: translate first. Never think in the code.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The question prints a key: for example "A @ B means A > B". Symbols like @, #, $, % and & are just labels with no meaning of their own.
The key changes from question to question. @ can be > in one question and ≤ in the next.
Step 1: write the key on rough paper as five rows: symbol, then the real sign.
Step 2: rewrite each coded statement with real signs, one symbol at a time, keeping the letter order.
Step 3: solve as before: walk the chain, test each conclusion, use either-or last.
Step 4: decode the conclusions the same way, then compare.
Reversed pair: "O & M" with & meaning < says O < M. Keep the letter order; do not swap.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Decode symbol by symbol: A @ B uses the key sign between A and B.
A coded statement with three letters, like P @ Q # R, is a chain: P > Q ≥ R if @ is > and # is ≥.
After decoding, all the rules of Lessons 1 and 2 apply unchanged.

Coded inequality is a user-defined symbol mapping: the question's key rules, not memory. Decode-then-solve: derived, Python-checked (every quiz and drill item is decoded by code and solved by enumeration).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Key: @ is >, # is ≥, $ is =, % is ≤, & is <. Statements: P @ Q # R; S $ R. Conclusion: P @ S

Step 1. Decode P @ Q # R: P > Q ≥ R. Decode S $ R: S = R.
Step 2. S = R, so S can stand in for R: P > Q ≥ S.
Step 3. One strict link, so P > S. The conclusion P @ S decodes to P > S.
Step 4. Definitely true.

Key: # is <, % is ≥, @ is >, $ is =, & is ≤. Statements: K # L; L & M. Conclusions: I. K % M II. M @ K

Step 1. K # L is K < L. L & M is L ≤ M. Chain: K < L ≤ M, so K < M.
Step 2. I: K % M decodes to K ≥ M. False in every case.
Step 3. II: M @ K decodes to M > K, the same as K < M. True.
Step 4. Only II follows.

5The trap

A habit trap: you memorised "@ is greater than" from one question and use it in the next, where @ is ≤.
Always rewrite the key first. Never decode from memory.
Second trap: reversing the letters. M @ K with @ as > is M > K, not K > M.
Check every decoded line once: letter order untouched, only the symbol swapped.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Copy the key as five short rows.
  2. Decode every symbol in place, left to right.
  3. Lessons 1 and 2: flip, line up, walk.

Syllogism

In one line: a syllogism question asks what MUST be true when a few statements about groups are true, and the answer comes from drawing circles, never from common sense.

Lesson 1 · The four statements and reversing them

In one line: learn what All, No, Some and Some-not mean as pictures, then learn which swaps are safe.

1Picture this

In your hostel, a notice says: “All hostel students must carry an ID card.” Draw one big circle for ID-card holders and a small circle for hostel students inside it.

ID-card holdershostel students sit inside

Can you say “all ID-card holders are hostel students”? No. Visitors carry ID cards too. That one thought is half of syllogism.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Think of each group as a circle (a set). A statement tells you how two circles sit.
All pens are books: the pens circle is fully inside the books circle.
No pens are books: the circles are apart. They do not touch.
Some pens are books: the circles overlap, with at least one item in the overlap. “Some” means at least one, maybe all.
Some pens are not books: at least one pen sits outside the books circle.
Rule of the game: treat the statements as true, even if real life disagrees (a pen can be a book here).
Rule of the game: every group named has at least one member (no empty circles).
A conclusion definitely follows only if it is true in EVERY picture the statements allow. One legal picture that breaks it means it does not follow.
Reverse means swapping the two groups. No and Some swap freely. All swaps only into a weaker Some. Some-not does not swap at all.

Lesson 3 · Possibility, either-or and “only a few”

In one line: Definite needs every picture, Possibility needs one, and Either-or covers two conclusions where one must be true.

1Picture this

Rahul has a ticket for a match on either Saturday or Sunday. Is it Saturday? Not certain. Is it Sunday? Not certain. Is it one of the two? Certain. That is either-or: neither alone is sure, but one of them must be true.

Saturday?Sunday?

Compare: “Could it be Saturday?” is a possibility. One imaginable picture is enough.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Three verdicts for a conclusion: Definite (true in every picture), Possible only (true in some pictures), Never (true in none).
A conclusion that starts with “Possibility that …” follows if the verdict is Definite or Possible only. It fails only if it is Never.
Fast test for possibility: it fails only when its opposite definitely follows (All vs Some-not, No vs Some).
Either-or: two conclusions about the same two groups. Neither is definite, but in every picture at least one is true.
Pairs that work: Some A are B / No A is B. All A are B / Some A are not B. Some A are B / Some A are not B.
Pair that does NOT work: All A are B / No A is B. Both can be false (some are, some are not).
Only a few A are B (our own convention, not a textbook term): some A are B AND some A are not B. It gives Some A are B, Some B are A, Some A are not B. It does not give Some B are not A.
Order of checking: test each conclusion for Definite. If neither, test either-or. If still no, answer Neither.

Coding-Decoding

In one line: a code is one rule applied to every letter or word. Find the rule from a short example, test it on two letters, then apply it. Three lessons: shifts and positions, opposite letters and word substitution, conditional and chained rules.

Lesson 1 · Letter shift and position values

In one line: coding means one fixed rule is applied to every letter. Find the rule on one or two letters, then apply it to the rest.

1Picture this

A friend texts you in a “secret” way: every letter is moved one step ahead in the alphabet. You see IFMMP. You step each letter back one: H E L L O. The message was HELLO. That is all coding-decoding is: a rule to code (write the secret form) and the reverse to decode (read it).

H (8)I (9)

One step forward: position 8 becomes position 9.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The English alphabet has 26 letters, A to Z. Position value means the place of a letter: A = 1, B = 2, ... Z = 26. (Convention used in this chapter.)
Learn five anchors: E = 5, J = 10, O = 15, T = 20, Y = 25 (every 5th letter). To find a letter, count from the nearest anchor (for example R: T is 20, so R is two before it, 18).
A coding rule replaces each letter by another using one fixed rule. Decoding means undoing it.
Forward shift +k: move k steps toward Z. CAT with +2: C→E, A→C, T→V, so ECV.
Backward shift −k: move k steps toward A. ECV with −2 gives back CAT.
Wrap-around: after Z comes A again, like a clock after 12 comes 1. So X, Y, Z with +3 give A, B, C. In numbers: new position = old + k, and if that is above 26, subtract 26.
Position-value coding: replace each letter by its number. CAB becomes 3, 1, 2. In this chapter the “value of a word” means the sum: 3 + 1 + 2 = 6.
Changing shifts: the shift can change by place: +1 for the 1st letter, +2 for the 2nd, +3 for the 3rd, and so on.
Finding the rule: compare the first coded letter with its original, then CHECK the second letter. A rule that fits only one letter is just a guess.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Position value: A = 1 ... Z = 26. Anchors: E 5, J 10, O 15, T 20, Y 25.
Shift +k: new position = old + k; if above 26, subtract 26. Shift −k: new = old − k; if below 1, add 26.
Decode a shift +k by applying −k (the opposite move).
A coding rule must send different letters to different letters, otherwise nobody could decode it (one-to-one).

Lesson 2 · Opposite letters and word substitution

In one line: the opposite letter is the mirror letter (A↔Z, B↔Y ...), and its position is always 27 minus the original position.

1Picture this

Imagine the 26 letters printed on a strip of paper, then you fold the strip in the middle. A lands on Z, B lands on Y, and M lands on N. That folded partner is the opposite letter. Reading the alphabet backwards from Z gives the same partners.

A24 letters in betweenZ

The fold sits between M (13) and N (14).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Write the alphabet forward on top and backward below: A B C ... Z over Z Y X ... A. Each column is a pair.
Each pair adds up to 27 in position: A 1 + Z 26, B 2 + Y 25, M 13 + N 14.
So the opposite letter of the letter at position p is the letter at position 27 − p. Also called “reverse alphabet” or “mirror letter”.
Opposite is its own undo: the opposite of the opposite is the original letter. So coding and decoding are the same move.
Example: DELHI. D (4) → 23 = W. E (5) → 22 = V. L (12) → 15 = O. H (8) → 19 = S. I (9) → 18 = R. Result WVOSR.
“Position from the right end” of the alphabet: the 7th letter from Z is at position 27 − 7 = 20, which is T.
Careful, two different “reverses”: reversing the ALPHABET swaps each letter with its partner (CAT → XZG). Reversing the WORD only changes the order of the same letters (CAT → TAC).
Word substitution: a made-up language gives each word a code word. Each word has exactly one code word and vice versa (one-to-one). Find a word that is common to two statements: its code is the code word that is common to both.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Opposite letter: position p ↔ 27 − p. A↔Z, B↔Y, ..., M↔N.
Opposite twice = original letter (self-inverse).
Reverse alphabet ≠ reverse word. Do exactly what the question says, in order.
Word substitution: common word → common code word (assuming one word, one code word).

Mirror-alphabet substitution and its self-inverse property: Wikipedia “Atbash”; letter-for-letter substitution and decoding by the inverse substitution: Wikipedia “Substitution cipher” (one-to-one requirement: derived, since an inverse must exist). Pair sum 27 and the shift/opposite link: derived, Python-checked on all 26 letters. The word-substitution puzzle was solved by checking all 120 ways of matching 5 words to 5 code words.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Write DELHI using the opposite letter for each letter.

Step 1. Positions: D 4, E 5, L 12, H 8, I 9.
Step 2. 27 minus each: 23, 22, 15, 19, 18.
Step 3. Letters: W, V, O, S, R. Answer: WVOSR. Check: opposite of WVOSR gives DELHI back.

In a language, “red hot tea” = “ku pa mo”, “hot green tea” = “mo ra pa”, “tea red cup” = “ku ni mo”. What is the code for “tea”?

Step 1. “tea” appears in all three statements. Which code word appears in all three? Statement 1 has ku, pa, mo. Statement 2 has mo, ra, pa. Statement 3 has ku, ni, mo. Only mo is in all three.
Step 2. So tea = mo. Then “hot” (in statements 1 and 2, besides tea) = pa, and “red” (in 1 and 3, besides tea) = ku.
Step 3. Answer: tea = mo. Checked by trying all 120 matchings: exactly one fits.

5The trap

Rule: “write the opposite letter of each letter”. Word: CAT.
Wrong move: write the letters backwards, TAC. That reverses the WORD, not the alphabet.
Another wrong move: use 26 − p instead of 27 − p. A (1) would give 25 = Y, but A↔Z.
Right: C (3) → 24 = X, A (1) → 26 = Z, T (20) → 7 = G. Answer: XZG. Test: A must go to Z.

Lesson 3 · Conditional and chained codes

In one line: a conditional or chained code is just several simple rules in a row. Write the word, do step 1 under it, step 2 under that, and never skip the order.

1Picture this

At a railway counter the clerk says: “If the ticket is for a sleeper coach, stamp it red. Otherwise, stamp it blue.” You first CHECK the ticket, then pick the stamp. A conditional code works the same way: check the word (or letter), then apply the matching rule. A chain is like a two-stage assembly line: parcel goes through stage 1, then stage 2.

WordStep 1Step 2

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A conditional rule has the form “if (test) then (rule A), otherwise (rule B)”. A vowel is one of A, E, I, O, U; every other letter is a consonant.
Tests can be about the whole word (starts with a vowel? even or odd number of letters?) or about each letter (vowel or consonant? even or odd position?).
Always test the ORIGINAL word, not the half-coded one, unless the question says otherwise.
Chain: apply step 1 to every letter, write the result, then apply step 2 to that result. This is like function composition: do one rule, then the next.
Order matters. Take A with “+2 then opposite”: A→C→X. Take “opposite then +2”: A→Z→B. Different answers (checked for all 26 letters).
Handy link: opposite after a +k shift equals the shift −k after the opposite. So the pair “+2 then opposite” is the same as “opposite then −2”.
Example: BAT with “+2 then opposite”: B→D→W, A→C→X, T→V→E. Result WXE.
Example with a test: rule “starts with a vowel: +1 each letter; otherwise reverse the word”. ORANGE starts with O (vowel) so +1: PSBOHF. MANGO starts with M (consonant) so reverse: OGNAM.
Write the working in rows under the word. Short, tidy rows beat doing it in your head.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Read the test first, decide the branch, then apply only that branch.
Chain: result of step 1 is the input of step 2. Do not swap the steps.
Test the original word unless told to test something else.
+k then opposite = opposite then −k (derived).

Doing one rule and then another (composition) and that the order matters: Wikipedia “Function composition”; vowels A E I O U: Wikipedia “English alphabet”; letter-by-letter substitution: Wikipedia “Substitution cipher”. The +k/opposite identity and order effect: derived, Python-checked (all 26 letters, k from −30 to 30).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Rule: “if the word starts with a vowel, move every letter +1; otherwise write the letters in reverse order.” Code ORANGE.

Step 1. Test: first letter O is a vowel. Branch: +1.
Step 2. O→P, R→S, A→B, N→O, G→H, E→F.
Step 3. Answer: PSBOHF.

Rule: “each letter moves +2 then is changed to its opposite letter.” Code BAT.

Step 1. After +2: B→D, A→C, T→V. Row: DCV.
Step 2. Opposite of each: D(4)→23 W, C(3)→24 X, V(22)→5 E. Row: WXE.
Step 3. Answer: WXE. If you swapped the steps you would get ABI, a wrong option.

5The trap

Rule: “+2, then opposite letter”. Word: BAT.
Wrong move: do the opposite first (B→Y, A→Z, T→G), then +2 (Y→A, Z→B, G→I). You write ABI.
Right: +2 first (DCV), then opposite: WXE.
Second trap: testing the coded word for the condition. Test the word you were GIVEN.

6Speed method: two rows and one-letter elimination

Routine. (1) Write the word. (2) Row 1 = after step 1. (3) Row 2 = after step 2. (4) Compute only the FIRST letter all the way through, then strike out options that do not start with it. (5) Check the last letter on the survivors.

Drill: do it in your head or on paper, then Show.

Blood Relations

In one line: name how two people in a family are related by walking a short path on a family tree. Three lessons: the words, the pointing puzzles, the coded versions.

Lesson 1 · The relation words

In one line: every family word is just a short walk from you using four moves: to a parent, to a child, to a sibling, to a spouse. Learn the walks and the names stop being a puzzle.

1Picture this

At a cousin’s wedding in Nagpur, an elderly relative points at a stranger and says, “He is my sister’s husband.” You instantly know what that makes him to your relative. That instant is the whole skill. Here we practise it until it is automatic.

Plain English first. Jargon is explained the first time it appears.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Four basic links. Parent (mother, father), child (son, daughter), sibling (brother, sister) and spouse (husband, wife). A sibling is someone who shares at least one parent with you; in this guide siblings share both parents.
Every other family word is a short chain of those four. “Mother’s brother” = go to your mother, then to her brother. Two moves.
Aunt / uncle = a sibling of one of your parents. Maternal means your mother’s side, paternal means your father’s side. So mother’s brother = maternal uncle, father’s sister = paternal aunt. (Everyday English also calls a parent’s sibling’s spouse aunt or uncle; this guide avoids that case.)
Nephew / niece = a child of your sibling (or of your sibling-in-law). Nephew is a boy, niece is a girl.
Cousin = a child of a parent’s sibling. The word does not tell you the gender.
Grandparent = the parent of a parent. Mother’s mother and mother’s father are the maternal grandparents; father’s mother and father’s father are the paternal grandparents.
In-laws are relatives by marriage (the formal word is affinity). Spouse’s parent = parent-in-law. Your child’s spouse = child-in-law (son-in-law, daughter-in-law). A spouse’s sibling, or a sibling’s spouse, is a sibling-in-law (brother-in-law or sister-in-law); so is the spouse of your spouse’s sibling.
Gender-free words to watch: cousin, sibling, spouse, parent, child. Examiners use them on purpose. If an answer needs a gender that was never given, “cannot be determined” may be correct.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Four moves: parent, child, sibling, spouse. Every relation name is a chain of them.
Uncle/aunt = parent’s sibling. Nephew/niece = sibling’s child. Cousin = child of a parent’s sibling. Grandparent = parent of a parent.
In-law = by marriage: spouse’s parent, child’s spouse, spouse’s sibling, sibling’s spouse, spouse’s sibling’s spouse.

Lesson 2 · Chains and pointing puzzles

In one line: a pointing puzzle is a walk. Start at the speaker, write one step per phrase, collapse the pairs that cancel, count generations, then name.

1Picture this

Someone posts an old photo in the family WhatsApp group: “She is the daughter of my father’s only son.” You trace it with a finger: up to the father, across to his only son, down to a daughter. Up, across, down. That finger-trace is the method.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Start from the speaker, not the person pointed at. The speaker is “me”.
One phrase = one step. “My mother’s brother’s son” becomes: mother, brother, son.
Each step moves you: up (to a parent), down (to a child) or sideways (sibling or spouse). Count the ups and downs.
The word only means “no new person”: “my father’s only son”, said by a man, is the man himself. Without “only”, a sibling or child step names a different person.
Pairs that cancel (collapse them): father’s wife = mother; mother’s husband = father; wife’s husband = the speaker (a man).
Some collapses depend on the speaker’s gender: son’s mother = the speaker’s wife if the speaker is a man, the speaker herself if a woman.
Gender clues live in the words son, daughter, brother, sister, father, mother, husband, wife. Cousin, sibling, spouse and child do not give a gender.
Name the person by net generation first, then side (father’s or mother’s), then gender. If the gender was never given, the right option may be “cannot be determined”.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Write the walk from the speaker. One phrase = one step.
Up = parent, down = child, sideways = sibling or spouse. Net generation = ups − downs.
“Only” re-uses a person already on the walk; no “only” means a new person.
Collapse: father’s wife = mother. Mother’s husband = father. Wife’s husband = the man himself.

The boy is therefore Dev’s own son. Test on a tree: father → child (only male) → that child is Dev.
Right: son. If the speaker were a woman, her father’s only son would be her brother and the boy would be her nephew. The speaker’s gender decides.

Lesson 3 · Coded relations

In one line: a coded relation swaps words for symbols. Write the English under each symbol, read it as a walk from the last person back to the first, and name.

1Picture this

A friend invents a secret code in the college canteen: “+ means mother of, − means father of.” Then writes A + B − C on a napkin and asks, “What is A to C?” You decode it into two plain sentences. Exam coded relations are the same game, and the key is always printed in the question.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

We use this made-up key (typical style, ours; in a real question the key is given): + mother, − father, × brother, ÷ sister.
“A + B” reads “A is the mother of B”. The symbol describes the person on its LEFT.
So each symbol also gives the left person’s gender: + female, − male, × male, ÷ female. The LAST person’s gender is never given.
A chain like A + B − C is read pair by pair: A is the mother of B; B is the father of C.
To find A’s relation to C, start at C and walk back: C’s father is B, B’s mother is A. Walk: father, then mother. That is the paternal grandmother.
Go right to left. Reading the symbols left to right as the walk is the classic mistake.
To find the relation of C to A (the other way) the gender of C is missing, so the answer may be “nephew or niece” or “grandson or granddaughter”. Then “cannot be determined” is the right option.
Siblings: in this guide a brother or sister shares both parents with you, so a brother’s father is your father.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Key used here: + mother, − father, × brother, ÷ sister.
A [symbol] B means: A is the [mother, father, brother or sister] of B.
First person’s relation to the LAST: start at the last person and apply the relations from the right-most pair back to the left.
Last person’s relation to the first: gender missing, so check whether the options force one.

Derived, Python-checked: every expression in this lesson was built as an explicit family tree twice (walking back from the last person, and growing the tree forward from the first person for both genders of the last person) and the answers agreed. The key is our own, not from any paper.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Key: + mother, − father, × brother, ÷ sister. In P × Q + R, how is P related to R?

Step 1. Decode pair by pair: P × Q = P is the brother of Q. Q + R = Q is the mother of R.
Step 2. Walk from R: R’s mother is Q (up one). Q’s brother is P (sideways).
Step 3. Mother’s brother = maternal uncle. P is male because × means brother.
Step 4. Check: net generation +1, mother’s side, male. Answer: maternal uncle. (R to P: nephew or niece, as R’s gender is not given.)

5The trap

Expression A + B − C, question: how is A related to C?
Wrong move: apply the symbols left to right from C: + mother, then − father. That gives mother’s father: maternal grandfather.
But the first symbol is between A and B, not between B and C. Walk back from C: the pair B − C comes first (father), then A + B (mother).
Right: father then mother = paternal grandmother. Always walk from the last person, right to left.

6Speed method: symbols to words, then walk back

Routine: (1) write the English word above every symbol, (2) circle the last person, (3) read the words right to left as a walk, (4) all steps are “up” or “sideways”, so the answer is a parent, grandparent, uncle, aunt or sibling (or a “great-” version in long chains), (5) check the side with the first mother or father on the walk.

Speed drill

Key: + mother, − father, × brother, ÷ sister. Say the answer, then tap Show.

Direction and Distance

In one line: draw the walk as (East, North) numbers, count turns clockwise and anticlockwise, and use Pythagoras for any straight-line distance.

Lesson 1 · Compass and turns

In one line: a right turn walks you one step clockwise round the compass (N → E → S → W), a left turn one step back, so count net turns and you know where you face.

1Picture this

You walk out of your school gate and the gate faces East. The main road is to your right, so you turn right and walk down it. Which way are you heading now? South. You just did a compass turn without thinking.

Facing EastRight → South

Left and right belong to the person, not to the map.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A compass has four main directions, called cardinal directions: North, East, South, West. They are 90° apart and go clockwise in that order (N → E → S → W).
Between them sit four more, the intercardinal directions: North-East, South-East, South-West, North-West. Each is 45° from its neighbours.
Clockwise means the way a clock’s hands go. A right turn is clockwise. A left turn is anticlockwise (the opposite way).
Stand facing North. Your right hand points East, your left hand points West, and behind you is South.
Each 90° right turn moves your heading one step along N → E → S → W → N. Each left turn moves one step back.
Two right turns (or two left turns) make an about-turn: you face the opposite way. A right turn then a left turn cancel out.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Order clockwise: N → E → S → W → N (90° each). Eight-point compass: add NE, SE, SW, NW (45° between).
Right turn = clockwise = +1 step of 90°. Left turn = anticlockwise = −1 step.
Net turns = (rights) − (lefts). If the net is negative, add 4 until it is 0 to 4 (so −1 counts as 3, −2 as 2). Net 1 = right, net 2 = about-turn, net 3 = left, net 0 or 4 = same heading.
Angles: heading in degrees clockwise from North (N 0°, E 90°, S 180°, W 270°). Add for a right turn, subtract for left, then wrap around 360°.

Compass points and angles, clockwise order: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Net-turn rule: derived, Python-checked (all sequences of up to 6 turns, from every start heading, compared with step-by-step turning and with a rotation-matrix check).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: Facing South, you turn left, left, right, left. Where do you face?
Step 1. Score each turn: right = +1, left = −1. Turns: −1, −1, +1, −1.
Step 2. Add: −1 −1 +1 −1 = −2. Two net left turns = an about-turn.
Step 3. South reversed is North. Check one step at a time: S → left → E → left → N → right → E → left → N. Same answer.

5The trap

Facing South. You turn right. Which way are you facing?
Wrong thought: “right means East, always”. That gives East.
Test with your own body: stand facing South. Your right shoulder points West (the side where the sun sets).
Right: a right turn moves one step clockwise from your current heading: S → W. Always start from where you face, never from the map.

6Speed method: net-turn count

Do not redraw the compass for every turn. Score right +1, left −1, add them, take the remainder after dividing by 4, then step that many places clockwise from the start.

Elimination. If the net is 0 or 4 you face the start direction. If the net is 2 the answer is the opposite direction. That alone removes two options before you do anything else.

Lesson 2 · Walking, coordinates and shortest distance

In one line: keep a running (East, North) score for every leg, then Pythagoras on the final pair gives the straight-line distance.

1Picture this

An auto driver in a grid-planned city goes 3 blocks North and then 4 blocks East. He paid for 7 blocks of road. But a crow flying straight from the start to the end covers only 5 blocks. Road distance and straight-line distance are two different numbers.

Walked: 3 + 4 = 7
Straight: 5

Exams usually ask for the straight-line one (“how far from the starting point?”).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw a plus sign and put the start in the middle: that is the point (0, 0). Call it (East, North).
Moving East adds to the first number, West takes away. Moving North adds to the second number, South takes away.
So every leg changes just ONE of the two numbers. Keep a running pair after each leg.
At the end, the pair (x, y) says where you are. x positive = East of start, y positive = North of start. Both non-zero? Name it by both, for example North-East.
Distance from the start in a straight line: make a right-angled triangle with legs |x| and |y|. Pythagoras: distance = √(x² + y²). (|x| means x with its sign ignored.)
Distance WALKED is different: just add every leg. It is never smaller than the straight-line distance.
To say where the START is from the END, flip both signs: North-East becomes South-West.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Position = (East, North). E: x + · W: x − · N: y + · S: y −.
Straight-line distance = √(x² + y²) (Pythagoras: a² + b² = c², c = hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle).
Distance walked = sum of all legs. Straight-line ≤ walked, equal only if the whole path is a straight line.
Direction of end from start: by signs of x and y (both non-zero = a quadrant name such as North-East). Start from end = flip both signs.
Convention in this book: when both x and y are non-zero we name the quadrant (North-East) even if the angle is not exactly 45°.

Pythagoras theorem, hypotenuse, 3-4-5 / 5-12-13 / 8-15-17, and distance formula: pages in Sources. Triples 6-8-10, 9-12-15, 7-24-25, 20-21-29, 12-16-20, 15-20-25, 10-24-26: derived, Python-checked. Coordinate method: derived, Python-checked (turn and walk simulator).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: Facing North, you walk 15 km, turn right and walk 24 km, turn right and walk 8 km. Distance and direction from the start?
Step 1. Leg 1: facing North, 15 km. Position (East, North) = (0, 15).
Step 2. Right turn from North = East. Leg 2: 24 km East. Position (24, 15).
Step 3. Right turn from East = South. Leg 3: 8 km South. Position (24, 7).
Step 4. East 24 and North 7: you are North-East of the start. Distance √(24² + 7²) = √(576 + 49) = √625 = 25 km. Total walked: 47 km (not the answer).

5The trap

Question: 5 km North then 12 km East. “How far from the start?”
Wrong move: add the legs. 5 + 12 = 17. This is a tempting option in the list.
Why it fails: 17 km is the road you walked, but the start and end are joined by the diagonal of a right-angled triangle.
Right: 5² + 12² = 169, √169 = 13 km. Always ask: walked, or straight-line? Second trap: a “right turn” means right of the way you FACE now, not East.

Lesson 3 · Shadows and two travellers

In one line: a shadow points away from the Sun (morning West, evening East), and two people leaving one point either add, subtract or use Pythagoras for their gap.

1Picture this

It is early morning on the school ground and the Sun is just coming up over the buildings in the East. Look at the flagpole’s shadow: it stretches away from the Sun, towards the West, across the ground.

Sun EastShadow → West

Light on one side, shadow on the other side.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A shadow always falls on the side AWAY from the light. Light from the East gives a shadow pointing West.
Exam convention: at sunrise the Sun is in the East, at sunset in the West. (In real life this is exact only at the equinoxes; questions ignore that.)
So morning shadows point West, evening shadows point East.
Now place the shadow on the person: facing North, right = East and left = West. Facing South, right = West and left = East. Facing East, right = South and left = North. Facing West, right = North and left = South.
Work backwards: shadow on your right in the evening means East is on your right, and East is on the right only when you face North.
Two travellers from one point: opposite directions add the distances, same direction subtract, right angles use Pythagoras.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Shadow points directly away from the Sun.
Convention: sunrise Sun = East, so shadow = West. Sunset Sun = West, so shadow = East.
Facing N: R = E, L = W · Facing S: R = W, L = E · Facing E: R = S, L = N · Facing W: R = N, L = S.
Two travellers from one point: opposite directions → add · same direction → subtract · at right angles → √(a² + b²).

Shadow definition (blocked light) and sunrise near due East: Wikipedia pages in Sources; “shadow points away from the Sun” is a geometric consequence, derived. Facing table and traveller rules: derived, Python-checked (facing table for all headings and sides; traveller rules on 2000 random cases). Morning/evening East/West is an exam convention, not exact for most dates.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Question: One morning Sita’s shadow falls on her left. Which way is she facing?
Step 1. Morning: Sun East, so shadow points West.
Step 2. The shadow is on her left, so West is on her left.
Step 3. Left = West when you face North (check: facing North, left hand points West). Answer: North.

5The trap

Question: in the morning a pole’s shadow falls towards the East. Which side is the Sun?
Wrong thought: “Sun in the East, so shadow in the East”. That puts the shadow on the Sun’s side.
Shadows point AWAY from the light. The picture only works if the shadow and the Sun are on opposite sides.
Right: a shadow pointing East means the Sun is West, so this is the evening (sunset). Always flip the Sun to get the shadow.

6Speed method: shadow-to-facing in one hop

Shortcut: shadow in front of you means you face the shadow’s direction; shadow behind means you face the Sun’s direction; shadow on your right means you face the direction one step ANTICLOCKWISE from the shadow; on your left means one step CLOCKWISE from the shadow.

Check: morning, shadow West, on your left: one step clockwise from West is North. That matches the worked example.

Order, Ranking, Alphanumeric series

In one line: these questions are all about "who or what stands where", and one tiny habit (count the person once, not twice) wins most of them.

Lesson 1 · Rank in a row

In one line: a person's rank from the left plus their rank from the right counts them twice, so total = left + right − 1.

1Picture this

You queue at a railway ticket window in Lucknow. Someone says "you are 9th from the front". You turn around and count 16 people from the back, counting yourself. How many people in the queue? You are in both counts, so you were counted twice.

8 in frontYou15 behind

8 + 1 + 15 = 24. Left rank 9 and right rank 16 add to 25, one too many.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A rank (or position) says "which number in the line am I", and you count yourself. First in line is rank 1.
You can count from either end. "9th from the left" and "16th from the right" can describe the same person.
Picture the row with the person in the middle. People in front of them: rank − 1. People behind: the other rank − 1. Add the person: that is the whole row.
So total = left rank + right rank − 1. The −1 removes the double-counted person.
To switch ends for one person: other-end rank = total − rank + 1.
"Swap" means two people exchange seats: each now stands in the other's old place. So "after the swap A is 15th from the left" tells you B's OLD place was 15th from the left.
"How many between A and B" means the people strictly in the gap, not A and not B. Put both on the same end, subtract the ranks, then subtract 1.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Total = rank from left + rank from right − 1
Rank from the other end = Total − rank + 1
Between two people = |rank of one − rank of the other| − 1, with both ranks counted from the SAME end. (The bars | | mean "drop any minus sign": the gap between 8 and 19 is 11 either way round.)
Different people, one rank from the left (a) and one from the right (b). "Overlap" means the two counts run past each other, so the person counted from the left stands further right than the one counted from the right. If a + b ≤ Total then between = Total − a − b; if a + b > Total + 1 (the ranks overlap) then between = a + b − Total − 2. If a + b = Total + 1 they are the same person.
Swap: after two people swap, each sits in the other's old place. Translate the new rank into the old place of the other person.

Total, other-end and between formulas: Oliveboard, Testbook, BYJU'S (see Sources). Overlap and non-overlap between rules, same-person case: Testbook, BYJU'S; also derived, Python-checked over every total up to 39 and every pair of ranks.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Ravi is 7th from the left and 12th from the right. How many people?

Step 1. Left rank 7, right rank 12.
Step 2. Add: 7 + 12 = 19. Ravi is counted twice.
Step 3. Subtract 1: 18 people.
Check: 6 in front + Ravi + 11 behind = 18.

Row of 30. A is 8th from the left, B is 12th from the right. How many between?

Step 1. B from the left: 30 − 12 + 1 = 19.
Step 2. Both ranks from the left: 8 and 19.
Step 3. 19 − 8 = 11, then − 1 for B himself: 10 between.
Check with the shortcut: 8 + 12 = 20 is less than 30, so between = 30 − 20 = 10.

5The trap

"9th from the left and 16th from the right. Total?" The tempting answer is 9 + 16 = 25.
That counts the person twice. The right total is 24.
Same trap for "between": 19 − 8 = 11 looks right, but it counts B. Always take 1 more away.
Overlap trap: if the two ranks add to MORE than the total, the people are on the "wrong" sides. Do not use total − a − b (it goes negative). Use a + b − total − 2.

6Speed method and drill

Elimination means crossing out options that cannot be right. Here: the total must be at least as big as each rank. If your answer is smaller than any given rank, it is wrong. Then use total = left + right − 1, which takes about 5 seconds.

Fast order: 1) write the total or the missing rank, 2) put both people on the same end, 3) subtract, 4) take 1 away for "between".

Lesson 2 · Alphanumeric series

In one line: a mixed row of digits, letters and symbols is just a numbered row, so number every element and count.

1Picture this

A train coach has seats marked 7, R, #, 3, K and so on. A friend says "go 4 seats to the right of seat number 9". You put a finger on seat 9, then hop 4 times. That is a whole alphanumeric series question.

Start at 9th4 hops → 13th

9 + 4 = 13. The finger spot itself is NOT a hop.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

An alphanumeric series is a row that mixes letters, numbers (digits) and symbols such as # @ $ % & *.
Number every element from the left: 1, 2, 3 ... Write the numbers under the series. This is the single most useful move.
Series of 20? Then the 1st from the right is the 20th from the left. In general, rank from right = total − rank from left + 1, just like Lesson 1.
"4th to the right of the 9th from the left": find the 9th, then go 4 steps right, so you land on 9 + 4 = 13th. "To the left" subtracts instead.
"Immediately followed by" means the very next element. "Between" means strictly inside, not counting the two ends.
Vowels are A, E, I, O, U. Every other letter is a consonant. Digits are 0 to 9. Symbols are everything else.

3The rules (now that you get it)

m-th to the right of the n-th from the left = position n + m from the left
m-th to the left of the n-th from the left = position n − m from the left
n-th from the right = position (total − n + 1) from the left
Elements strictly between positions i and j = j − i − 1

Question types ("n-th from left/right", "m-th to the right of n-th", "immediately preceded/followed", "between"): TutorialsPoint and Unacademy (see Sources). Position arithmetic: derived, Python-checked on the series below.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Series: 7 R # 3 K @ 9 M $ 2 T 5 & E 8 % L 4 * U (20 elements). Which is the 3rd to the right of the 5th from the left?

Step 1. Number them: 1:7 2:R 3:# 4:3 5:K 6:@ 7:9 8:M 9:$ 10:2 11:T 12:5 13:& 14:E 15:8 16:% 17:L 18:4 19:* 20:U.
Step 2. 5th from the left is K.
Step 3. 3 steps right: @, 9, M. Land on 5 + 3 = 8th: M.

Same series. How many vowels are immediately preceded by a symbol and immediately followed by a digit?

Step 1. List the vowels: E (14th) and U (20th).
Step 2. E: before it is & (symbol), after it is 8 (digit). Both tests pass.
Step 3. U: before it is * (symbol), but U is last so nothing follows. It fails.
Step 4. Answer: 1.

5The trap

"4th to the right of the 9th": the 9th is hop zero. Start your count on the NEXT element.
So hops land on 10, 11, 12, 13. Answer is the 13th, not the 12th.
"To the right" always means towards the end of the series, even if the reference was counted from the right. Convert the reference to a left position first, then add for right and subtract for left.
Also check the edge: "followed by" for the last element has no answer, so that element does not count.

6Speed method

1) Write position numbers under the series once. 2) For "how many" questions, circle the target type (for example every letter) first, then check the neighbour rule on each circle. 3) For removed or reversed series, rewrite the series once, then renumber.

Elimination: if the answer must be a symbol, cross out digits and letters in the options. Two options left means a 50% guess, well above the 23% line.

Lesson 3 · Rank by marks, and the alphabet as a row

In one line: class ranks work like a row from the top, and the English alphabet is simply a row of 26 letters.

1Picture this

A school notice board lists class ranks. Anil is 12th from the top in a class of 40. Bhavna is 9 places below him. Top of the list is rank 1, bottom is rank 40. It is the same row as Lesson 1, just standing upright.

Top 11A8B19 below

Anil 12th, Bhavna 12 + 9 = 21st from the top, so 40 − 21 + 1 = 20th from the bottom.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

"Top" is the left end and "bottom" is the right end. Every rule from Lesson 1 works. Total = rank from top + rank from bottom − 1.
"9 places below" means 9 ranks further down: rank + 9. "9 places above" means rank − 9.
The English alphabet has 26 letters. Treat it as a row: A is 1st from the left, Z is 1st from the right.
Because 26 + 1 = 27, a letter's place from the left plus its place from the right is 27. So the 7th from the right is the 27 − 7 = 20th letter, T.
Letter at the same place from the other end is the "opposite letter": A and Z, B and Y, F and U, K and P.
Handy anchors: E, J, O, T, Y are the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, 25th letters. Vowels A E I O U are 1, 5, 9, 15, 21.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Rank from bottom = Total − rank from top + 1
Alphabet: place from left + place from right = 27
Letters between two letters = (bigger place − smaller place) − 1
Two clues, "exactly k between": try both orders (B right of A and B left of A). Each can give a different total.

Top/bottom ranking and minimum-persons idea: Oliveboard, BYJU'S (see Sources). 26 letters and vowels A E I O U: Wikipedia "English alphabet". Sum-27 rule, opposite letters, E-J-O-T-Y and two-case totals: derived, Python-checked. Minimum total in the two-case example also matches BYJU'S "sum of positions − persons between − 2" (8 + 12 − 5 − 2 = 13).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

A is 8th from the left, B is 12th from the right, exactly 5 between. Possible totals?

Step 1. Case 1: B is right of A. B is 8 + 5 + 1 = 14th from the left. Total = 14 + 12 − 1 = 25.
Step 2. Case 2: B is left of A. B is 8 − 5 − 1 = 2nd from the left. Total = 2 + 12 − 1 = 13.
Step 3. Both are valid rows. If the question says "minimum", answer 13. If "maximum", 25. If it asks "the total" with no more clues, it cannot be fixed.

In the word CHAMPION, how many letter pairs have as many letters between them in the word as in the alphabet? (Convention: a pair counts whether its letters appear in the word in alphabet order or in reverse order.)

Step 1. Number the word: C1 H2 A3 M4 P5 I6 O7 N8. Alphabet places: C3 H8 A1 M13 P16 I9 O15 N14.
Step 2. For each pair, count letters between them in the word (place gap − 1) and in the alphabet. C (word 1, alphabet 3) and A (word 3, alphabet 1): 3 − 1 − 1 = 1 in the word, 3 − 1 − 1 = 1 in the alphabet. H (2, 8) and N (8, 14): 8 − 2 − 1 = 5 and 14 − 8 − 1 = 5. O (7, 15) and N (8, 14): 8 − 7 − 1 = 0 and 15 − 14 − 1 = 0.
Step 2b. Those three pairs match: CA and ON (reverse order), HN (alphabet order). Every other pair differs.
Step 3. Answer: 3 pairs. We tested all 28 pairs by code. Careful: if a question counts only pairs in alphabet order (forward), the answer would be 1 (HN). Read the convention.

5The trap

"7th from the right end of the alphabet": many people compute 26 − 7 = 19 and land on S.
That gives the number of letters in front of the target, not its place. The place is 27 − 7 = 20, which is T.
The same off-by-one hides in "between": letters between E (5) and R (18) is 18 − 5 − 1 = 12, not 13.
And with two clues, one order is rarely the only order. Check both.

Elimination: an answer outside A to Z, or a place number above 26, is wrong immediately. For ranks, an answer larger than the total is wrong.

Input-Output (machine)

In one line: a machine rearranges a line of words and numbers step by step. Learn the move once, simulate it on paper, and every question becomes “write the next line”. Important: every step rule in this chapter is our own typical-style machine, written by us. These are NOT confirmed PFRDA rules; no official PFRDA page gives Input-Output topics or step rules. In the exam, always read the rule off the given steps.

Lesson 1 · One item at a time: sort the line step by step

In one line: a machine fixes ONE item per step. Sort the target order once on paper, and every step is just “bring the next item to its slot”.

1Picture this

A teacher lines up five children by height, one move at a time. First she finds the shortest and pulls that child to the front. The others just shuffle back and keep their order. Next she finds the second shortest and puts that child in place two. That is the whole machine.
Placed (fixed)
Not yet touched

The fixed part grows by one each step.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

An Input-Output question gives an Input line and a rule. The machine changes the line step by step: Step I, Step II, and so on.
A slot means a position in the line: slot 1 is the first place from the left, slot 2 the second, and so on.
Words are put in dictionary order (alphabetical order). Compare first letters; if they match, compare the next letters. A shorter word that is a prefix comes first, so “bat” comes before “bath”.
Numbers are put in size order: ascending means smallest first, descending means largest first.
In our machines, step k takes the k-th item of the sorted target list and PULLS it out of the line. It is put in slot k. The items it jumps over move one place along; all other items keep their order.
Because of that, after step k the line is: the first k sorted items, then everything else in its ORIGINAL order. This is the shortcut that makes options easy to eliminate.
Right-end version: slots are counted from the right. Step 1 puts the item in the far-right slot, step 2 in the slot beside it.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Sort the target list ONCE on rough paper. Step k then only touches slot k.
Shift rule: pull the item out, insert it in slot k, keep the other items in their old order.
Stop count: n items need at most n − 1 steps (the last item settles itself). The machine stops after the last step that changes the line.

Dictionary and shift-versus-swap ideas: selection sort (Wikipedia) describes the same “place the next item in the next slot” loop; its shifting version keeps the other items in order. Dictionary order: Lexicographic order (Wikipedia). The “at most n − 1 steps” and “first k sorted + the rest in original order” facts: derived, Python-checked (all orders of up to 7 items).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Words: pen map zoo cup bat

Dictionary order: bat cup map pen zoo.
Step I: bat pen map zoo cup (bat goes to slot 1, the rest keep their order).
Step II: bat cup pen map zoo (cup goes to slot 2, the rest keep their order).
Step III: bat cup map pen zoo (map goes to slot 3, the rest keep their order).
No more changes after Step III, so the machine stops. Three steps for five items.

Numbers, smallest first to the left: 42 17 63 8 29

Ascending: 8 17 29 42 63.
Step I: 8 42 17 63 29
Step II: 8 17 42 63 29
Step III: 8 17 29 42 63
Check with the shortcut: after Step II the line is the 2 smallest (8 17) then the rest in original order (42 63 29). It matches.

5The trap

Input: 42 17 63 8 29. Rule: smallest number to slot 1, others keep their order.
Wrong move: SWAP the smallest number (8) with the number in slot 1 (42). That gives 8 17 63 42 29.
Right move: pull 8 out and put it in slot 1; 42, 17, 63 each shift one place right. That gives 8 42 17 63 29.
Test: the rule says the others keep their order. In the swap result, 42 and 63 and 17 are in a different order (17 now before 63 before 42). So swap breaks the rule. Always check Step I against the given line to see which move the machine uses.

6Speed method and elimination

Elimination: for Step k, look only at slots 1 to k. They must equal the first k items of the sorted list. Cross out any option that fails. Then check that the remaining items are in their ORIGINAL relative order.

Routine. (1) Write the sorted list. (2) Cross out options whose first k slots are wrong. (3) Among the rest, find the one whose other items keep input order.

Lesson 2 · Words and numbers together

In one line: two sorts run side by side. Words fill the left end, numbers fill the right end, and the machine alternates between them.

1Picture this

At a railway counter, a clerk keeps tickets by name on the left shelf and cash notes by size on the right shelf. One turn he files the next name on the left; next turn he files the next biggest note on the right. The loose stuff in the middle stays in its old order.
Words
Untouched middle
Numbers

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A mixed Input has words and numbers jumbled together, for example tea 89 hen 58 elk 55 owl 40.
The rule has two halves: what happens to the words, and what happens to the numbers. It also tells you which goes first and that the two alternate.
Our standard machine: odd steps (I, III, V) bring the next word, in dictionary order, to the next free slot from the LEFT. Even steps (II, IV, VI) bring the next largest number to the next free slot from the RIGHT.
“Next free slot from the right” means: the first number goes to the far right, the second goes BESIDE it, the third beside that.
Everything not yet placed keeps its original order in the middle.
If one kind runs out (say all words are placed), the other kind simply continues on every step.
Shortcut (derived, Python-checked): after each step the line = placed words, then the untouched items in input order, then placed numbers.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Odd step = word to the left. Even step = number to the right (when words go first).
Words: dictionary order. Numbers: largest first, each new one beside the last.
Middle items keep their input order.

Dictionary order: Lexicographic order (Wikipedia). Placing an item and shifting the others while the rest keep order: Insertion sort (Wikipedia). The alternation rule and the prefix + middle + suffix shortcut are our machine, derived, Python-checked (3000 random inputs).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Input tea 89 hen 58 elk 55 owl 40. Words first.

Words in order: elk hen owl tea. Numbers largest first: 89 58 55 40.
Step I: elk tea 89 hen 58 55 owl 40 (word elk to slot 1).
Step II: elk tea hen 58 55 owl 40 89 (number 89 to the far right).
Step III: elk hen tea 58 55 owl 40 89 (word hen to slot 2).
Step IV: elk hen tea 55 owl 40 58 89 (number 58 beside 89).
Step V: elk hen owl tea 55 40 58 89 (word owl to slot 3).
Step VI: elk hen owl tea 40 55 58 89 (number 55 beside 58).
Step VII would not change anything, so the machine stops after six steps.

5The trap

Input tea 89 hen 58 elk 55 owl 40. Words first. After Step IV the correct line is elk hen tea 55 owl 40 58 89.
Wrong move: send EVERY number to the far-right end, one after another. After Step IV that gives elk hen tea 55 owl 40 89 58.
Spot it: 58 should sit BESIDE 89 (next free slot), not at the very end. 89 is the largest, so it must stay at the far right.
Rule: the first number takes the far right; each later number takes the next free slot to its left.

6Speed method and elimination

Split the Input into two lists first: words and numbers. Sort each. Then write one line per step by only adding one item to the left block (words) or the right block (numbers).

Elimination. After step k, the left block has the first a sorted words and the right block the largest b numbers (a + b = k). Cross out any option where the ends do not match.

Lesson 3 · Find the rule, then predict

In one line: when the rule is hidden, compare Input with Step I and Step II, test each guess by simulating it, and only then predict.

1Picture this

A friend shuffles cards in a way you cannot see. After one shuffle you guess a trick. After two you know. One look can fit two tricks; two looks usually fit one. Input-Output rule-finding works the same way.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Read Input and Step I. Which item moved? Where did it go: left end, right end, or a slot?
Look at the order of the moved items: ascending or descending, dictionary or length, by size?
Read Step II. Does it repeat the pattern, or switch end, or switch kind (word, then number)?
Guess a rule and SIMULATE it on the Input. If any given line differs, the guess is wrong.

Only after the rule fits every given line, predict the asked step.
Two-ended machine (numbers): odd steps bring the next smallest to the left, even steps bring the next largest to the right.

3The rule (now that you get it)

A rule is right only if it reproduces EVERY given line.
Step I alone can fit shift AND swap. Use Step II.
Word-length order: shortest word first; all lengths must differ for a clear answer.

Seating Arrangement (linear, circular, two rows)

Lesson 1 · One row (linear)

In one line: a seating puzzle is a row of empty slots; you fill them one clue at a time, and the only tricky word is "left" (it depends on which way the person faces).

1Picture this

Five friends sit in a row on a bench at Lucknow station, all looking at the platform ahead of them. Imagine you stand behind them and look at the backs of their heads. The friend on the far left of your view is also on the far left of the bench from their own point of view. Everyone faces the same way you do, so left matches left.

Now picture the same bench with everyone turned to face you. Each person's left is now YOUR right. That one flip causes most seating mistakes.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw one box per person, in a line: [1][2][3][4][5]. Number them from the left end of YOUR drawing.
Decide the facing first. In a row, "facing north" means looking up the page. Then their left = the left of your drawing (west) and their right = the right of your drawing (east).
"Immediate left of X" means the box right next to X on X's left. "Between" means strictly inside: the two ends do not count.
"Extreme left" or "extreme end" means the very first (or last) box. "Somewhere to the left of X" means any box further left, not necessarily next to X. "Second to the right of X" means two boxes along: X's box + 2.
"Second from the left end" is box 2. "Second from the right end" in a row of 5 is box 4.
Write the sure clues first (anyone at an end or given a fixed box). Then use "not at" clues to cross out boxes. When one name fits only one box, lock it in.

3The rule (only after the picture)

All face North: their left = West = left of your drawing. All face South: their left = East = RIGHT of your drawing.
Row of n people: k-th from the left = (n + 1 − k)-th from the right.
Strictly between box a and box b (a < b): b − a − 1 people.

Compass bearings N 0°, E 90°, S 180°, W 270° measured clockwise from north: Wikipedia "Cardinal direction". The left/right rule for each heading and the place-from-right formula: derived, Python-checked (coordinates for every heading; all slot counts by enumeration).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Five people A, B, C, D, E sit in a row facing north. C is at the extreme left. E is second from the right end. D is not at the extreme right. A is not at either end. D sits somewhere to the left of A. Find the order, left to right.

Step 1. Draw five boxes: [1][2][3][4][5].
Step 2. C is at the extreme left: C in box 1.
Step 3. E is second from the right end: box 4. E in box 4.
Step 4. Boxes left: 2, 3, 5 for A, B, D. D is not in 5, A is not in 5 (not at either end). So B takes box 5.
Step 5. A and D share boxes 2 and 3. D is to the left of A, so D in 2, A in 3.
Step 6. Answer: C D A E B. Check each clue once. Python brute force (trying every one of the 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120 orders) found exactly 1 order that fits.

5The trap

A row faces SOUTH. A person at the extreme east end says "I have a neighbour on my left". Is that possible?
No. Facing south, left = east. At the east end there is nobody further east, so nobody on the left. The neighbour is on his right (west).
Rule: before using "left" or "right", write the compass facing above the row. If the row faces south, flip your drawing: left of the person = right of the drawing.
Python check: with a south-facing row of 4, the east-end seat has left neighbour: none, right neighbour: the next seat to the west.

Lesson 2 · Round table (circular)

In one line: a round table has no start and no end, so fix one person, number the seats clockwise, and remember that facing the centre makes "left" mean clockwise.

1Picture this

Six cousins sit on cushions around a plate of laddoos at a Diwali dinner, everyone facing the plates in the middle. If you walk round the ring the way a clock hand moves, you pass each cousin in turn.

Each cousin has one neighbour on each side and exactly one person straight across the plates (in a ring with an even number of seats).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw a circle with n dots. Number them 1, 2, 3 ... clockwise (the way clock hands move). Anticlockwise is the other way round.
Rotating the whole table gives the same seating. So put the first named person in seat 1. This is why a ring has (n − 1)! patterns, not n!. (The "!" is a factorial: multiply the number by every smaller whole number down to 1. For example 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24, so a ring of 5 has 24 patterns.)
Facing the centre: stand at the bottom of the circle looking up. Your left hand points west, and walking west from the bottom goes clockwise. So left neighbour = next seat clockwise, right neighbour = next seat anticlockwise.
Facing outside (back to the centre): everything flips. Left neighbour = anticlockwise, right neighbour = clockwise.
Opposite = n ÷ 2 seats away. Only rings with an even number of people have an exact opposite (our stated convention).
"Second to the left of X" means two seats on from X in the left direction (clockwise when facing the centre). Between X and the person opposite, one side holds n ÷ 2 − 1 people (ring of 10: 5 − 1 = 4).
Fill sure clues (opposite pairs, fixed gaps), then use "immediate left/right" to add neighbours.

3The rule (only after the picture)

Facing centre: left = clockwise, right = anticlockwise. Facing outside: left = anticlockwise, right = clockwise.
Opposite of seat k in a ring of n (n even) = seat k + n/2 (wrap around).
Rotations count as the same seating, so a ring of n has (n − 1)! patterns.

Clockwise/anticlockwise definition and compass order: Wikipedia "Clockwise", "Cardinal direction". (n − 1)! circular arrangements: Wikipedia "Permutation". Left = clockwise when facing the centre: derived, Python-checked (coordinates, rings of 3 to 12 seats).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Six people A to F sit round a table, all facing the centre. A sits opposite D. B sits immediately to the left of A. F sits immediately to the right of D. C sits immediately to the right of A. Give the order clockwise from A.

Step 1. Draw 6 dots numbered 1 to 6 clockwise. Put A in seat 1 (rotations are equal).
Step 2. Opposite is 3 seats away. D in seat 4.
Step 3. Facing the centre, left = clockwise. B is immediately left of A, so B in seat 2.
Step 4. Right = anticlockwise. F is immediately right of D (seat 4), so go anticlockwise one seat: F in seat 3.
Step 5. C is immediately right of A (seat 1): anticlockwise one seat wraps to seat 6. E takes seat 5.
Step 6. Clockwise from A: A, B, F, D, E, C. Python tried all 720 seatings: 6 fit (the same ring turned 6 ways), so the ring is unique.

5The trap

Six people sit facing the centre. B is immediately left of A. Now everyone turns round to face outside.
Wrong move: "B is still on A's left". The seats did not move, but each person's left and right swapped.
Facing outside, A's left is the anticlockwise neighbour. That is the person who was on A's RIGHT before.
Rule: re-read "facing in" or "facing out" for every clue. Mixed facing (some in, some out) is read person by person: use that person's own facing.

Lesson 3 · Two rows facing each other

In one line: in two parallel rows facing each other, people straight across are "opposite", and the two rows face opposite ways, so "left" points to opposite sides in each row.

1Picture this

Two rows of chairs face each other in a Mumbai office meeting room: the top row looks toward the bottom of your page, the bottom row looks toward the top. A person in the top row and the person straight across from them look into each other's eyes.

When the top-row person lifts her left hand, it points to the same side of the room as the bottom-row person's RIGHT hand.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw two lines of boxes, one above the other, lined up. Add compass letters: west on the left of the page, east on the right.
Standard set-up used here: Row 1 (top) faces South. Row 2 (bottom) faces North.
Row 2 faces north: left = west (left of the page). Row 1 faces south: left = east (right of the page).
"Opposite" means the box straight across: same compass position in the other row.
To be safe, give each clue the compass word first, then place the person.

3The rule (only after the picture)

Row facing South: left = East, right = West. Row facing North: left = West, right = East.
Opposite of seat i from the west in one row = seat i from the west in the other row.
Two rows of m people give m opposite pairs.

Compass bearings (E is 90° clockwise from N): Wikipedia "Cardinal direction". Row rules and pair count: derived, Python-checked (coordinates; brute force over all seatings).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Rows of three. Row 1 (faces south) holds A, B, C. Row 2 (faces north) holds D, E, F. C sits at the east end of row 1. A sits immediately to the right of C. D sits opposite C. E sits immediately to the left of D. Who sits immediately to the left of A?

Step 1. Draw two rows of three boxes; label west and east.
Step 2. C in the east box of row 1.
Step 3. Row 1 faces south, so right = west. A is immediately right of C: A in the middle, B in the west box.
Step 4. D sits opposite C: D in the east box of row 2.
Step 5. Row 2 faces north, so left = west. E is immediately left of D: E in the middle, F in the west box.
Step 6. Question: left of A. A faces south, so left = east = C. Python brute force over all 720 seatings (names kept in their own rows) found exactly one fit: row 1 B A C, row 2 F E D, west to east.

5The trap

Four people sit in a row facing SOUTH. X sits at the extreme east end. Does X have a neighbour on his left?
Wrong move: "East end is on the right of my drawing, so his neighbour is on his left". The drawing is not his view.
Facing south, his left = east. Nobody is further east. His neighbour is on his right (west).
Same trap across rows: the top row's "immediate left" goes east, the bottom row's goes west. Always translate with compass words.

Method. (1) Two rows of boxes with W and E. (2) Write each row's facing. (3) Place fixed people and opposite pairs first (they fill both rows at once). (4) Translate "left/right" clues with the row's own facing. (5) Count the leftover names.

Lesson 1 · Floor and box puzzles (slot order)

In one line: a floor or box puzzle is five names to be placed into five numbered slots, and every clue crosses out some slots.

1Picture this

Your cousin's building in Pune has 5 floors and one family per floor. Someone says: "The Sharmas live above the Iyers" and "the Khans are on the top floor". You do not need to see the building. You only need five empty slots and a pencil.

5 top4321 bottom

Floors are counted from the bottom, so floor 1 is the lowest. A stack of boxes works the same way: position 1 is the bottom box.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A puzzle gives a few names and the same number of slots (floors, or places in a stack). Convention for this chapter: every name gets exactly one slot, and every slot gets exactly one name.
Number the slots from the bottom. Draw them as a column from top to bottom: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1.
Clue words are just slot arithmetic. "On the top floor" means slot 5. "Immediately above B" means B's number plus 1. "Above B" means any higher number.
"Between B and C" means strictly in the middle: a number between theirs, and B and C may come in either order. "Neighbouring" (or "next to") means the numbers differ by 1, on either side.
Step A: write in the fixed clues first ("on the top floor", "on floor 2"). They are your anchors.
Step B: treat "X immediately above Y" as one glued two-block. Find where a two-block still fits in the empty slots.
Step C: whenever a name has only one slot left, write it in. Repeat until all are placed.
Step D: re-read every clue against your finished picture. One broken clue means redo.

3The rules (now that you get it)

n names in n slots can be arranged in n! ways (5! = 120). Every clue deletes some of those 120.
A immediately above B: slot(A) = slot(B) + 1. A above B: slot(A) > slot(B).
A between B and C: slot(A) is strictly between slot(B) and slot(C). Exactly k between A and B: the slot numbers differ by k + 1.
Neighbouring: slots differ by 1. Slots strictly between p and q (p < q) = q − p − 1.

The n! count: Wikipedia "Permutation". Clue-deduction style, and "immediately to the right" versus "next to": Wikipedia "Logic puzzle" and "Zebra Puzzle". The slot arithmetic and the one-name-per-slot convention: derived, Python-checked (all 120 arrangements enumerated).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Five people (Asha, Bala, Charu, Dev, Esha) live on floors 1 to 5, one per floor. Dev lives on the top floor. Charu lives immediately above Bala. Esha lives on floor 2. Charu lives above Asha. Who lives on floor 3?

Step 1. Anchors: Dev = floor 5. Esha = floor 2.
Step 2. Free floors: 1, 3, 4. Free names: Asha, Bala, Charu.
Step 3. Charu is immediately above Bala, so they need two consecutive free floors. Among 1, 3, 4 the only such pair is 3 and 4. So Bala = 3, Charu = 4.
Step 4. Asha gets the last floor, 1. Check: Charu (4) above Asha (1). Yes.
Step 5. Answer: Bala (floor 3). Picture: 5 Dev, 4 Charu, 3 Bala, 2 Esha, 1 Asha.

Five boxes (Red, Blue, Green, Yellow, Pink) are stacked, position 1 at the bottom. Pink is at the top. Red is directly above Blue. Exactly one box is between Green and Pink. Yellow is somewhere above Blue. Which box is at the bottom?

Step 1. Anchor: Pink = position 5.
Step 2. Exactly one box between Green and Pink: positions differ by 2, so Green = 3.
Step 3. Free positions 1, 2, 4 for Red, Blue, Yellow. Red directly above Blue needs consecutive positions: only 1 and 2. So Blue = 1, Red = 2.
Step 4. Yellow takes position 4. Check: Yellow (4) above Blue (1). Yes.
Step 5. Answer: Blue at the bottom. Stack from the top: Pink, Yellow, Green, Red, Blue.

5The trap

Clue: "Asha lives above Bala" in a 5-floor building.
Wrong move: read it as "directly above" and put Asha exactly one floor up.
Python count: of all 120 arrangements, 60 fit "above" but only 24 fit "immediately above". The wrong reading throws away 36 valid arrangements.
Second trap, counting between. Floors 1 and 5: how many floors are between? Not 4, not 5. Floors 2, 3, 4 = 3 (that is 5 − 1 − 1).
Third trap, direction. "A is immediately above B" gives A the BIGGER number. Say it as an equation: A = B + 1.

6Speed method: biggest clue first, then test options

Order of attack. (1) Clues that name an exact slot, or top or bottom. (2) Glued pairs and "exactly k between". (3) "Between" and "above". (4) "Not" clues last, to trim what is left.
Option test. If the question gives options, put an option in and scan the clues. One broken clue kills it. This can be quicker than solving the whole puzzle.
Partial is fine. Ask whether the question needs the full order. Sometimes it does not (see quiz Q5).

Lesson 2 · Day and month puzzles (timelines)

In one line: a day or month puzzle is the same slot game on a calendar: Monday (or January) is slot 1, and "before" means a smaller number.

1Picture this

A coaching centre in Jaipur books five guest teachers, one for each day from Monday to Friday. The notice board says: "Mr Lalit comes the day before Ms Kavya." You can already feel the week as five boxes in a row.

Mon 1Tue 2Wed 3Thu 4Fri 5

Monday is slot 1. "Before" means a smaller number and "after" a bigger one. Months work the same way.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Conventions for this chapter: five working days, Monday to Friday (slots 1 to 5), one person per day. Months work the same: January to May as slots 1 to 5, one birthday per month.
The week does not wrap round here. No day comes before Monday and none comes after Friday. A real puzzle states its own days, so read the stem first.
Write the numbers under the day names: Mon 1, Tue 2, Wed 3, Thu 4, Fri 5. Now every clue is slot arithmetic you already know.
"Before" or "earlier than" means a smaller number. "After" or "later than" means a bigger number. "The day immediately after" means +1. "The day before" means −1.
"Exactly one day between A and B" means the numbers differ by 2. "Consecutive days" means they differ by 1.
"Not on Tuesday" crosses out slot 2 for that person. When a person has one uncrossed slot, place them.
Anchor first: use the fixed day or the "first day" or "last day" clue, then build outward.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Before: slot(A) < slot(B). After: slot(A) > slot(B). Immediately after: slot(A) = slot(B) + 1.
Exactly k days (or months) between A and B: slots differ by k + 1.
Days between X and Y = |slot X − slot Y| − 1.
"A visits before B" says nothing about distance. Only words like "immediately", "next day" and "consecutive" mean 1 apart.

Wikipedia "Constraint satisfaction problem": a constraint restricts which placements are allowed, and a solution satisfies all constraints at once. The slot translations of before, after and between: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Step 1. Anchor: Kavya = Friday (5).
Step 2. Lalit is the day before Kavya: Thursday (4).
Step 3. Free days: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday (1, 2, 3) for Mohan, Nisha, Ojas.
Step 4. Mohan is between Kavya (Friday, the last day) and Ojas. So Ojas must be earlier than Mohan.
Step 5. Exactly one day between them: Mohan = Ojas + 2. Among 1, 2, 3 only Ojas = 1, Mohan = 3 works.
Step 6. Nisha gets Tuesday. Answer: Nisha. Week: Ojas, Nisha, Mohan, Lalit, Kavya.

Five friends (Kavya, Lalit, Mohan, Nisha, Ojas) were born in different months from January to May. Lalit was born in March. Mohan was born in a later month than Lalit. Exactly one month falls between Lalit's and Nisha's birth months. Kavya's birth month is between Lalit's and Ojas's. Who was born in February?

Step 1. Anchor: Lalit = March (3).
Step 2. Exactly one month between Lalit and Nisha: Nisha is January (1) or May (5).
Step 3. Mohan is later than March: April or May.
Step 4. Try Nisha = January. Then Kavya, Mohan, Ojas share Feb, April, May. Kavya between Lalit and Ojas fails in every case that also keeps Mohan in April or May (checked by enumeration). So Nisha is not January.
Step 5. So Nisha = May, and Mohan = April.
Step 6. Kavya and Ojas share January and February. Kavya must be between Lalit (March) and Ojas, so Kavya = February, Ojas = January.

Lesson 3 · Table puzzles and comparison puzzles

In one line: when each person has two or more facts (colour, drink), draw a small table and fill what you know; when the clues only compare (taller, older), build a chain and ask what is fixed.

1Picture this

Four friends order at a Kolkata cafe. Everyone likes a different colour and drinks a different drink. You hear: "The blue-lover drinks coffee" and "Ravi likes red". There is no order, no floors: just a table of who has what.

PersonColourDrink
RaviRed?
Sita??
Tarun??
Uma??

Clues fill the question marks one at a time.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Convention: each person has exactly one value from each category (colour, drink), and no two people share a value.
Draw a table. Rows are people, columns are categories. Start empty.
A direct clue ("Ravi likes red") goes straight into its cell. It is settled for good.
A "not" clue ("Tarun does not like blue") becomes a small X in that cell. X marks are knowledge too, but they do not place anyone yet.
A link clue ("the blue-lover drinks coffee") says blue and coffee belong to the SAME person. Bracket them together. When one is placed, so is the other.
Elimination: if a value is crossed out for every person but one, it belongs to that one.
Comparison clues ("A is taller than B") are different. They give an order, not a table. Chain them: Esha > Dev > Charu > Bala.
Chains join at a shared name. "Asha > Bala" and "Charu > Bala" both hang from Bala, but say nothing about Asha versus Charu.
Ask the question first. "Who is shortest?" only needs one name that has everyone above it, not the full order.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Table rule: each value belongs to exactly one person per category. A link puts two values in one row. A "not" crosses one cell.
Chain rule: A > B and B > C give A > C. The ">" carries through a chain.
A person is definitely the shortest only if the clues put every other person above them, directly or through a chain. A person is definitely the tallest by the mirror rule.
If both orders of two names can happen in some valid arrangement, the clues do not decide which is taller: "cannot tell".

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Ravi, Sita, Tarun, Uma each like a different colour (Red, Blue, Green, Yellow) and drink a different drink (Tea, Coffee, Milk, Juice). Ravi likes red. Uma drinks juice. The person who likes green drinks juice. The person who likes blue drinks coffee. Tarun does not like blue. Tarun does not drink tea. What does Ravi drink?

Step 1. Write in: Ravi = Red. Uma = Juice.
Step 2. Green and Juice are one person, and Uma drinks juice. So Uma likes Green.
Step 3. Blue is not Ravi (red), not Uma (green), not Tarun (clue). So Sita likes Blue, and drinks Coffee.
Step 4. Yellow is left for Tarun.
Step 5. Tea and Milk are left for Ravi and Tarun. Tarun does not drink tea, so Tarun = Milk and Ravi = Tea.
Step 6. Answer: Tea. Table: Ravi Red Tea; Sita Blue Coffee; Tarun Yellow Milk; Uma Green Juice.

Asha is taller than Bala. Charu is taller than Bala. Dev is taller than Charu. Esha is taller than Dev. Who is the shortest?

Step 1. Chain the last three: Esha > Dev > Charu > Bala.
Step 2. Asha > Bala: Asha sits somewhere above Bala. Her place in the chain is open.
Step 3. Bala has everyone above. Answer: Bala.
Step 4. Python check: 4 arrangements fit the clues. Bala is shortest in all 4. Asha is 2nd, 3rd, 4th or 5th shortest, so her place is not fixed, and the answer does not need it.

5The trap

Same clues as above: Asha > Bala, Charu > Bala, Dev > Charu, Esha > Dev.
Wrong move: "Asha is only named above Bala, so she must be second shortest."
Python: in the 4 valid arrangements Asha is 2nd, 3rd, 4th or 5th shortest. Also, Asha is taller than Charu in some and shorter in others. The clues do not say.
Right: never fill a gap with a guess. Ask "is there a valid arrangement the other way?" If yes, you cannot tell.

Critical Reasoning

In one line: read a short statement, then decide what it quietly needs, what must follow, what weakens or strengthens it, and what sensible step to take. Not in the official syllabus (none is printed); third-party lists only.

Lesson 1 · Assumptions and conclusions

In one line: an assumption is the unspoken idea a statement needs; a conclusion follows only if it MUST be true.

1Picture this

Your mother says: “Take an umbrella, it will rain.” She is quietly counting on two things you never heard: that you will go out, and that an umbrella keeps you dry. Those unspoken supports are assumptions.

Critical reasoning questions ask you to spot the hidden support, or to say what must be true.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A statement is a short passage. It usually gives a reason for a plan or a claim. In argument language, the reasons are premises and what they aim to prove is the conclusion; inference is the step from one to the other.
An assumption is an unstated idea that must be true for the statement to make sense (the same thing as a tacit or implicit assumption).
Negation test. Imagine the assumption is false. If the statement now collapses or makes no sense, the assumption is implicit. If the statement still works, it is not.
Example: “To cut bills, the society replaced all corridor bulbs with LEDs.” Suppose LEDs do NOT use less power. The plan is pointless. So “LEDs use less power” is implicit.
Now suppose “residents will stop using corridor lights” is true. Then no bulb matters at all, so this idea works against the plan. Not implicit.
Habit (our advice, not a theorem): an assumption with big words like all, every, always, never, will stop is usually too strong. The statement only needs a little: some, at least partly.
A different question type: statement and conclusion. Take everything in the statement as true. A conclusion follows only if it must be true from the statement alone, with no outside knowledge.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Implicit assumption: true in EVERY situation where the statement’s plan or claim works. (Negation test.)
Conclusion follows: true in EVERY situation where the statement is true. If it could be false, it does not follow.
Use only the statement. Outside knowledge and new facts do not count.
Conventions in this chapter: “Some” means at least one. Answer set: Only I / Only II / Both / Neither. (Some papers, e.g. IndiaBIX, add a fifth option “Either I or II”. Items in this chapter never pair two exact opposites, so that option never arises here.)

Assumption, premise, conclusion, inference definitions: sources below. The “true in every working situation” test is derived, Python-checked by enumerating all situations of the items in this lesson.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Statement: All trains on the Konkan route were late today. Some trains on the Konkan route are express trains. I. Some express trains were late today. II. All express trains were late today.

Step 1. Draw it: a big circle “late” with the circle “Konkan trains” fully inside.
Step 2. “Some Konkan trains are express” puts at least one express train inside the Konkan circle, hence inside “late”.
Step 3. I: at least one express train is late. Must be true. Follows.
Step 4. II: other express trains outside the Konkan route could be on time. Not forced. Does not follow. Answer: Only I. (Python checked every set model on 3 trains.)

Step 1. Negate I: no woman finds fare a barrier. Then the free pass changes nothing. Plan collapses, so I is implicit.
Step 2. Negate II: no such woman can reach a workplace by bus. A free pass is useless. Plan collapses, so II is implicit.
Step 3. Answer: Both are implicit.

5The trap

Statement: “Every employee who finished the audit got a bonus. Karan got a bonus.”
Wrong move: conclude “Karan finished the audit”. It feels natural because bonus and audit are linked.
Check: the statement never says audit is the ONLY way to a bonus. Karan could have earned it another way. Both “finished” and “did not finish” are possible.
Right: “Karan finished the audit” does not follow. Nor does “Karan earned it some other way”. All-A-are-B does not reverse (Python-checked).

Lesson 2 · Strengthen, weaken and cause-effect

In one line: ask what a new fact removes: a rival cause (strengthens), the cause itself (weakens), or nothing (irrelevant).

1Picture this

At a hostel, the boys who drink protein shakes also lift heavier weights. A friend says: “Shakes make you strong.” Maybe. Or strong boys buy shakes. Or serious gym-goers do both. Strengthen and weaken questions ask: does a new fact make the claim more or less believable?

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

An argument claims that X causes Y (the cause is X, the effect is Y), because X and Y go together.
“Go together” is a correlation. Correlation alone does not prove cause. Other explanations: reverse causation (Y causes X), a common cause (a hidden Z causes both), coincidence (pure chance).
“After this, therefore because of this” (post hoc) is the mistake of thinking B was caused by A just because B came after A.
In general, an argument from samples gets stronger with a larger, more representative sample, and a single clear contrary case can hurt a universal claim.
Strengthen = a fact that rules out rival explanations (for example, people were assigned to X by lottery and the gap stayed).
Weaken = a fact that rules out the cause itself, shows the gap vanishes when like is compared with like, or points to a hidden common cause.
Irrelevant = a fact that rules out nothing (timing, colour, popularity). It changes nothing.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Four rival structures for “X and Y go together”: X causes Y · Y causes X · hidden Z causes both · chance.
A fact strengthens “X causes Y” if it removes rivals other than X→Y; weakens if it removes X→Y itself, or points to a specific rival (for example, shows a hidden Z driving both); is neutral if it does neither.
Convention in this guide: we count the four structures equally likely at the start (a teaching model, not a law). Then strengthen = share of X→Y rises; weaken = it falls. Removing a structure sets its weight to 0; evidence pointing to a rival raises that rival’s weight, which also lowers X→Y’s share.

Correlation, reverse causation, common cause, coincidence, post hoc: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Model of effects: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Claim: Students who attend the morning yoga class score higher, so yoga improves scores. Which fact strengthens it most? Students were put in the yoga group by lottery and the group still scored higher.

Step 1. Four rivals: yoga→scores, scores→yoga (high scorers join), hidden Z (study habit) → both, chance. Start: each 1 in 4.
Step 2. Lottery means nobody chose yoga based on scores or habits. That kills “scores→yoga” and “hidden Z”.
Step 3. Left: yoga→scores and chance. X→Y share rises from 25% to 50%. Strengthens.
Step 4. Opposite case: “Yoga students already scored higher the year BEFORE joining.” This removes yoga→scores. Share falls to 0. Weakens. “The class is at 6 am” removes nothing: no change.

5The trap

Claim: “After the new coach joined, the team won 5 matches in a row, so the coach is the reason.”
Wrong move: accept it because the wins came AFTER the coach.
Check: “came after” rules out none of the rivals. The wins could come from the coach, from something else that changed at the same time (say, new stronger players), or from luck. Nothing is ruled out, so the claim is no more likely than before.
Right: spot “after this, therefore because of this” (post hoc).

6Speed method: classify the fact in 5 seconds

Ask one question of each option: what does it remove? Removes the cause, or points to a rival → weakens. Removes rivals → strengthens. Neither → neutral. Also check the direction asked: “strengthen” or “weaken”.

Words that usually mean “the gap vanishes”: “when drivers of the same age are compared”, “both rise in summer”, “was already higher before”.

Lesson 3 · If-then logic and courses of action

In one line: know what an if-then rule really allows, then pick steps that tackle the stated problem without going extreme.

1Picture this

Rule at your coaching centre: “Only students who clear the entry test can join the batch.” Your friend cleared the test. Is he in the batch? You cannot say. Clearing the test is necessary, not enough. This tiny idea decides many reasoning questions, and it also tells you what a sensible course of action looks like.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

“If P then Q”: P is sufficient for Q (P guarantees Q). Q is necessary for P (without Q, no P).
P true → Q must be true. ✔ (affirm the antecedent, i.e. the “if” part)
Q false → P must be false. ✔ (contrapositive)
Q true → P can be either. ✘ P false → Q can be either. ✘ These two are the classic traps.
Course of action questions: assume the statement is true; ask which suggested step logically follows for pursuing.
A sensible step is practical, aimed at the stated problem or its cause, and not extreme or unrelated. Our test (a convention, not a theorem): follows only if it targets the problem and is neither extreme nor off-topic.
Two suggested steps can both follow, or neither. Judge each alone.

3The rules (now that you get it)

If P then Q. Valid: P true ⇒ Q true. Q false ⇒ P false.
Not valid: Q true ⇒ P true; P false ⇒ Q false. (Both are Open.)
“Only P can Q” means: if Q then P.
Course of action: assume the statement is true; pick steps that deal with the stated problem directly; reject extreme, unrelated or impossible steps.

Necessary/sufficient: Wikipedia page below. Four if-then cases: derived, Python-checked by truth table. Course-of-action convention: IndiaBIX instructions; the extreme/off-topic test is our reading.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

If it rains, the match is cancelled. The match was not cancelled. What follows?

Step 1. P = rain, Q = cancelled. Given: if P then Q, and Q is false.
Step 2. If it had rained, the match would have been cancelled. It was not.
Step 3. So it did not rain. (Truth table: only world with P⇒Q and not Q has P false.)

Statement: Many residents of a colony have diarrhoea after the water supply turned muddy. I. The municipality should check and clean the supply pipeline immediately. II. The residents should be asked to leave the colony permanently.

Step 1. Problem: muddy water, illness. Cause: pipeline or supply.
Step 2. I targets the cause and is practical. Follows.
Step 3. II is extreme and not needed. Does not follow. Answer: Only I.

5The trap

Rule: “Only candidates who cleared Phase I can sit Phase II.” Ravi cleared Phase I.
Wrong move: conclude “Ravi sits Phase II”. Clearing felt like the ticket.
Check: clearing is necessary, not sufficient. The rule does not say clearing is enough.
Right: it cannot be determined. “Only” points backwards: sits ⇒ cleared.

Write the rule as P → Q in 3 seconds. Then test only the 4 cases: P true, Q false are the safe two; Q true, P false are the two traps.

Series and Logical Misc

In one line: three small toolkits that each fit in a minute: series gaps, odd-one-out rules, and if-then logic. Only items with provable answers are used here.

Lesson 1 · Letter and number series

In one line: a series is a list with a hidden rule. Write the gaps between neighbours, find how the gaps behave, and push the rule one step further.

1Picture this

You are on a platform and the station boards show 2, 6, 12, 20 minutes between four trains in a row. Your brain asks “how much did the wait grow each time?”. Gaps 4, 6, 8. That habit, looking at the gaps and not at the numbers, is the whole skill.

+4+6, then +8, then +10

The gaps themselves form a pattern. Always check the gaps of the gaps.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A series (sequence) is a list of things in a fixed order. Each item is a term.
Alphabet first. The English alphabet has 26 letters, A first and Z last. Give each a number: A=1, B=2, … Z=26. So M = 13 and T = 20.
Convention used in this guide: after Z the alphabet wraps back to A. So three steps after X is A (X→Y→Z→A).
The gap is how many steps you move to get from one term to the next. B to D is +2. K to P is +5.
A gap can be negative: moving backwards. Z (26) to X (24) is −2. U (21) to Q (17) is −4. Example: Z, X, U, Q has gaps −2, −3, −4, so the next gap is −5 and 17 − 5 = 12 = L.
Arithmetic pattern: the gap is the same every time (2, 4, 6, 8: gap +2). Geometric pattern: each term is the previous one times a fixed number (5, 10, 20, 40: times 2).
Harder but common: the gaps grow by a fixed amount (2, 6, 12, 20: gaps 4, 6, 8) or the gaps double (3, 7, 15, 31: gaps 4, 8, 16).
Other friends: add the previous two terms (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8: Fibonacci) and squares 1, 4, 9, 16 (a number times itself) or triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10 (the gaps are 2, 3, 4, so they grow by 1; as a formula n(n+1)/2, where n is the term number: n = 4 gives 4×5/2 = 10).
Number-letter series like 2A 4C 6E: split into two series (numbers 2, 4, 6 and letters A, C, E) and solve each alone.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Gap ladder (a ratio means one term divided by the one before): (1) gaps equal → keep the gap. (2) gaps change by the same amount → keep that change. (3) gaps double (or triple) → keep that. (4) ratios equal → multiply. (5) each term = sum of previous two.
Letters: A=1 … Z=26, wrap after Z. Opposite letter of a letter: positions add to 27 (A↔Z, F↔U).
Number-letter or two-part terms: solve each part as its own series.

Sequence, arithmetic and geometric sequences, Fibonacci, squares, triangular numbers: Math Is Fun and Wikipedia pages in Sources. Alphabet order: Wikipedia English alphabet. 4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find the next term: 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ?

Step 1. Gaps: 7−3=4, 15−7=8, 31−15=16, 63−31=32.
Step 2. Gaps 4, 8, 16, 32 double each time, so the next gap is 64.
Step 3. 63 + 64 = 127.
Step 4. Cross-check with a second rule: each term = previous × 2 + 1 (3×2+1=7, 7×2+1=15, 63×2+1=127). Both rules agree.

Step 3. Next gap is +6. 16 + 6 = 22. The 22nd letter is V (count on from T=20: U=21, V=22).

Lesson 2 · Odd one out, classification, analogy

In one line: odd one out, classification and analogy all ask “what rule do these share?”. Name the rule first, then apply it to the answer.

1Picture this

Your mother sorts the vegetable basket: potato, onion, tomato, and a pencil that fell in. You spot the pencil at once because you silently named a rule (“things we cook”). Reasoning questions are the same game with numbers and letters.

4 items follow the rule1 breaks

Find the rule the four follow. The fifth is your answer.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Odd one out: four items share a property, one does not. Your job: find the shared property.
Classification means putting items into categories by shared properties. In a “which belongs with these” question, the shared property is the category.
Analogy means A is to B as C is to D. What matters is the relation inside a pair, not the items themselves.
Number properties to know cold. Prime: a whole number above 1 that cannot be made by multiplying other whole numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13). Square: a whole number times itself (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49). Cube: a whole number times itself three times (8, 27, 64, 125). Classification can use two lists at once: 4, 9, 25, 49 are squares of the primes 2, 3, 5, 7 (“squares of primes”), so 121 = 11×11 fits, but 16 = 4×4 does not (4 is not prime).
Letter groups: convert to positions and look at the gap inside each group (CE is +2, OR is +3).
Pair analogy for numbers: guess a rule from the first pair, then test it on the second pair. Keep only a rule that fits both.
Letter analogy: find the shift in each letter from the first word to its code, then apply the same shift to the new word (CAT to ECV is +2 for every letter).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Odd one out: the rule must hold for ALL FOUR others and fail for exactly one.
Analogy: the rule must fit every given pair before you apply it to the question pair. If only two pairs are given, check that no second simple rule also fits.
Prime: above 1, no whole-number product except 1 times itself. 2 is prime AND even. 1 is not prime.

Analogy definition (“A is to B as C is to D”): Wikipedia Analogy. Classification: Wikipedia Classification. Prime definition: Math Is Fun dictionary. “One rule fits only one item” checks: derived, Python-checked against a bank of simple number properties.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Odd one out: 27, 64, 100, 125, 216

Step 1. Try “cube”: 27 = 3×3×3, 64 = 4×4×4, 125 = 5×5×5, 216 = 6×6×6. 100 is not a cube (4×4×4 = 64 and 5×5×5 = 125, so no whole number cubes to 100).
Step 2. Four items follow the rule “cube of a whole number”, one fails. Answer: 100. (Our code also checked parity (odd or even), digit count, primes, squares and multiples of 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11: no rule singles out a different number.)

Analogy: 2 : 6 :: 3 : 12 :: 5 : 30 :: 4 : ?

Step 1. 2 to 6 is 2×3. 3 to 12 is 3×4. 5 to 30 is 5×6. The second factor is one more than the number.
Step 2. Rule: n × (n + 1). Check all three pairs: 2×3=6, 3×4=12, 5×6=30. Why three pairs? With only 2 : 6 and 3 : 12, the rule 6 × (n − 1) also fits (6×1=6, 6×2=12) and gives 18. Two pairs can hide two rules.
Step 3. 4 × 5 = 20. (Our code tested n(n+1), every rule a×n+b and other simple rules: only n(n+1) fits all three pairs.)

5The trap

Set: 2, 3, 5, 9, 11. Which is the odd one out?

Lesson 3 · Simple if-then logic

In one line: “if P then Q” only breaks when P is true and Q is false. From it you may flip-and-negate (contrapositive), but never just flip.

1Picture this

Your hostel rule: “If you are late, the gate is locked.” You see the gate open. So you were not late (nobody late gets in). You see the gate locked: were you late? Not sure, it might be locked for another reason, like after midnight.

gate openso not late

The first deduction is safe. The second is a guess.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. We call them P, Q, R.
“If P then Q” is a promise. It is broken only when P happens and Q does not. P false: promise not broken, Q can be anything.
Four cousins of “If P then Q”. Converse: if Q then P. Inverse: if not P then not Q. Contrapositive: if not Q then not P. (“Not P” just means P is false.)
Only the contrapositive says the same thing as the original. Converse and inverse can be false even when the original is true.
“P only if Q” means: if P then Q (P cannot happen without Q). “Ravi passes only if he studies”: passing needs studying.
“Or” in this guide means at least one is true (inclusive). “P or Q” with P false forces Q true.
Negation of a statement is its exact opposite: true exactly when the original is false. Not (P and Q) = (not P) or (not Q): “not both rich and famous” means “not rich, or not famous”. Not (P or Q) = (not P) and (not Q). Checked on all four true/false pairs (Python).
Negation of “every student passed” is “at least one student did not pass”, not “no student passed”.
Truth-teller puzzles: knights always tell the truth, knaves always lie. Try each assignment and keep the one that does not contradict itself.

3The rules (now that you get it)

If P then Q is equivalent to: if not Q then not P.
Not equivalent: converse (if Q then P) and inverse (if not P then not Q).
If P then Q is false only when P is true and Q is false.
Not (P and Q) = (not P) or (not Q). Not (P or Q) = (not P) and (not Q). Not every = at least one not.

Conditional truth table and contrapositive: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Knights and knaves definition: Wikipedia. “Only if”, De Morgan rules, negation of every, chain deduction and the knight/knave example: derived, Python-checked by full truth-table enumeration.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

If P then Q. If Q then R. R is false. What follows?

Step 1. R false. “If Q then R” would break if Q were true, so Q is false.
Step 2. Q false. “If P then Q” would break if P were true, so P is false.
Step 3. All three false: P false and Q false. (Check by listing every true/false combination of P, Q, R: the only combination where both conditionals hold with R false is P, Q, R all false.)

A says: “At least one of us two is a knave.” Who is who?

Step 1. Suppose A is a knave. Then the statement is a lie, so nobody is a knave. But A is a knave. Contradiction (it clashes with itself, so the supposition is impossible).
Step 2. So A is a knight, and the statement is true: at least one knave, and it is not A.
Step 3. A knight, B knave. (Brute force means trying every case: all four knight/knave assignments: only this one is consistent.)

5The trap

Statement: “If a number ends in 0, it is divisible by 5.” True.
Wrong move: flip it. “If divisible by 5, it ends in 0.”
Test 15: divisible by 5, does not end in 0. The flipped statement is false.
Right: flip AND negate. “If not divisible by 5, it does not end in 0.” Example 7: not divisible by 5, ends in 7. Fine.

6Speed method: write it as arrows

Write the statement as P → Q on rough paper. Then the only safe moves are: P true gives Q true; Q false gives P false.

Routine. (1) Rewrite “if” and “only if” sentences as P → Q. (2) Mark what you are told: true or false. (3) Move along the arrow forward from true, backward from false. (4) Anything else: “cannot be decided”.

Lesson 1 · Elimination and the guess rule

In one line: a guess is only worth making when your chance of being right is above about 23%.

1Picture this

You are in a Pune chai shop. The menu has four snacks and you cannot taste them first. You already know two are stale, so you cross them out. Now you pick between two. That is a fair bet, much better than picking from four. Exam options work the same way: every option you can prove wrong makes the remaining guess better.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Only one option is right. Your job is to cross out options, not to "find" the answer by force.
Process of elimination means finding the answer by excluding the options that cannot be it.
Chance of being right if you guess among n options left = 1 out of n. Four left: 1/4 = 25%. Three left: 1/3 = 33%. Two left: 1/2 = 50%.
Expected value (EV) is the average result if you could repeat the same bet many times: multiply each outcome by its probability and add. Here: EV = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25, where p is your chance of being right.
If EV is above 0, guessing gains marks on average. If it is below 0, it loses marks on average. EV = 0 happens at p = 0.25 ÷ (0.8333 + 0.25), which is about 23%: 0.25 ÷ 1.0833 = 0.2308.
Blank with 4 options (p = 1/4): 0.25 × 0.8333 = 0.2083 gained, 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.1875 lost, EV = +0.02, just above zero.
Cross out one option (p = 1/3): EV = +0.11 (worked on the next cards). Two left (p = 1/2): 0.5 × 0.8333 − 0.5 × 0.25 = +0.29. Five options blind (p = 1/5): 0.1667 − 0.2 = −0.03.

3The rule (now that you get it)

EV of a guess = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25
Answer if p is above about 23%. Skip if p is below it.
23% break-even
4 left: 25%
3 left: 33%
2 left: 50%

4Worked example, one tap per step

You have crossed out one of four options. Should you guess among the other three?

Step 1. Three options left, so p = 1/3 = 33%.
Step 2. Gain part: 1/3 × 0.8333 = 0.2778.
Step 3. Loss part: 2/3 × 0.25 = 0.1667.
Step 4. EV = 0.2778 − 0.1667 = +0.11. And 33% is above 23%. Answer it.

5The trap

Trap 1: "I will guess everything, it is free." It is not free. A blind guess from 4 options is only +0.02 on average, and from 5 options it is −0.03.
Twenty blind 4-option guesses: average 20 × 0.0208 = +0.42 marks in total, with a typical swing (spread) of about ±2.1 marks (derived, Python-checked). The swing is five times the average, so the result is mostly luck.
Trap 2: "I crossed one out, so I am 50% sure." No. Crossing out one of four leaves three, so 33%.
Trap 3: crossing out an option because it "looks odd" is not elimination. Cross out only what you can prove wrong, for example a number that breaks a rule you have checked.

6Speed method + drill

Our suggestion (strategy, not a fact): the 3-second routine: (1) read the question again, (2) cross out every option that breaks a fact you checked, (3) count what is left, then apply the rule: 3 or 2 left, answer; 4 left, only if you feel a pull toward one (the maths says a 4-option guess is barely positive, +0.02).

Quick EV table (4-option paper, derived, Python-checked). 4 left: +0.02. 3 left: +0.11. 2 left: +0.29. 5 left (if a paper has 5 options): −0.03.

1Picture this

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Paper 1 has 90 questions and one shared 60-minute timer (official notification).

Reasoning has 30 questions. At the average pace that is 30 × 40 s = 20 minutes of the pot.
Some questions take 15 seconds, some 2 minutes. The average only works if you stop the 2-minute ones from becoming 5-minute ones.

One stalled 3-minute question costs 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5 average questions of time.
Expected marks from several guesses simply add up: 6 questions each worth +0.29 on average give about 6 × 0.2917 = +1.75 (derived, Python-checked).

Seconds per question = total seconds ÷ questions. Paper 1: 3600 ÷ 90 = 40 s.

Our suggestion, last-10-minutes plan: (1) go back to marked questions, (2) finish those with 2 or 3 options left, (3) leave 4-option blanks only if you have no pull toward any option.

Lesson 3 · Resetting after a hard puzzle

In one line: a bad question is one question. Reset in 20 seconds and the next one is worth full marks again.

1Picture this

A batter in a local cricket match gets bowled for a duck (out for zero runs) in the first innings. The second innings still starts at zero. An exam works like that: the next question does not know that the last one went badly, and it is worth the same.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Assumption (stated): every question in a section carries equal marks, as the notification's table suggests. Then a hard puzzle is worth 0.8333, the same as an easy one.
Marks per minute = marks you can earn ÷ minutes it takes. Hard questions cost more minutes for the same marks. A 5-question puzzle set that needs 8 minutes pays 5 × 0.8333 ÷ 8 = 0.52 marks per minute. Short questions at 40 s each pay 0.8333 ÷ (40 ÷ 60) = 1.25 marks per minute.
Our suggestion: when you are stuck, the best move is usually to go where the marks per minute are higher.
One right answer wipes out a lot: +0.8333 covers three wrong answers (−0.75) with 0.083 to spare. A bad patch is recoverable.
Reset routine (our suggestion, about 20 seconds): put the pen down, take three slow breaths, say "next question", read it fresh. Diaphragmatic (slow belly) breathing "may help with relaxation", per the source; this is not proven for exams.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Marks per minute = marks available ÷ minutes needed. Our suggestion: spend time where it is higher.
After a stall: pen down, 3 slow breaths, "next question". Our suggestion, not a fact.
3 wrong answers = −0.75. One right answer = +0.8333. One right puts you back above zero.
