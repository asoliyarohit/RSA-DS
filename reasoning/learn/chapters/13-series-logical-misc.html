Series and Logical Misc

In one line: three small toolkits that each fit in a minute: series gaps, odd-one-out rules, and if-then logic. Only items with provable answers are used here.

Lesson 1 · Letter and number series

In one line: a series is a list with a hidden rule. Write the gaps between neighbours, find how the gaps behave, and push the rule one step further.

1Picture this

You are on a platform and the station boards show 2, 6, 12, 20 minutes between four trains in a row. Your brain asks “how much did the wait grow each time?”. Gaps 4, 6, 8. That habit, looking at the gaps and not at the numbers, is the whole skill.

+4+6, then +8, then +10

The gaps themselves form a pattern. Always check the gaps of the gaps.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A series (sequence) is a list of things in a fixed order. Each item is a term.
Alphabet first. The English alphabet has 26 letters, A first and Z last. Give each a number: A=1, B=2, … Z=26. So M = 13 and T = 20.
Convention used in this guide: after Z the alphabet wraps back to A. So three steps after X is A (X→Y→Z→A).
The gap is how many steps you move to get from one term to the next. B to D is +2. K to P is +5.
A gap can be negative: moving backwards. Z (26) to X (24) is −2. U (21) to Q (17) is −4. Example: Z, X, U, Q has gaps −2, −3, −4, so the next gap is −5 and 17 − 5 = 12 = L.
Arithmetic pattern: the gap is the same every time (2, 4, 6, 8: gap +2). Geometric pattern: each term is the previous one times a fixed number (5, 10, 20, 40: times 2).
Harder but common: the gaps grow by a fixed amount (2, 6, 12, 20: gaps 4, 6, 8) or the gaps double (3, 7, 15, 31: gaps 4, 8, 16).
Other friends: add the previous two terms (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8: Fibonacci) and squares 1, 4, 9, 16 (a number times itself) or triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10 (the gaps are 2, 3, 4, so they grow by 1; as a formula n(n+1)/2, where n is the term number: n = 4 gives 4×5/2 = 10).
Number-letter series like 2A 4C 6E: split into two series (numbers 2, 4, 6 and letters A, C, E) and solve each alone.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Gap ladder (a ratio means one term divided by the one before): (1) gaps equal → keep the gap. (2) gaps change by the same amount → keep that change. (3) gaps double (or triple) → keep that. (4) ratios equal → multiply. (5) each term = sum of previous two.
Letters: A=1 … Z=26, wrap after Z. Opposite letter of a letter: positions add to 27 (A↔Z, F↔U).
Number-letter or two-part terms: solve each part as its own series.

Sequence, arithmetic and geometric sequences, Fibonacci, squares, triangular numbers: Math Is Fun and Wikipedia pages in Sources. Alphabet order: Wikipedia English alphabet. 4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find the next term: 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ?

Step 1. Gaps: 7−3=4, 15−7=8, 31−15=16, 63−31=32.
Step 2. Gaps 4, 8, 16, 32 double each time, so the next gap is 64.
Step 3. 63 + 64 = 127.
Step 4. Cross-check with a second rule: each term = previous × 2 + 1 (3×2+1=7, 7×2+1=15, 63×2+1=127). Both rules agree.

Find the next letter: B, D, G, K, P, ?

Step 1. Positions: B=2, D=4, G=7, K=11, P=16.
Step 2. Gaps: +2, +3, +4, +5. The gap grows by 1.
Step 3. Next gap is +6. 16 + 6 = 22. The 22nd letter is V (count on from T=20: U=21, V=22).

5The trap

Series: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?
Wrong move: look only at the last step (+10) and repeat it, giving 40.
Look at the gaps: 4, 6, 8, 10. They are not constant, they grow by 2. The next gap is 12.
Right: 30 + 12 = 42. Second trap: letters wrap. After Z comes A, so R, U, X, A, D, G is a normal +3 series.

6Speed method: the gap ladder

Spend about 20 seconds: write the gaps under the series, then the gaps of the gaps. Stop at the first level that is flat or doubling.

Routine. (1) Gaps flat? (2) Gaps of gaps flat? (3) Gaps double? (4) Ratio flat? (5) Sum of two before? Not found in 30 seconds: skip, no negative marks for skipping.

Lesson 2 · Odd one out, classification, analogy

In one line: odd one out, classification and analogy all ask “what rule do these share?”. Name the rule first, then apply it to the answer.

1Picture this

Your mother sorts the vegetable basket: potato, onion, tomato, and a pencil that fell in. You spot the pencil at once because you silently named a rule (“things we cook”). Reasoning questions are the same game with numbers and letters.

4 items follow the rule1 breaks

Find the rule the four follow. The fifth is your answer.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Odd one out: four items share a property, one does not. Your job: find the shared property.
Classification means putting items into categories by shared properties. In a “which belongs with these” question, the shared property is the category.
Analogy means A is to B as C is to D. What matters is the relation inside a pair, not the items themselves.
Number properties to know cold. Prime: a whole number above 1 that cannot be made by multiplying other whole numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13). Square: a whole number times itself (4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49). Cube: a whole number times itself three times (8, 27, 64, 125). Classification can use two lists at once: 4, 9, 25, 49 are squares of the primes 2, 3, 5, 7 (“squares of primes”), so 121 = 11×11 fits, but 16 = 4×4 does not (4 is not prime).
Letter groups: convert to positions and look at the gap inside each group (CE is +2, OR is +3).
Pair analogy for numbers: guess a rule from the first pair, then test it on the second pair. Keep only a rule that fits both.
Letter analogy: find the shift in each letter from the first word to its code, then apply the same shift to the new word (CAT to ECV is +2 for every letter).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Odd one out: the rule must hold for ALL FOUR others and fail for exactly one.
Analogy: the rule must fit every given pair before you apply it to the question pair. If only two pairs are given, check that no second simple rule also fits.
Prime: above 1, no whole-number product except 1 times itself. 2 is prime AND even. 1 is not prime.

Analogy definition (“A is to B as C is to D”): Wikipedia Analogy. Classification: Wikipedia Classification. Prime definition: Math Is Fun dictionary. “One rule fits only one item” checks: derived, Python-checked against a bank of simple number properties.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Odd one out: 27, 64, 100, 125, 216

Step 1. Try “cube”: 27 = 3×3×3, 64 = 4×4×4, 125 = 5×5×5, 216 = 6×6×6. 100 is not a cube (4×4×4 = 64 and 5×5×5 = 125, so no whole number cubes to 100).
Step 2. Four items follow the rule “cube of a whole number”, one fails. Answer: 100. (Our code also checked parity (odd or even), digit count, primes, squares and multiples of 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11: no rule singles out a different number.)

Analogy: 2 : 6 :: 3 : 12 :: 5 : 30 :: 4 : ?

Step 1. 2 to 6 is 2×3. 3 to 12 is 3×4. 5 to 30 is 5×6. The second factor is one more than the number.
Step 2. Rule: n × (n + 1). Check all three pairs: 2×3=6, 3×4=12, 5×6=30. Why three pairs? With only 2 : 6 and 3 : 12, the rule 6 × (n − 1) also fits (6×1=6, 6×2=12) and gives 18. Two pairs can hide two rules.
Step 3. 4 × 5 = 20. (Our code tested n(n+1), every rule a×n+b and other simple rules: only n(n+1) fits all three pairs.)

5The trap

Set: 2, 3, 5, 9, 11. Which is the odd one out?
Rule “prime” isolates 9. Rule “odd” isolates 2. Rule “below 10” isolates 11. Three rules, three different answers.
Lesson: one rule is never enough. If two good rules point to different items, the question is flawed or your rule is too loose. Look for a cleaner set.
In the guide quizzes we only use sets where every natural rule agrees. In the exam, if you cannot find one clean rule in about 30 seconds, skip (strategy, not fact).

Lesson 3 · Simple if-then logic

In one line: “if P then Q” only breaks when P is true and Q is false. From it you may flip-and-negate (contrapositive), but never just flip.

1Picture this

Your hostel rule: “If you are late, the gate is locked.” You see the gate open. So you were not late (nobody late gets in). You see the gate locked: were you late? Not sure, it might be locked for another reason, like after midnight.

gate openso not late

The first deduction is safe. The second is a guess.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. We call them P, Q, R.
“If P then Q” is a promise. It is broken only when P happens and Q does not. P false: promise not broken, Q can be anything.
Four cousins of “If P then Q”. Converse: if Q then P. Inverse: if not P then not Q. Contrapositive: if not Q then not P. (“Not P” just means P is false.)
Only the contrapositive says the same thing as the original. Converse and inverse can be false even when the original is true.
“P only if Q” means: if P then Q (P cannot happen without Q). “Ravi passes only if he studies”: passing needs studying.
“Or” in this guide means at least one is true (inclusive). “P or Q” with P false forces Q true.
Negation of a statement is its exact opposite: true exactly when the original is false. Not (P and Q) = (not P) or (not Q): “not both rich and famous” means “not rich, or not famous”. Not (P or Q) = (not P) and (not Q). Checked on all four true/false pairs (Python).
Negation of “every student passed” is “at least one student did not pass”, not “no student passed”.
Truth-teller puzzles: knights always tell the truth, knaves always lie. Try each assignment and keep the one that does not contradict itself.

3The rules (now that you get it)

If P then Q is equivalent to: if not Q then not P.
Not equivalent: converse (if Q then P) and inverse (if not P then not Q).
If P then Q is false only when P is true and Q is false.
Not (P and Q) = (not P) or (not Q). Not (P or Q) = (not P) and (not Q). Not every = at least one not.

Conditional truth table and contrapositive: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Knights and knaves definition: Wikipedia. “Only if”, De Morgan rules, negation of every, chain deduction and the knight/knave example: derived, Python-checked by full truth-table enumeration.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

If P then Q. If Q then R. R is false. What follows?

Step 1. R false. “If Q then R” would break if Q were true, so Q is false.
Step 2. Q false. “If P then Q” would break if P were true, so P is false.
Step 3. All three false: P false and Q false. (Check by listing every true/false combination of P, Q, R: the only combination where both conditionals hold with R false is P, Q, R all false.)

A says: “At least one of us two is a knave.” Who is who?

Step 1. Suppose A is a knave. Then the statement is a lie, so nobody is a knave. But A is a knave. Contradiction (it clashes with itself, so the supposition is impossible).
Step 2. So A is a knight, and the statement is true: at least one knave, and it is not A.
Step 3. A knight, B knave. (Brute force means trying every case: all four knight/knave assignments: only this one is consistent.)

5The trap

Statement: “If a number ends in 0, it is divisible by 5.” True.
Wrong move: flip it. “If divisible by 5, it ends in 0.”
Test 15: divisible by 5, does not end in 0. The flipped statement is false.
Right: flip AND negate. “If not divisible by 5, it does not end in 0.” Example 7: not divisible by 5, ends in 7. Fine.

6Speed method: write it as arrows

Write the statement as P → Q on rough paper. Then the only safe moves are: P true gives Q true; Q false gives P false.

Routine. (1) Rewrite “if” and “only if” sentences as P → Q. (2) Mark what you are told: true or false. (3) Move along the arrow forward from true, backward from false. (4) Anything else: “cannot be decided”.
