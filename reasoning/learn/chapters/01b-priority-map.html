Priority Map

In one line: puzzles and seating arrangements are the biggest block everywhere, so learn them first, then the short topics that fill the rest.

1Picture this

You have 30 questions in a shared 60-minute paper. A few long puzzle sets are like three big parcels at a post office counter. They hold many of your marks but take the most time. The short questions, like coding or blood relations, are the small envelopes. A good clerk knows which parcels are worth opening first.

Honest warning. These counts come from coaching-site "memory-based" analyses, not official papers. Sites disagree. A few years is a hint, not a forecast.

2What the papers show, one body at a time

PFRDA 2020 (one source, ixambee, reported in memory-based exam analyses): 40 reasoning questions. Seating 10, puzzle 5, input-output 5, coding-decoding 5, syllogism 5, commonsense reasoning 7, inequalities 3. Seating plus puzzles was 15 of 40, which is 37.5%.
PFRDA 2022 (5 Nov, edutap and practicemock agree): 20 questions. Seating 6, puzzle 5, data sufficiency 5, syllogism 2, coded inequality 1, direction 1. Seating plus puzzles was 11 of 20, which is 55%. The pages' own "11 questions" grouping does not match their table, so trust the table.
PFRDA 2025 (6 Sep): 20 questions, sources differ. Puzzles 9 or 10, input-output 4 or 5, critical reasoning 2 or 3, syllogism 2, alphabet/alphanumeric series 1, coding 0 or 1. Good attempts "9+" (edutap) against "about 14 to 16" (practicemock).
IRDAI Assistant Manager (40 reasoning questions; 2017, 2023, 2024 only): puzzles plus seating roughly 15 to 20 of 40. SEBI Grade A (20 questions): about 12 of 20 in 2024 and 2025. RBI Grade B (60 questions): about 25 to 30 of 60. Sources vary on every number.
The 2026 paper: the official notification lists Reasoning as 30 questions for 25 marks (90 questions in Paper 1). One coaching page still says 20, which does not match the official PDF. No 2026 topic weights exist yet, and every topic list is third-party.
One clear signal: puzzles and seating are the largest block at every body where counts exist. The rest of the marks rotate among short topics from year to year.

Sources: research/pfrda-reasoning-trends.md and research/other-bodies-reasoning-trends.md in this repo, each citing its pages (edutap, practicemock, oliveboard, ixambee, c4scourses). Percentages computed: 15/40 = 37.5%, 11/20 = 55%, 12/20 = 60%.

5Order of attack (coaching opinion, sources conflict)

  • Skip a stuck puzzle: several coaching pages say to give up a puzzle you cannot set up within about 3 to 4 minutes (Oliveboard SEBI page, Practicemock RBI page). Opinion, not tested.
  • Short topics first: C4S (IRDAI) and Oliveboard (SEBI) suggest syllogism, inequality, blood relations and direction before puzzles.
  • Puzzles first: Practicemock's SEBI page treats puzzles and seating as the most important scoring topics. Sources differ with the others.

No named topper's own Reasoning order was found. Try the short-topics-first order in your mocks and keep the one that scores better for you.

6The trap

Trap: spending 12 minutes on one hard puzzle set because "puzzles are the biggest block".
Maths: with 60 minutes shared by 90 questions, you get about 40 seconds a question on average (3600 ÷ 90 = 40). One 12-minute puzzle eats the time of 18 average questions.
Each right answer is worth 25 ÷ 30 = 0.8333 marks. Learn the puzzles so you can do them fast. Skip the one that will not open.

7How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • A five-question set: "Eight people sit in a row. B is third to the left of D..." followed by five questions on the same arrangement.
  • Single questions: a syllogism with two statements and two conclusions, a coded inequality, a blood-relation chain, a direction walk.
  • Reported in memory-based exam analyses: a set of seating plus puzzle questions made a large share of the section in each year with data.

8Quiz · exam-scale marks

Right = +0.8333, wrong = −0.25 (set by you for this guide; the official wording is ¼ of the marks assigned, so check your call letter).
