Safety Net: what to do when you blank

In one line: when a question freezes you, you do not fight it. You run a fixed routine: cross out, decide, move on.

Label: the arithmetic here is checked in Python. The advice (skip rules, time plans, reset steps) is our suggested strategy, not an official fact. Marks used: +0.8333 per right answer (Reasoning: 25 marks ÷ 30 questions = 0.8333), −0.25 per wrong answer (flat, as this guide assumes; the call letter settles the real rule).

Lesson 1 · Elimination and the guess rule

In one line: a guess is only worth making when your chance of being right is above about 23%.

1Picture this

You are in a Pune chai shop. The menu has four snacks and you cannot taste them first. You already know two are stale, so you cross them out. Now you pick between two. That is a fair bet, much better than picking from four. Exam options work the same way: every option you can prove wrong makes the remaining guess better.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Only one option is right. Your job is to cross out options, not to "find" the answer by force.
Process of elimination means finding the answer by excluding the options that cannot be it.
Chance of being right if you guess among n options left = 1 out of n. Four left: 1/4 = 25%. Three left: 1/3 = 33%. Two left: 1/2 = 50%.
Expected value (EV) is the average result if you could repeat the same bet many times: multiply each outcome by its probability and add. Here: EV = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25, where p is your chance of being right.
If EV is above 0, guessing gains marks on average. If it is below 0, it loses marks on average. EV = 0 happens at p = 0.25 ÷ (0.8333 + 0.25), which is about 23%: 0.25 ÷ 1.0833 = 0.2308.
Blank with 4 options (p = 1/4): 0.25 × 0.8333 = 0.2083 gained, 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.1875 lost, EV = +0.02, just above zero.
Cross out one option (p = 1/3): EV = +0.11 (worked on the next cards). Two left (p = 1/2): 0.5 × 0.8333 − 0.5 × 0.25 = +0.29. Five options blind (p = 1/5): 0.1667 − 0.2 = −0.03.

3The rule (now that you get it)

EV of a guess = p × 0.8333 − (1 − p) × 0.25
Answer if p is above about 23%. Skip if p is below it.
23% break-even
4 left: 25%
3 left: 33%
2 left: 50%

4Worked example, one tap per step

You have crossed out one of four options. Should you guess among the other three?

Step 1. Three options left, so p = 1/3 = 33%.
Step 2. Gain part: 1/3 × 0.8333 = 0.2778.
Step 3. Loss part: 2/3 × 0.25 = 0.1667.
Step 4. EV = 0.2778 − 0.1667 = +0.11. And 33% is above 23%. Answer it.

5The trap

Trap 1: "I will guess everything, it is free." It is not free. A blind guess from 4 options is only +0.02 on average, and from 5 options it is −0.03.
Twenty blind 4-option guesses: average 20 × 0.0208 = +0.42 marks in total, with a typical swing (spread) of about ±2.1 marks (derived, Python-checked). The swing is five times the average, so the result is mostly luck.
Trap 2: "I crossed one out, so I am 50% sure." No. Crossing out one of four leaves three, so 33%.
Trap 3: crossing out an option because it "looks odd" is not elimination. Cross out only what you can prove wrong, for example a number that breaks a rule you have checked.

6Speed method + drill

Our suggestion (strategy, not a fact): the 3-second routine: (1) read the question again, (2) cross out every option that breaks a fact you checked, (3) count what is left, then apply the rule: 3 or 2 left, answer; 4 left, only if you feel a pull toward one (the maths says a 4-option guess is barely positive, +0.02).

Quick EV table (4-option paper, derived, Python-checked). 4 left: +0.02. 3 left: +0.11. 2 left: +0.29. 5 left (if a paper has 5 options): −0.03.

Lesson 2 · Time: skip rules and the last 10 minutes

In one line: 60 minutes is one shared pot for 90 questions, so a stuck question steals time from easy marks elsewhere.

1Picture this

You are at a railway ticket queue with 40 seconds for each person behind you. One passenger starts arguing about a fare for 3 minutes. Four or five people behind him wait for nothing. A hard question is that passenger: it eats the time of the easy questions behind it.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Paper 1 has 90 questions and one shared 60-minute timer (official notification).
Average time = 60 min × 60 s ÷ 90 = 40 seconds per question.
Reasoning has 30 questions. At the average pace that is 30 × 40 s = 20 minutes of the pot.
Some questions take 15 seconds, some 2 minutes. The average only works if you stop the 2-minute ones from becoming 5-minute ones.
Time-boxing means giving an activity a maximum block of time (a "timebox"). Our suggestion (not a fact): after about 90 seconds on one question with no progress, mark it, take your best guess only if the rule from Lesson 1 allows, and move on.
One stalled 3-minute question costs 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5 average questions of time.
Expected marks from several guesses simply add up: 6 questions each worth +0.29 on average give about 6 × 0.2917 = +1.75 (derived, Python-checked).

3The rule (now that you get it)

Seconds per question = total seconds ÷ questions. Paper 1: 3600 ÷ 90 = 40 s.
Our suggestion, skip rule: no progress by about 90 seconds, then mark and move on.
Our suggestion, last-10-minutes plan: (1) go back to marked questions, (2) finish those with 2 or 3 options left, (3) leave 4-option blanks only if you have no pull toward any option.

Paper timing: PFRDA 2026 notification. Time-boxing definition: Wikipedia "Timeboxing". 40 s and 4.5 questions: derived, Python-checked. The 90-second cap and the 10-minute plan are our suggestions, not tested facts.

4Worked example, one tap per step

With 10 minutes left you have 12 unattempted questions. How much time can each get?

Step 1. 10 minutes = 600 seconds.
Step 2. 600 ÷ 12 = 50 seconds each.
Step 3. Split them: if 6 are easy (25 s) and 6 are hard (75 s) it still fits: 150 + 450 = 600.
Step 4. Our suggestion: do the 25-second ones first. They are the same marks for half the time.

5The trap

Trap: "I have already spent 4 minutes, so I must finish this puzzle." That is the sunk cost fallacy: letting time already spent decide a future choice.
The 4 minutes are gone either way. Ask only: what will the next 2 minutes buy here, versus on other questions?
Second trap: skipping and never returning. Our suggestion: mark skipped questions on your rough sheet by number so the last-10-minutes plan can find them.

6Speed method + drill

Three passes (our suggestion, not a fact): Pass 1 take every question that starts moving within 10 seconds. Pass 2 return to the marked ones. Pass 3 in the final minutes apply the Lesson 1 rule to what is left.

Lesson 3 · Resetting after a hard puzzle

In one line: a bad question is one question. Reset in 20 seconds and the next one is worth full marks again.

1Picture this

A batter in a local cricket match gets bowled for a duck (out for zero runs) in the first innings. The second innings still starts at zero. An exam works like that: the next question does not know that the last one went badly, and it is worth the same.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Assumption (stated): every question in a section carries equal marks, as the notification's table suggests. Then a hard puzzle is worth 0.8333, the same as an easy one.
Marks per minute = marks you can earn ÷ minutes it takes. Hard questions cost more minutes for the same marks. A 5-question puzzle set that needs 8 minutes pays 5 × 0.8333 ÷ 8 = 0.52 marks per minute. Short questions at 40 s each pay 0.8333 ÷ (40 ÷ 60) = 1.25 marks per minute.
Our suggestion: when you are stuck, the best move is usually to go where the marks per minute are higher.
One right answer wipes out a lot: +0.8333 covers three wrong answers (−0.75) with 0.083 to spare. A bad patch is recoverable.
Reset routine (our suggestion, about 20 seconds): put the pen down, take three slow breaths, say "next question", read it fresh. Diaphragmatic (slow belly) breathing "may help with relaxation", per the source; this is not proven for exams.

3The rule (now that you get it)

Marks per minute = marks available ÷ minutes needed. Our suggestion: spend time where it is higher.
After a stall: pen down, 3 slow breaths, "next question". Our suggestion, not a fact.
3 wrong answers = −0.75. One right answer = +0.8333. One right puts you back above zero.

Sunk cost: Wikipedia "Sunk cost". Breathing "may help with relaxation": Wikipedia "Diaphragmatic breathing". Marks per minute and the −0.75 versus +0.8333 comparison: derived, Python-checked. The 20-second routine is our suggestion, not a tested method.

4Worked example, one tap per step

A 5-question puzzle set needs about 8 minutes. Is it a good use of time early on?

Step 1. Marks available: 5 × 0.8333 = 4.167.
Step 2. Marks per minute: 4.167 ÷ 8 = 0.52.
Step 3. Short questions at 40 s each give 0.8333 ÷ (40 ÷ 60) = 1.25 marks per minute.
Step 4. Our suggestion: take the short ones first, then come back to the set with whatever time is left. (This changes if you are very fast at puzzles. Use your own mock times.)

5The trap

Trap: carrying the last question's mood into the next one. Two wrong in a row make people read slowly and doubt correct answers.
Fix: the marks maths. Two wrong answers cost only −0.50, and one right answer gives back +0.8333.
Second trap: "changing" answers out of panic. Our suggestion: change an answer only if you can name the specific step you got wrong.

6Speed method + drill

Our suggestion (not a fact), when you blank: (1) pen down, (2) three slow breaths, (3) re-read the question word by word, (4) cross out what you can prove wrong, (5) apply the 23% rule, (6) mark it and go. Six steps, about 30 seconds.
