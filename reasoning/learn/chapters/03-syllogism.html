Syllogism

In one line: a syllogism question asks what MUST be true when a few statements about groups are true, and the answer comes from drawing circles, never from common sense.

Lesson 1 · The four statements and reversing them

In one line: learn what All, No, Some and Some-not mean as pictures, then learn which swaps are safe.

1Picture this

In your hostel, a notice says: “All hostel students must carry an ID card.” Draw one big circle for ID-card holders and a small circle for hostel students inside it.

ID-card holdershostel students sit inside

Can you say “all ID-card holders are hostel students”? No. Visitors carry ID cards too. That one thought is half of syllogism.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Question format: you get statements (facts to accept as true) and conclusions (claims you must test against them).
Think of each group as a circle (a set). A statement tells you how two circles sit.
All pens are books: the pens circle is fully inside the books circle.
No pens are books: the circles are apart. They do not touch.
Some pens are books: the circles overlap, with at least one item in the overlap. “Some” means at least one, maybe all.
Some pens are not books: at least one pen sits outside the books circle.
Rule of the game: treat the statements as true, even if real life disagrees (a pen can be a book here).
Rule of the game: every group named has at least one member (no empty circles).
A conclusion definitely follows only if it is true in EVERY picture the statements allow. One legal picture that breaks it means it does not follow.
A conclusion true in some legal pictures but not all is only possible, so it does not follow. One true in no picture is never true. (Lesson 3 uses these names fully.)
Reverse means swapping the two groups. No and Some swap freely. All swaps only into a weaker Some. Some-not does not swap at all.

3The rules (now that you get it)

All A are B ⇒ Some A are B, Some B are A. (NOT All B are A.)
No A is B ⇒ No B is A, Some A are not B, Some B are not A.
Some A are B ⇒ Some B are A. Nothing else.
Some A are not B ⇒ nothing new. (Some B are not A does NOT follow.)

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Statement: All pens are books. Does “Some books are pens” follow?

Step 1. Draw the pens circle inside the books circle.
Step 2. Pens exist (no empty circles), so at least one item sits in pens.
Step 3. That item is inside books too, so some books are pens.
Step 4. Answer: follows. (Checked: true in all pictures.)

Statement: No pens are books. Does “Some pens are not books” follow?

Step 1. Draw two separate circles.
Step 2. Pens exist, and none is inside books.
Step 3. So at least one pen is not a book.
Step 4. Answer: follows. This only works because we agreed pens is not empty.

5The trap

Statement: All doctors are men. Conclusion: All men are doctors.
Wrong move: flip the sentence. It feels natural.
Draw it: doctors circle inside the men circle. Men can be a much bigger circle.
Right: after All, the only safe reverse is Some men are doctors.

6Speed method: say the shape, then test the swap

10-second routine. (1) Name the picture: inside, apart, overlap, or sticking out. (2) Ask: is the conclusion true in that picture AND in any other legal picture? (3) If a single legal picture breaks it, cross it out. Memory line: No and Some swap freely. All shrinks to Some. Some-not never swaps.

Speed drill

Say Follows or Does not follow before you tap Show. Premise on the left of the bar, conclusion on the right. “Only possible” means true in some pictures but not all, so it does not follow.

7Lab: one-statement tester

Pick a statement, then a conclusion. “Only a few” is taught in lesson 3, so leave it for now. A “valid picture” is one way of filling the circles that keeps your statement true. The tester tries every picture of three circles (pens, books, cups) with no empty circle and tells you if the conclusion is definite, only possible, or impossible.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • “Statement: Some books are pens. Conclusions: I. Some pens are books. II. Some books are not pens.” Options: Only I, Only II, Both, Neither.
  • “Which of the following definitely follows?” with four statements to choose from.
  • Look out for: the wrong reverse of All, and the word “Some” sneaking into a No-statement.
How wrong options are built. Common plants are the reverse of All, “Some not” offered as certain, and a conclusion that is merely possible. If an option looks right only because it sounds natural, draw the picture.

Lesson 2 · Two statements, one conclusion

In one line: link two statements through the shared group, then test each conclusion by trying to break it with one legal picture.

1Picture this

Two college rules: “All first-years are students.” and “All students have a library card.” Picture first-years inside students, and students inside card-holders. So all first-years have a library card, and (since first-years exist) some card-holders are first-years.

first-yearsstudents, then card-holders around

Nested circles make the chain obvious. Other pairs of statements are less kind. Today we learn which ones give an answer.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Two statements share a middle group, the one named in both (students above).
Draw the first statement. Then add the second without breaking the first.
Ask of each conclusion: is it true in my picture?
Then try to move the circles into a different legal picture (a Some can mean one item or many; circles can touch more or less). Does the conclusion survive?
If ANY legal picture breaks the conclusion, it does not follow. That is the whole test.
Quick screens (checked for every arrangement of the middle group): two Some/Some-not statements give nothing about the outer groups, and two negative statements (No or Some-not) give nothing.
Two more checks: one negative statement means the conclusion must be negative. One Some statement means the conclusion must be a Some or Some-not.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Some cats are dogs. All dogs are rats. Does “Some cats are rats” follow?

Step 1. Draw cats and dogs overlapping. Mark the overlap: the cat-dogs.
Step 2. Dogs sit fully inside rats, so the cat-dogs are inside rats.
Step 3. So at least one cat is a rat.
Step 4. Does “All cats are rats” follow? No: cats outside dogs may sit outside rats.
Answer: Some cats are rats follows.

No pens are books. All books are cups. What follows?

Step 1. Books are inside cups, and pens stay away from books.
Step 2. The books are cups that are not pens. Books exist.
Step 3. So Some cups are not pens follows.
Step 4. Does “No pens are cups” follow? No: pens may sit in cups outside the books. Answer: only the Some-not.

5The trap

Statements: Some tables are chairs. Some chairs are beds. Conclusion: Some tables are beds.
Wrong move: Some plus Some gives Some. It sounds like a chain.
Draw tables and beds far apart. Tables touch chairs in one spot, beds touch chairs in a different spot.
Both statements are true and the conclusion is false. So it does not follow.

Statements: All cats are dogs. Some dogs are rats. Conclusion: Some cats are rats.
Wrong move: it looks like “Some then All”. But the order is All then Some.
Draw cats inside dogs, and the rat-dogs in a different part of dogs.
Right: nothing follows about cats and rats. (Some rats are dogs does follow, because it is the premise swapped.)

6Speed method: break it in 10 seconds

Break-it routine. (1) Screens: two Some-type or two negative statements? Cross out any conclusion linking the outer groups. (2) Line up the chain with the shared group in the middle. (3) Look the pair up in the table: that is the full list. (4) Anything else, try to draw one picture that breaks it. One picture is enough to kill a conclusion.

Lesson 3 · Possibility, either-or and “only a few”

In one line: Definite needs every picture, Possibility needs one, and Either-or covers two conclusions where one must be true.

1Picture this

Rahul has a ticket for a match on either Saturday or Sunday. Is it Saturday? Not certain. Is it Sunday? Not certain. Is it one of the two? Certain. That is either-or: neither alone is sure, but one of them must be true.

Saturday?Sunday?

Compare: “Could it be Saturday?” is a possibility. One imaginable picture is enough.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Three verdicts for a conclusion: Definite (true in every picture), Possible only (true in some pictures), Never (true in none).
A conclusion that starts with “Possibility that …” follows if the verdict is Definite or Possible only. It fails only if it is Never.
Fast test for possibility: it fails only when its opposite definitely follows (All vs Some-not, No vs Some).
Either-or: two conclusions about the same two groups. Neither is definite, but in every picture at least one is true.
Pairs that work: Some A are B / No A is B. All A are B / Some A are not B. Some A are B / Some A are not B.
Pair that does NOT work: All A are B / No A is B. Both can be false (some are, some are not).
Only a few A are B (our own convention, not a textbook term): some A are B AND some A are not B. It gives Some A are B, Some B are A, Some A are not B. It does not give Some B are not A.
Order of checking: test each conclusion for Definite. If neither, test either-or. If still no, answer Neither.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Possibility fails ⇔ the opposite (All↔Some-not, No↔Some) definitely follows.
Either-or: no conclusion definite alone, and one of the two is true in every picture. Works for the pairs Some/No, All/Some-not, Some/Some-not.
Only a few A are B = Some A are B + Some A are not B.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Some pens are books. All books are cups. I. All pens are cups. II. Some pens are not cups.

Step 1. Some pens are books, and books are inside cups. So some pens are cups (certain).
Step 2. But the other pens may or may not be cups. So I is not certain, and II is not certain.
Step 3. Each pen is a cup or not. One of the two sentences is true in every picture.
Step 4. Answer: Either I or II follows.

Only a few pens are books. All books are cups. I. Some pens are cups. II. Some pens are not books.

Step 1. Only a few means some pens are books AND some pens are not books.
Step 2. Pen-books are inside cups, so some pens are cups: I follows.
Step 3. II is part of the meaning of Only a few: it follows.
Step 4. Answer: Both follow.

5The trap

Statements: Some pens are cups. Some cups are bags. I. All pens are bags. II. No pens are bags.
Wrong move: they look like opposites, so pick Either I or II.
Draw: some pens in bags and some pens not in bags. Both conclusions are false in that picture.
Right: All and No can both be false (they are called contraries: never both true, but both can be false). Not either-or. Answer: Neither follows.

Statements: No pens are books. All books are cups. Conclusion: Possibility that some pens are books.
Wrong move: a possibility is easy to grant.
But the statement No pens are books is the exact opposite, so Some pens are books can never be true here.
Right: the possibility fails.

6Speed method: the decision ladder

Ladder. (1) Is it a Possibility conclusion? Only check that the opposite does not definitely follow. (2) Otherwise test I and II for Definite. (3) Both Definite: Both. One Definite: Only that one. (4) Neither Definite and the pair is Some/No, All/Some-not or Some/Some-not about the same two groups: Either-or (the tester confirms). (5) Else Neither.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • Options usually include “Either I or II follows”. Check it only after both fail Definite.
  • “Only a few” statements, sometimes chained with an All statement.
How wrong options are built. Common plants: calling contraries (All/No) an either-or pair, treating a Possibility as Definite, and reading Only a few as Some B are not A.
