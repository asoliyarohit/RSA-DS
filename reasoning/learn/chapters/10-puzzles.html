Puzzles (floor, box, day, month, comparison)

In one line: every puzzle here is the same game: put names into slots, one each, and let each clue cross out options. Learn the slot arithmetic once and three lessons follow from it.

Lesson 1 · Floor and box puzzles (slot order)

In one line: a floor or box puzzle is five names to be placed into five numbered slots, and every clue crosses out some slots.

1Picture this

Your cousin's building in Pune has 5 floors and one family per floor. Someone says: "The Sharmas live above the Iyers" and "the Khans are on the top floor". You do not need to see the building. You only need five empty slots and a pencil.

5 top4321 bottom

Floors are counted from the bottom, so floor 1 is the lowest. A stack of boxes works the same way: position 1 is the bottom box.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A puzzle gives a few names and the same number of slots (floors, or places in a stack). Convention for this chapter: every name gets exactly one slot, and every slot gets exactly one name.
Number the slots from the bottom. Draw them as a column from top to bottom: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1.
Clue words are just slot arithmetic. "On the top floor" means slot 5. "Immediately above B" means B's number plus 1. "Above B" means any higher number.
"Between B and C" means strictly in the middle: a number between theirs, and B and C may come in either order. "Neighbouring" (or "next to") means the numbers differ by 1, on either side.
Step A: write in the fixed clues first ("on the top floor", "on floor 2"). They are your anchors.
Step B: treat "X immediately above Y" as one glued two-block. Find where a two-block still fits in the empty slots.
Step C: whenever a name has only one slot left, write it in. Repeat until all are placed.
Step D: re-read every clue against your finished picture. One broken clue means redo.

3The rules (now that you get it)

n names in n slots can be arranged in n! ways (5! = 120). Every clue deletes some of those 120.
A immediately above B: slot(A) = slot(B) + 1. A above B: slot(A) > slot(B).
A between B and C: slot(A) is strictly between slot(B) and slot(C). Exactly k between A and B: the slot numbers differ by k + 1.
Neighbouring: slots differ by 1. Slots strictly between p and q (p < q) = q − p − 1.

The n! count: Wikipedia "Permutation". Clue-deduction style, and "immediately to the right" versus "next to": Wikipedia "Logic puzzle" and "Zebra Puzzle". The slot arithmetic and the one-name-per-slot convention: derived, Python-checked (all 120 arrangements enumerated).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Five people (Asha, Bala, Charu, Dev, Esha) live on floors 1 to 5, one per floor. Dev lives on the top floor. Charu lives immediately above Bala. Esha lives on floor 2. Charu lives above Asha. Who lives on floor 3?

Step 1. Anchors: Dev = floor 5. Esha = floor 2.
Step 2. Free floors: 1, 3, 4. Free names: Asha, Bala, Charu.
Step 3. Charu is immediately above Bala, so they need two consecutive free floors. Among 1, 3, 4 the only such pair is 3 and 4. So Bala = 3, Charu = 4.
Step 4. Asha gets the last floor, 1. Check: Charu (4) above Asha (1). Yes.
Step 5. Answer: Bala (floor 3). Picture: 5 Dev, 4 Charu, 3 Bala, 2 Esha, 1 Asha.

Five boxes (Red, Blue, Green, Yellow, Pink) are stacked, position 1 at the bottom. Pink is at the top. Red is directly above Blue. Exactly one box is between Green and Pink. Yellow is somewhere above Blue. Which box is at the bottom?

Step 1. Anchor: Pink = position 5.
Step 2. Exactly one box between Green and Pink: positions differ by 2, so Green = 3.
Step 3. Free positions 1, 2, 4 for Red, Blue, Yellow. Red directly above Blue needs consecutive positions: only 1 and 2. So Blue = 1, Red = 2.
Step 4. Yellow takes position 4. Check: Yellow (4) above Blue (1). Yes.
Step 5. Answer: Blue at the bottom. Stack from the top: Pink, Yellow, Green, Red, Blue.

5The trap

Clue: "Asha lives above Bala" in a 5-floor building.
Wrong move: read it as "directly above" and put Asha exactly one floor up.
Python count: of all 120 arrangements, 60 fit "above" but only 24 fit "immediately above". The wrong reading throws away 36 valid arrangements.
Second trap, counting between. Floors 1 and 5: how many floors are between? Not 4, not 5. Floors 2, 3, 4 = 3 (that is 5 − 1 − 1).
Third trap, direction. "A is immediately above B" gives A the BIGGER number. Say it as an equation: A = B + 1.

6Speed method: biggest clue first, then test options

Order of attack. (1) Clues that name an exact slot, or top or bottom. (2) Glued pairs and "exactly k between". (3) "Between" and "above". (4) "Not" clues last, to trim what is left.
Option test. If the question gives options, put an option in and scan the clues. One broken clue kills it. This can be quicker than solving the whole puzzle.
Partial is fine. Ask whether the question needs the full order. Sometimes it does not (see quiz Q5).

7Lab: clue switchboard (floors)

Names: Asha, Bala, Charu, Dev, Esha on floors 1 to 5. Tick clues one at a time. A tick means that floor is still possible for that name.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper. Real sets may use more people than our five; the method is the same.

  • "Six friends live on six floors of a building. P lives above Q but not on the top floor. R lives on an even floor. ... Who lives on the third floor?"
  • "Five boxes are stacked one over another. Box M is directly above N. Exactly two boxes are between P and Q. ... Which box is at the top?"
  • Question shapes: who is on slot k, which slot is X on, how many are between X and Y, who is immediately above X, which statement is true.

Word clues: "immediately", "directly", "just" mean +1. "Above", "higher", "over" mean bigger, any gap. "Between" is strict. "Not necessarily" adds no information.

How wrong options are built. In our quiz they are the neighbours of the right answer (one slot off, or the same count counted wrongly). After solving, re-read the question: above or below, who or which floor.

Lesson 2 · Day and month puzzles (timelines)

In one line: a day or month puzzle is the same slot game on a calendar: Monday (or January) is slot 1, and "before" means a smaller number.

1Picture this

A coaching centre in Jaipur books five guest teachers, one for each day from Monday to Friday. The notice board says: "Mr Lalit comes the day before Ms Kavya." You can already feel the week as five boxes in a row.

Mon 1Tue 2Wed 3Thu 4Fri 5

Monday is slot 1. "Before" means a smaller number and "after" a bigger one. Months work the same way.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Conventions for this chapter: five working days, Monday to Friday (slots 1 to 5), one person per day. Months work the same: January to May as slots 1 to 5, one birthday per month.
The week does not wrap round here. No day comes before Monday and none comes after Friday. A real puzzle states its own days, so read the stem first.
Write the numbers under the day names: Mon 1, Tue 2, Wed 3, Thu 4, Fri 5. Now every clue is slot arithmetic you already know.
"Before" or "earlier than" means a smaller number. "After" or "later than" means a bigger number. "The day immediately after" means +1. "The day before" means −1.
"Exactly one day between A and B" means the numbers differ by 2. "Consecutive days" means they differ by 1.
"Not on Tuesday" crosses out slot 2 for that person. When a person has one uncrossed slot, place them.
Anchor first: use the fixed day or the "first day" or "last day" clue, then build outward.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Before: slot(A) < slot(B). After: slot(A) > slot(B). Immediately after: slot(A) = slot(B) + 1.
Exactly k days (or months) between A and B: slots differ by k + 1.
Days between X and Y = |slot X − slot Y| − 1.
"A visits before B" says nothing about distance. Only words like "immediately", "next day" and "consecutive" mean 1 apart.

Wikipedia "Constraint satisfaction problem": a constraint restricts which placements are allowed, and a solution satisfies all constraints at once. The slot translations of before, after and between: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Five trainers (Kavya, Lalit, Mohan, Nisha, Ojas) each visit on a different day, Monday to Friday. Kavya visits the day immediately after Lalit. Kavya visits on Friday. Mohan visits between Kavya's and Ojas's visits. Exactly one day falls between Mohan's and Ojas's visits. Who visits on Tuesday?

Step 1. Anchor: Kavya = Friday (5).
Step 2. Lalit is the day before Kavya: Thursday (4).
Step 3. Free days: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday (1, 2, 3) for Mohan, Nisha, Ojas.
Step 4. Mohan is between Kavya (Friday, the last day) and Ojas. So Ojas must be earlier than Mohan.
Step 5. Exactly one day between them: Mohan = Ojas + 2. Among 1, 2, 3 only Ojas = 1, Mohan = 3 works.
Step 6. Nisha gets Tuesday. Answer: Nisha. Week: Ojas, Nisha, Mohan, Lalit, Kavya.

Five friends (Kavya, Lalit, Mohan, Nisha, Ojas) were born in different months from January to May. Lalit was born in March. Mohan was born in a later month than Lalit. Exactly one month falls between Lalit's and Nisha's birth months. Kavya's birth month is between Lalit's and Ojas's. Who was born in February?

Step 1. Anchor: Lalit = March (3).
Step 2. Exactly one month between Lalit and Nisha: Nisha is January (1) or May (5).
Step 3. Mohan is later than March: April or May.
Step 4. Try Nisha = January. Then Kavya, Mohan, Ojas share Feb, April, May. Kavya between Lalit and Ojas fails in every case that also keeps Mohan in April or May (checked by enumeration). So Nisha is not January.
Step 5. So Nisha = May, and Mohan = April.
Step 6. Kavya and Ojas share January and February. Kavya must be between Lalit (March) and Ojas, so Kavya = February, Ojas = January.
Step 7. Answer: Kavya. Order: Ojas, Kavya, Lalit, Mohan, Nisha.

5The trap

Clue: "Mohan visits between Kavya's and Ojas's visits".
Wrong move: assume Kavya comes first because her name is first.
Python count: 40 of the 120 arrangements fit "between". In 20 of them Kavya comes first, in the other 20 Ojas comes first. Assuming one order drops half.
Right: try both directions. Another clue (like "Kavya on Friday") usually kills one of them.
Second trap, counting days. Days between Monday and Friday: Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday = 3, not 4.

6Speed method: write numbers under the days

Routine. Draw Mon 1 to Fri 5 once, then run a pencil down it. Fixed day first. Then "immediately after" pairs. Then "exactly k between". Then "before" and "after". "Not on" clues last.
Edge check. The first and last days are special: nothing can come before Monday or after Friday. A clue that needs a day beyond the edge rules that placement out at once.

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Six friends were born in six different months. P was born two months before Q. R was born after S but before P. ... Who was born in the last month?"
  • "A seminar runs from Monday to Saturday, one speaker a day. X speaks the day after Y. Exactly two days are between Z and W. ... Who speaks on Thursday?"
  • Question shapes: who is on day k, on which day is X, how many days between, who comes just after X.

Word clues: "earlier than", "prior to", "before" are smaller numbers. "Later than", "following", "after" are bigger. "Just before" and "the previous day" are −1. "Next day" is +1.

Lesson 3 · Table puzzles and comparison puzzles

In one line: when each person has two or more facts (colour, drink), draw a small table and fill what you know; when the clues only compare (taller, older), build a chain and ask what is fixed.

1Picture this

Four friends order at a Kolkata cafe. Everyone likes a different colour and drinks a different drink. You hear: "The blue-lover drinks coffee" and "Ravi likes red". There is no order, no floors: just a table of who has what.

PersonColourDrink
RaviRed?
Sita??
Tarun??
Uma??

Clues fill the question marks one at a time.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Convention: each person has exactly one value from each category (colour, drink), and no two people share a value.
Draw a table. Rows are people, columns are categories. Start empty.
A direct clue ("Ravi likes red") goes straight into its cell. It is settled for good.
A "not" clue ("Tarun does not like blue") becomes a small X in that cell. X marks are knowledge too, but they do not place anyone yet.
A link clue ("the blue-lover drinks coffee") says blue and coffee belong to the SAME person. Bracket them together. When one is placed, so is the other.
Elimination: if a value is crossed out for every person but one, it belongs to that one.
Comparison clues ("A is taller than B") are different. They give an order, not a table. Chain them: Esha > Dev > Charu > Bala.
Chains join at a shared name. "Asha > Bala" and "Charu > Bala" both hang from Bala, but say nothing about Asha versus Charu.
Ask the question first. "Who is shortest?" only needs one name that has everyone above it, not the full order.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Table rule: each value belongs to exactly one person per category. A link puts two values in one row. A "not" crosses one cell.
Chain rule: A > B and B > C give A > C. The ">" carries through a chain.
A person is definitely the shortest only if the clues put every other person above them, directly or through a chain. A person is definitely the tallest by the mirror rule.
If both orders of two names can happen in some valid arrangement, the clues do not decide which is taller: "cannot tell".

Table and one-value-each setup: Wikipedia "Logic puzzle" (grid and table puzzles) and "Zebra Puzzle". Chain rule and the "cannot tell" test: derived, Python-checked over all arrangements.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Ravi, Sita, Tarun, Uma each like a different colour (Red, Blue, Green, Yellow) and drink a different drink (Tea, Coffee, Milk, Juice). Ravi likes red. Uma drinks juice. The person who likes green drinks juice. The person who likes blue drinks coffee. Tarun does not like blue. Tarun does not drink tea. What does Ravi drink?

Step 1. Write in: Ravi = Red. Uma = Juice.
Step 2. Green and Juice are one person, and Uma drinks juice. So Uma likes Green.
Step 3. Blue is not Ravi (red), not Uma (green), not Tarun (clue). So Sita likes Blue, and drinks Coffee.
Step 4. Yellow is left for Tarun.
Step 5. Tea and Milk are left for Ravi and Tarun. Tarun does not drink tea, so Tarun = Milk and Ravi = Tea.
Step 6. Answer: Tea. Table: Ravi Red Tea; Sita Blue Coffee; Tarun Yellow Milk; Uma Green Juice.

Asha is taller than Bala. Charu is taller than Bala. Dev is taller than Charu. Esha is taller than Dev. Who is the shortest?

Step 1. Chain the last three: Esha > Dev > Charu > Bala.
Step 2. Asha > Bala: Asha sits somewhere above Bala. Her place in the chain is open.
Step 3. Bala has everyone above. Answer: Bala.
Step 4. Python check: 4 arrangements fit the clues. Bala is shortest in all 4. Asha is 2nd, 3rd, 4th or 5th shortest, so her place is not fixed, and the answer does not need it.

5The trap

Same clues as above: Asha > Bala, Charu > Bala, Dev > Charu, Esha > Dev.
Wrong move: "Asha is only named above Bala, so she must be second shortest."
Python: in the 4 valid arrangements Asha is 2nd, 3rd, 4th or 5th shortest. Also, Asha is taller than Charu in some and shorter in others. The clues do not say.
Right: never fill a gap with a guess. Ask "is there a valid arrangement the other way?" If yes, you cannot tell.
Table trap: "Tarun does not like blue" is not a placement. Blue could still be any of three people until other clues cross them out.

6Speed method: fill, cross, link; or draw one chain

Table puzzles. Direct clues first. Then link clues that touch a placed value. Then eliminate: a value crossed out for all but one person goes to that person.
Comparison puzzles. Draw one line, tallest on the left. Join clues at shared names. The tallest is the name with nobody above; the shortest is the name with nobody below. Check that the clues really connect everyone before you call it.

7Lab: clue switchboard (heights)

Rank 1 = shortest, rank 5 = tallest. Tick the four comparison clues one by one. Notice how many places Asha keeps.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Four friends P, Q, R, S each like a different sport and live in a different city. P lives in Delhi. The person who likes chess lives in Pune. ... Who likes cricket?"
  • "A is older than B but younger than C. D is older than C. Who is the youngest?"
  • Question shapes: who has value X, what does person P have, who is the tallest or shortest, how many are older than X, "cannot be determined".

If a question can end in "cannot be determined", test both directions before you pick it. Data-sufficiency questions train exactly this habit.

Memorise this
1. Table: direct clue fills, "not" crosses, link joins two values in one row.
2. Last value left for a person goes to that person.
3. A > B > C means A > C.
4. Do not guess what the clues leave open: test the other order.
5. Shortest = everyone else is above them (directly or by chain).
