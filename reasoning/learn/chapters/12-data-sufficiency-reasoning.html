Data Sufficiency (reasoning style)

In one line: you are not asked for the answer, only whether the statements pin down exactly ONE answer. Three lessons: ordering, blood relations, directions. Every verdict was checked by trying all possibilities.

The five options used in every question here: I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough. The fetched source page uses only four options; the fifth, “Either alone” (each statement by itself is enough), is our own extension so every outcome has a choice. The exact option wording and order used in PFRDA papers is unverified: read the options on the real screen every time.

Lesson 1 · Ordering: is the order pinned down?

In one line: a data sufficiency question does not ask “what is the answer?”. It asks “do the statements pin down ONE answer?”. Here the answers are about who is taller, rank, tallest or shortest.

1Picture this

At the chai stall your friend says: “Ravi stands ahead of Sunil in the queue.” You ask: “So is Ravi first?” You cannot say; someone else may be ahead of both. Then she adds: “Ravi is ahead of everyone else too.” Now you can say yes. You did not need the whole queue order. You only needed enough to be certain.

Asha
Bala
Chitra

A chain Asha > Bala > Chitra settles Asha vs Chitra without ever comparing them directly.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A data sufficiency question gives a question and two statements, I and II. You decide whether the statements give enough information to answer. You do not need to find a full solution.
Think of every possible arrangement of the people that obeys the statement. If all of those arrangements give the SAME answer, the statement is sufficient. If two of them give different answers, it is not sufficient.
Conventions used in this chapter: everyone has a different height; only the people named exist; “taller” is the only thing compared; you use nothing outside the statements. (Each convention stops guessing.)
A definite “No” is a full answer. If the statements prove Asha is NOT taller than Chitra, that is sufficient. “Sufficient” means certain, not “yes”.
Three tests, in this order: (1) I alone, pretending II does not exist; (2) II alone, pretending I does not exist; (3) both together.
To prove “not sufficient”, build two different line-ups that both obey the statement but disagree. If you can build them, stop: not sufficient.
Then pick the option that matches your three results (table in the next card).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Sufficient: every arrangement obeying the statement(s) gives one answer. Insufficient: two obeying arrangements disagree.
If I alone is sufficient, I and II together are sufficient too (extra true information cannot hurt).
Chain rule. Write each “A taller than B” as an arrow A → B. “X taller than Y?” is settled exactly when a chain of arrows leads from X to Y or from Y to X. “Who is tallest?” is settled exactly when one person has a chain to EVERY other person.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

Is Dev taller than Chitra? (people: Asha, Bala, Chitra, Dev)
I. Dev is taller than Bala.
II. Bala is taller than Chitra.

Test I alone: Dev > Bala. Chitra is not mentioned. Chitra could be above Dev or below Bala. Two line-ups disagree, so I is not sufficient.
Test II alone: Bala > Chitra. Dev is not mentioned. Dev could be above or below Chitra. Not sufficient.
Together: Dev > Bala and Bala > Chitra give the chain Dev → Bala → Chitra. Dev is taller than Chitra, in every line-up.
Verdict (checked by trying all 24 line-ups): Both needed. Answers: I alone {No / Yes}, II alone {No / Yes}, together {Yes}.

What is Chitra's rank from the top among the five? (people: Asha, Bala, Chitra, Dev, Esha)
I. Asha is taller than Chitra; Bala is taller than Chitra.
II. Chitra is taller than Dev; Chitra is taller than Esha.

Rank 1 means tallest. Count how many people are above Chitra, then add 1.
I alone: Asha and Bala are above Chitra. Dev and Esha are unknown, so Chitra is rank 3, 4 or 5.
II alone: Chitra is above Dev and Esha, so at most 2 people (Asha, Bala) can be above her: rank 1, 2 or 3.
Together: exactly Asha and Bala above (2 people), so Chitra is rank 3. Verdict (all 120 line-ups tried): Both needed.

5The trap

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

Is Asha taller than Chitra? (people: Asha, Bala, Chitra)
I. Chitra is taller than Bala.
II. Bala is taller than Asha.

Together: Chitra > Bala > Asha. So Asha is NOT taller than Chitra.
Wrong thought: “the answer is No, so the statements did not help. Not enough.”
Why that is wrong: the question has a definite answer, No, in every line-up that fits. Pinned down is pinned down.
Right: Neither statement alone settles it (each leaves both line-ups open), together they do: Both needed. Never confuse “No” with “cannot say”.

6Speed method: draw arrows, look for a chain

The arrow method (10 seconds). Draw each statement as an arrow from taller to shorter. Question about two people: is there a path of arrows between them? Question about “tallest”: does one person have a path to everybody? Do this for I, then II, then both drawn together. No path means not settled.

Speed drill (8 items)

Say your verdict aloud before tapping Show. The brackets show which answers each statement leaves possible.

7Lab: ordering tester

Four people A, B, C, D. Type statements as A>B (A taller than B), separated by commas. For the question type A>B to ask “is A taller than B?”, or type tallest or shortest.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • “Is P taller than Q? I. P is taller than R. II. R is taller than Q.”
  • “Who is the tallest among five people? I. … II. …”
  • “What is the rank of X from the top? I. Four people are taller than X. II. …”
  • Options in our five-option form: I alone / II alone / both needed / either alone / not enough.

Lesson 2 · Blood relations: link plus gender

In one line: to answer “is X the uncle/daughter/grandmother of Y?” you need two things, the LINK between the two people and the GENDER the relation word demands. Statements may give one, the other, or both.

1Picture this

In the family WhatsApp group someone writes: “Mohan is Priya’s parent.” Is Mohan her father? You cannot tell: “parent” hides gender. Someone adds “Mohan is a man.” Now it is settled. The word “parent” gave the link; the extra line gave the gender.

link: parent ofgender: male

Link + gender = “father”. One without the other is not enough.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Here people are letters A, B, C. A letter says nothing about gender. Gender is only what a statement says.
Some words HIDE gender: parent, child, sibling. Some GIVE it: father, mother, son, daughter, brother, sister.
Convention: brother and sister mean full siblings, sharing the same father AND the same mother. (A half-sibling shares only one parent; Wikipedia, Sibling.)
Two facts can be separate. “C is the daughter of A” needs: A is a parent of C, AND C is female.
A sibling link copies parents. If A is the father of B and C is B’s sister, then A is C’s father too: same parents.
The reverse is NOT true. “A is a parent of B” and “A is a parent of C” do not make B and C full siblings; they could be half-siblings.
To prove “not sufficient”, draw two different family trees that both obey the statements but give different answers. We checked every tree for three people (details below).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Link words: parent / child / sibling hide gender. Gender words: father, mother, son, daughter, brother, sister fix it.
Sibling = same two parents. A sibling’s parent is your parent.
Grandparent = parent of a parent. Uncle (this chapter) = a man who is a brother of one of your parents. Aunt = a woman who is a sister of one of your parents.
A definite “No” is sufficient. Example: “C is female” proves C is not “the son of A”.

Sibling definitions: Wikipedia “Sibling”. Grandparent, uncle, aunt and cousin as relatives of parents: Wikipedia “Kinship”. Rules and verdicts: derived, Python-checked on all 24,948 family models of three people (each person has a gender, a father and a mother who may be one of the three or an outsider; nobody is their own ancestor). No marriage or in-law relations are used.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

Is A the uncle of C?
I. A is the brother of B.
II. B is a parent of C.

Uncle needs: A is male AND A is a brother of one of C’s parents.
I alone: A is male and B’s brother. But C may not be B’s child. Link to C missing. Not sufficient.
II alone: B is a parent of C. Nothing about A. Not sufficient.
Together: A (male) is a full brother of B, and B is a parent of C, so A is a brother of C’s parent: uncle. Verdict (all 3-person family models): Both needed.

Is A the grandmother of C?
I. A is the mother of B.
II. B is the father of C.

Grandmother needs: A is a parent of a parent of C, AND A is female.
I alone: A is B’s mother (so female), but C is not linked. Not sufficient.
II alone: B is C’s father. A is not mentioned. Not sufficient.
Together: A → B → C is a chain of two parent steps, and A is a mother, so A is C’s grandmother. Verdict: Both needed.

5The trap

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

Is A the father of B?
I. A is a parent of B.
II. B is a child of A.

Statement I: A is a parent of B. Statement II: B is a child of A. These are the same fact said twice.
Wrong thought: “two statements agree, so I am sure. A is the father.”
Why wrong: “parent” does not say male. A could be B’s mother. Agreeing statements are still just one link.
Right: no gender anywhere, so Not enough. Look for the gender word before you say yes.

6Speed method: link? gender? route?

Three quick questions. (1) Do the statements connect the two people in the question (a route)? (2) Does the answer word need a gender, and does any statement give it? (3) Does the route pass through a sibling link (copy parents) or a parent link (go up one generation)? If any answer is no, not sufficient. Remember: a gender fact that contradicts the relation word can prove a definite “No”.

Say the verdict aloud, then Show.

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • “How is P related to Q? I. P is the sister of R. II. R is the father of Q.”
  • “Is M the grandfather of N? I. … II. …”
  • “What is the gender of T? I. T is a sibling of U. II. T is the mother of V.”
  • Watch for words that hide gender (parent, child, sibling, cousin) versus words that give it.

Lesson 3 · Directions: arrows on a map

In one line: each statement is an arrow on a map (a direction, sometimes a distance). You can place a point only if the arrows pin it down.

1Picture this

An auto driver says: “Go east to the temple.” You ask: “How far?” He shrugs. You know the direction but not the distance. Another person says: “The temple is 4 km from the school, due east of it.” Together you could place the temple exactly. Direction-only and distance-only are both partial.

4 km east3 km north

4 east and 3 north: the straight-line gap is 5 km (3-4-5 triangle).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Draw a map. North is up, east is right. Put the first person at the middle point, called the origin. Every other person has an east-west position and a north-south position.
Conventions: flat map, straight-line moves, distances in km. “Q is east of P” means due east: exactly on the east line from P, distance unknown. “Q is 4 km east of P” gives the distance too.
“North-east of” means strictly north AND strictly east (not on a straight line). We use it for answers, not for statements.
Each statement is an arrow. To relate R to P, chain arrows P → Q → R. Add the east-west pieces together and, separately, the north-south pieces.
To state the DIRECTION of R from P you need only the sign of each total: east or west or zero, north or south or zero. To state the DISTANCE you need both totals as numbers.
Distance formula (Pythagoras): gap = √(east-west total² + north-south total²). Example 3 and 4 give 5.
The danger: an unknown-length piece. East 3 then west by an unknown amount could end east, at the start, or west. Same-way pieces never cancel; opposite-way pieces can.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Sum each axis separately: east-west pieces together, north-south pieces together.
Pieces on one axis all pointing the same way: the sign is fixed even if lengths are unknown.
Exactly ONE unknown-length piece on an axis: add up the known pieces. If their total points the same way as the unknown piece (or is zero), the direction is fixed. If it points the opposite way, the direction can flip, so not sufficient.
Two or more unknown-length pieces on an axis: do not trust a shortcut; test with two small examples.
Distance needs every piece known (or fixed by a statement that gives the whole chain).

Cardinal and intercardinal directions: Wikipedia “Cardinal direction”. Coordinates and distance formula: Wikipedia “Cartesian coordinate system”. Sum and one-unknown-piece rules: derived, Python-checked (518 cases: 0 to 3 known pieces of length 2, 3 or 5 in either direction plus one unknown piece of length 1 to 39 on the same axis; 0 mismatches). Verdicts below: every point of an integer grid from −12 to 12 km tried; “sufficient” answers also follow from the sign rules, and “not sufficient” answers come with a concrete pair of maps.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

How far is R from P?
I. Q is 3 km north of P.
II. R is 4 km east of Q.

I alone: Q is 3 km north of P. R is not placed. Not sufficient.
II alone: R is 4 km east of Q, but Q is not placed. Not sufficient.
Together: P → Q is 3 north, Q → R is 4 east. East-west total 4, north-south total 3. Distance = √(4² + 3²) = √25 = 5 km.
Verdict (all grid maps tried): Both needed. Together the only possible answer is {5 km}.

In which direction is R from P?
I. Q is 6 km north of P.
II. R is 2 km west of Q.

I alone: Q is north of P, R unplaced: R could be anywhere. Not sufficient.
II alone: R is 2 km west of Q, Q unplaced. Not sufficient.
Together: north-south total 6 north, east-west total 2 west. North and west: north-west. Verdict: Both needed, answer {north-west}.

5The trap

Options (our five-option set): I alone / II alone / Either alone / Both needed / Not enough.

Is R to the east of P?
I. Q is east of P.
II. R is west of Q.

Q is east of P (unknown distance). R is west of Q (unknown distance).
Wrong thought: “east, then west: R is back towards P, so R is west of P.” Or the opposite, “R is still east”.
Test: Q 5 km east, R 2 km west of Q puts R 3 km east of P. Q 2 km east, R 5 km west of Q puts R 3 km west of P. Both fit.
Right: opposite directions with unknown lengths can flip the answer. Not enough, even with both statements.

6Speed method: arrows head to tail, mark the question marks

The 10-second map. Draw each statement as an arrow head to tail. Write the length on it, or a ? if unknown. On each axis, circle any ? arrow that points against another arrow. A circle means danger: the direction can flip.

Speed drill (8 items)

Say the verdict aloud, then Show. In a yes/no question, “Is R north of P?” gets the same verdict whether you read it as due north or anywhere to the north (we checked both readings for every item here).
