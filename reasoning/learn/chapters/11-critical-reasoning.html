Critical Reasoning

In one line: read a short statement, then decide what it quietly needs, what must follow, what weakens or strengthens it, and what sensible step to take. Not in the official syllabus (none is printed); third-party lists only.

Lesson 1 · Assumptions and conclusions

In one line: an assumption is the unspoken idea a statement needs; a conclusion follows only if it MUST be true.

1Picture this

Your mother says: “Take an umbrella, it will rain.” She is quietly counting on two things you never heard: that you will go out, and that an umbrella keeps you dry. Those unspoken supports are assumptions.

Critical reasoning questions ask you to spot the hidden support, or to say what must be true.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A statement is a short passage. It usually gives a reason for a plan or a claim. In argument language, the reasons are premises and what they aim to prove is the conclusion; inference is the step from one to the other.
An assumption is an unstated idea that must be true for the statement to make sense (the same thing as a tacit or implicit assumption).
Negation test. Imagine the assumption is false. If the statement now collapses or makes no sense, the assumption is implicit. If the statement still works, it is not.
Example: “To cut bills, the society replaced all corridor bulbs with LEDs.” Suppose LEDs do NOT use less power. The plan is pointless. So “LEDs use less power” is implicit.
Now suppose “residents will stop using corridor lights” is true. Then no bulb matters at all, so this idea works against the plan. Not implicit.
Habit (our advice, not a theorem): an assumption with big words like all, every, always, never, will stop is usually too strong. The statement only needs a little: some, at least partly.
A different question type: statement and conclusion. Take everything in the statement as true. A conclusion follows only if it must be true from the statement alone, with no outside knowledge.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Implicit assumption: true in EVERY situation where the statement’s plan or claim works. (Negation test.)
Conclusion follows: true in EVERY situation where the statement is true. If it could be false, it does not follow.
Use only the statement. Outside knowledge and new facts do not count.
Conventions in this chapter: “Some” means at least one. Answer set: Only I / Only II / Both / Neither. (Some papers, e.g. IndiaBIX, add a fifth option “Either I or II”. Items in this chapter never pair two exact opposites, so that option never arises here.)

Assumption, premise, conclusion, inference definitions: sources below. The “true in every working situation” test is derived, Python-checked by enumerating all situations of the items in this lesson.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Statement: All trains on the Konkan route were late today. Some trains on the Konkan route are express trains. I. Some express trains were late today. II. All express trains were late today.

Step 1. Draw it: a big circle “late” with the circle “Konkan trains” fully inside.
Step 2. “Some Konkan trains are express” puts at least one express train inside the Konkan circle, hence inside “late”.
Step 3. I: at least one express train is late. Must be true. Follows.
Step 4. II: other express trains outside the Konkan route could be on time. Not forced. Does not follow. Answer: Only I. (Python checked every set model on 3 trains.)

Statement: A free bus pass for women is announced to raise women’s job participation. I. For some women, bus fare is a barrier to taking a job. II. At least some women who want jobs can reach workplaces by bus.

Step 1. Negate I: no woman finds fare a barrier. Then the free pass changes nothing. Plan collapses, so I is implicit.
Step 2. Negate II: no such woman can reach a workplace by bus. A free pass is useless. Plan collapses, so II is implicit.
Step 3. Answer: Both are implicit.

5The trap

Statement: “Every employee who finished the audit got a bonus. Karan got a bonus.”
Wrong move: conclude “Karan finished the audit”. It feels natural because bonus and audit are linked.
Check: the statement never says audit is the ONLY way to a bonus. Karan could have earned it another way. Both “finished” and “did not finish” are possible.
Right: “Karan finished the audit” does not follow. Nor does “Karan earned it some other way”. All-A-are-B does not reverse (Python-checked).

6Speed method: the 3-second filter

Per option: (1) kill it if it brings a NEW fact the statement never touches; (2) kill it if it has an extreme word the statement does not need; (3) run the negation test on what is left.

Reverse trap: “All A are B” says nothing about B going back to A. “Only A are B” does the opposite: every B is an A.

Speed drill

Answer aloud first: Yes, No or Cannot say. All checked by Python on set models. x is one named person or thing.

Lesson 2 · Strengthen, weaken and cause-effect

In one line: ask what a new fact removes: a rival cause (strengthens), the cause itself (weakens), or nothing (irrelevant).

1Picture this

At a hostel, the boys who drink protein shakes also lift heavier weights. A friend says: “Shakes make you strong.” Maybe. Or strong boys buy shakes. Or serious gym-goers do both. Strengthen and weaken questions ask: does a new fact make the claim more or less believable?

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

An argument claims that X causes Y (the cause is X, the effect is Y), because X and Y go together.
“Go together” is a correlation. Correlation alone does not prove cause. Other explanations: reverse causation (Y causes X), a common cause (a hidden Z causes both), coincidence (pure chance).
“After this, therefore because of this” (post hoc) is the mistake of thinking B was caused by A just because B came after A.
In general, an argument from samples gets stronger with a larger, more representative sample, and a single clear contrary case can hurt a universal claim.
Strengthen = a fact that rules out rival explanations (for example, people were assigned to X by lottery and the gap stayed).
Weaken = a fact that rules out the cause itself, shows the gap vanishes when like is compared with like, or points to a hidden common cause.
Irrelevant = a fact that rules out nothing (timing, colour, popularity). It changes nothing.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Four rival structures for “X and Y go together”: X causes Y · Y causes X · hidden Z causes both · chance.
A fact strengthens “X causes Y” if it removes rivals other than X→Y; weakens if it removes X→Y itself, or points to a specific rival (for example, shows a hidden Z driving both); is neutral if it does neither.
Convention in this guide: we count the four structures equally likely at the start (a teaching model, not a law). Then strengthen = share of X→Y rises; weaken = it falls. Removing a structure sets its weight to 0; evidence pointing to a rival raises that rival’s weight, which also lowers X→Y’s share.

Correlation, reverse causation, common cause, coincidence, post hoc: Wikipedia pages in Sources. Model of effects: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Claim: Students who attend the morning yoga class score higher, so yoga improves scores. Which fact strengthens it most? Students were put in the yoga group by lottery and the group still scored higher.

Step 1. Four rivals: yoga→scores, scores→yoga (high scorers join), hidden Z (study habit) → both, chance. Start: each 1 in 4.
Step 2. Lottery means nobody chose yoga based on scores or habits. That kills “scores→yoga” and “hidden Z”.
Step 3. Left: yoga→scores and chance. X→Y share rises from 25% to 50%. Strengthens.
Step 4. Opposite case: “Yoga students already scored higher the year BEFORE joining.” This removes yoga→scores. Share falls to 0. Weakens. “The class is at 6 am” removes nothing: no change.

5The trap

Claim: “After the new coach joined, the team won 5 matches in a row, so the coach is the reason.”
Wrong move: accept it because the wins came AFTER the coach.
Check: “came after” rules out none of the rivals. The wins could come from the coach, from something else that changed at the same time (say, new stronger players), or from luck. Nothing is ruled out, so the claim is no more likely than before.
Right: spot “after this, therefore because of this” (post hoc).

6Speed method: classify the fact in 5 seconds

Ask one question of each option: what does it remove? Removes the cause, or points to a rival → weakens. Removes rivals → strengthens. Neither → neutral. Also check the direction asked: “strengthen” or “weaken”.

Words that usually mean “the gap vanishes”: “when drivers of the same age are compared”, “both rise in summer”, “was already higher before”.

Speed drill

Say Strengthens, Weakens or No effect, on the claim “X causes Y”. Checked in the model above.

Lesson 3 · If-then logic and courses of action

In one line: know what an if-then rule really allows, then pick steps that tackle the stated problem without going extreme.

1Picture this

Rule at your coaching centre: “Only students who clear the entry test can join the batch.” Your friend cleared the test. Is he in the batch? You cannot say. Clearing the test is necessary, not enough. This tiny idea decides many reasoning questions, and it also tells you what a sensible course of action looks like.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

“If P then Q”: P is sufficient for Q (P guarantees Q). Q is necessary for P (without Q, no P).
P true → Q must be true. ✔ (affirm the antecedent, i.e. the “if” part)
Q false → P must be false. ✔ (contrapositive)
Q true → P can be either. ✘ P false → Q can be either. ✘ These two are the classic traps.
Course of action questions: assume the statement is true; ask which suggested step logically follows for pursuing.
A sensible step is practical, aimed at the stated problem or its cause, and not extreme or unrelated. Our test (a convention, not a theorem): follows only if it targets the problem and is neither extreme nor off-topic.
Two suggested steps can both follow, or neither. Judge each alone.

3The rules (now that you get it)

If P then Q. Valid: P true ⇒ Q true. Q false ⇒ P false.
Not valid: Q true ⇒ P true; P false ⇒ Q false. (Both are Open.)
“Only P can Q” means: if Q then P.
Course of action: assume the statement is true; pick steps that deal with the stated problem directly; reject extreme, unrelated or impossible steps.

Necessary/sufficient: Wikipedia page below. Four if-then cases: derived, Python-checked by truth table. Course-of-action convention: IndiaBIX instructions; the extreme/off-topic test is our reading.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

If it rains, the match is cancelled. The match was not cancelled. What follows?

Step 1. P = rain, Q = cancelled. Given: if P then Q, and Q is false.
Step 2. If it had rained, the match would have been cancelled. It was not.
Step 3. So it did not rain. (Truth table: only world with P⇒Q and not Q has P false.)

Statement: Many residents of a colony have diarrhoea after the water supply turned muddy. I. The municipality should check and clean the supply pipeline immediately. II. The residents should be asked to leave the colony permanently.

Step 1. Problem: muddy water, illness. Cause: pipeline or supply.
Step 2. I targets the cause and is practical. Follows.
Step 3. II is extreme and not needed. Does not follow. Answer: Only I.

5The trap

Rule: “Only candidates who cleared Phase I can sit Phase II.” Ravi cleared Phase I.
Wrong move: conclude “Ravi sits Phase II”. Clearing felt like the ticket.
Check: clearing is necessary, not sufficient. The rule does not say clearing is enough.
Right: it cannot be determined. “Only” points backwards: sits ⇒ cleared.

6Speed method

Write the rule as P → Q in 3 seconds. Then test only the 4 cases: P true, Q false are the safe two; Q true, P false are the two traps.

For courses of action, cross out any step with “permanently”, “all”, “ban all”, “punish severely” unless the statement demands it (our habit, not a theorem).

Speed drill

Given “If P then Q”. Say True, False or Open. Checked by truth table.
