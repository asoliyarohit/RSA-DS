Inequalities (direct and coded)

In one line: letters stand for numbers, and you only ever ask "is this true in every possible case?" Three lessons: chains, either-or, coded symbols.

Lesson 1 · Direct chains: can I walk from P to R?

In one line: letters stand for numbers, and a chain like P > Q ≥ R lets you walk from the left end to the right end only while the arrows all point the same way.

1Picture this

Four friends in a Pune hostel compare heights. Ravi is taller than Sunil. Sunil is at least as tall as Tara. Is Ravi taller than Tara? Yes: you walked down two steps, and one step was a strict "taller".

Ravi
Sunil
Tara

Ravi › Sunil ≥ Tara. Tara could equal Sunil, but never beat Ravi.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Each letter is a hidden number (marks, heights, ages). We do not know the numbers, only how they compare.
Symbols: > means bigger than, < means smaller than, = means same, ≥ means bigger or equal ("at least"), ≤ means smaller or equal ("at most"). The small end of the symbol points at the smaller number.
A chain is a row like P > Q ≥ R. It is two facts glued together: P > Q and Q ≥ R.
Same direction: you can walk the whole chain. P > Q ≥ R means P is above R, so P > R.
One strict link makes the whole walk strict. P ≥ Q ≥ R only gives P ≥ R. P > Q ≥ R gives P > R.
An = link is invisible: P > Q = R gives P > R, as if Q were not there.
Direction change stops the walk. P < Q > R: Q is the biggest, but P and R can be in any order. No relation.
A conclusion definitely follows only if it is true for every possible set of numbers that fit the statements. One counter-example kills it.
Strong implies weak: P > R also means P ≥ R and P ≠ R. But P ≥ R does not give P > R.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Same direction chain: the ends are related. Direction change: the ends are NOT related (not definite).
Strict wins: if any link is > (or <), the ends are strict. If all links are ≥ (or all ≤), the ends are ≥ (or ≤). If all links are =, the ends are =.
= links are invisible: they take on the neighbour's relation.
Sign-combine table (walking left to right): > then > = >; > then ≥ = >; ≥ then ≥ = ≥; = then X = X.

Why it works: < and ≤ are transitive, and ≠ is not (Wikipedia: Transitive relation); the symbol meanings are on the Math Is Fun and Wikipedia inequality pages. The sign-combine table and the 'direction change gives no relation' rule: derived, Python-checked (all 25 symbol pairs, every possible ordering of 3 letters).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

P > Q ≥ R = S. Is P > S definitely true?

Step 1. Walk from P: P > Q (down). Q ≥ R (down). R = S (flat). All the same direction, so we can walk.
Step 2. The = is invisible, so ignore it: P > Q ≥ S.
Step 3. One strict link (P > Q) makes the walk strict. P > S. Yes, definitely true.
Check: P ≥ S is also true (strong implies weak), but S ≥ P is definitely false.

H ≥ J; J > K; L ≤ K. Is H > L definitely true?

Step 1. The statements are separate. Find the shared letters: J and K link them.
Step 2. Flip L ≤ K so it points the same way: K ≥ L.
Step 3. Line up: H ≥ J > K ≥ L. All point down.
Step 4. One strict link (J > K) so H > L. Yes, definitely true.

5The trap

The chain is P ≤ Q ≥ R. Wrong thought: "Q is big, so P ≥ R, or at least P ≤ R."
Try numbers. P = 0, Q = 2, R = 1: everything fits, and P is smaller than R.
Now P = 1, Q = 2, R = 0: everything fits, and P is bigger than R.
So neither P ≤ R nor P ≥ R is certain. Both arrows point towards Q (or both away): no walk. Mark it not definite.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Rewrite every statement so all arrows point the same way (flip the ‹ and ≤ ones).
  2. Stack them into one line using the shared letters.
  3. For each conclusion: are its two letters on one same-direction stretch? If not, cross it out.
  4. If yes: does it claim strict (›) but the stretch has only ≥? Cross out. Does it claim = but a link is not =? Cross out. Opposite direction? Cross out.

Drill: the relation of P to R after walking the chain.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Statements: P › Q ≥ R = S; T ‹ R. Conclusions: I. P › T II. Q ≥ S". Then five options: only I, only II, either, neither, both.
  • "Which of the following is definitely true?" with four or five single relations.
  • Words used: "definitely true", "follows", "is not true". Always the same test: true in every case.
How wrong options are built. They flip a direction, upgrade ≥ to ›, or join two arrows that point towards or away from the same letter.

Lesson 2 · Two conclusions: only I, only II, either-or, neither, both

In one line: test each conclusion alone for "always true"; if neither is, check whether together they cover every case (either-or).

1Picture this

Two cricketers, Asha and Bela, have unknown scores. Someone offers two claims: I, "Asha scored more than Bela"; II, "Asha scored at most what Bela scored". You do not know the scores. Is one of the two claims certainly true? Yes: either Asha is ahead, or she is not. One of the two must hold.

Asha
Bela

I: Asha › Bela. II: Asha ≤ Bela. Between them they cover every case.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Take conclusion I alone. Is it true in every case? If yes, I follows. Do the same for II. Use the chain walk from Lesson 1.
If I and II both follow: both. If only one does: only I or only II.
If neither follows, do not stop. Look at the letters: do I and II talk about the same two letters?
If yes, ask: could both be false at once? The three possible cases for A and B are A > B, A = B, A < B.
I: A > B covers one case. II: A ≤ B covers the other two. Together all three cases are covered, so one is always true: either I or II follows.
If some case makes both false (A = B makes both A > B and A < B false), the answer is neither.
Order of checks: definite first, either-or second, neither last. Either-or is only for when neither is definite on its own.

3The rules (now that you get it)

A conclusion follows only if it is true in every case.
Either-or: neither conclusion follows alone, both talk about the same two letters, and in every case at least one of them is true.
Complementary pairs (checked, with no statement linking the two letters): < with ≥, > with ≤, and ≤ with ≥.
NOT either-or: > with < (both fail when equal), > with =, > with ≥ (both fail when A < B).

Either-or as the 'cover every case' idea: derived, Python-checked (all 15 pairs of the five symbols over every ordering of two letters; exactly these three pairs qualify, and only when the statements do not link the two letters). The term 'either-or' comes from coaching pages: see UNVERIFIED.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

P ≥ Q; R > Q. I. P > R II. P ≤ R

Step 1. P ≥ Q and R > Q: both P and R sit above Q, so P and R are not linked.
Step 2. I alone: P > R can fail (P = R). II alone: P ≤ R can fail (P > R). Neither follows.
Step 3. Same letters P and R. I is > and II is ≤: a complementary pair.
Step 4. Either I or II follows.

A > B = C ≥ D. I. A > D II. B ≥ D

Step 1. Walk from A: A > B, B = C, C ≥ D. All the same direction. A > D: I follows.
Step 2. B = C ≥ D, so B ≥ D: II follows.
Step 3. Both follow. Do not use either-or here: it is only for when neither follows alone.

5The trap

Statements: F > G ≥ H. I. F > H II. H ≥ F. Wrong move: "same letters, looks like a pair, so either-or".
Test I alone: F > G ≥ H, so F > H. I follows.
Test II: H ≥ F is the opposite of what the chain says. False in every case.
Answer: only I. Either-or is the last test, never the first.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Chain-walk each conclusion first. Mark it definite, false or not definite.
  2. One or both definite: the answer is only I, only II or both.
  3. Neither definite: are the letters the same two, and is the pair ‹ and ≥, › and ≤, or ≤ and ≥? Then either-or.
  4. Otherwise: neither.

Drill: the statements do not link the letters. What is the answer?

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Statements: P ≥ Q; R › Q. Conclusions: I. P › R II. P ≤ R." Options: only I, only II, either I or II, neither, both.
  • Either-or is often planted with one pair of the same letters and statements giving no link.
  • Wrong options are built from: a chain that turns, a ≥ upgraded to ›, or a missed either-or.
Warning. A clean either-or needs the two letters to be unlinked. If the statements already connect them, finish the walk first.

Lesson 3 · Coded inequalities: decode first, then solve

In one line: a coded question swaps the usual symbols for @, #, $, % and &; the question gives a key, you translate every symbol, then it is Lessons 1 and 2 again.

1Picture this

Your friend texts in code: "Ravi @ Sunil". Before you can read it you need the code book: say @ means "taller than". Once decoded it is plain English. Coded inequalities are a code book plus a chain.

@ means >
# means ≥

Your job: translate first. Never think in the code.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The question prints a key: for example "A @ B means A > B". Symbols like @, #, $, % and & are just labels with no meaning of their own.
The key changes from question to question. @ can be > in one question and ≤ in the next.
Step 1: write the key on rough paper as five rows: symbol, then the real sign.
Step 2: rewrite each coded statement with real signs, one symbol at a time, keeping the letter order.
Step 3: solve as before: walk the chain, test each conclusion, use either-or last.
Step 4: decode the conclusions the same way, then compare.
Reversed pair: "O & M" with & meaning < says O < M. Keep the letter order; do not swap.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Decode symbol by symbol: A @ B uses the key sign between A and B.
A coded statement with three letters, like P @ Q # R, is a chain: P > Q ≥ R if @ is > and # is ≥.
After decoding, all the rules of Lessons 1 and 2 apply unchanged.

Coded inequality is a user-defined symbol mapping: the question's key rules, not memory. Decode-then-solve: derived, Python-checked (every quiz and drill item is decoded by code and solved by enumeration).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Key: @ is >, # is ≥, $ is =, % is ≤, & is <. Statements: P @ Q # R; S $ R. Conclusion: P @ S

Step 1. Decode P @ Q # R: P > Q ≥ R. Decode S $ R: S = R.
Step 2. S = R, so S can stand in for R: P > Q ≥ S.
Step 3. One strict link, so P > S. The conclusion P @ S decodes to P > S.
Step 4. Definitely true.

Key: # is <, % is ≥, @ is >, $ is =, & is ≤. Statements: K # L; L & M. Conclusions: I. K % M II. M @ K

Step 1. K # L is K < L. L & M is L ≤ M. Chain: K < L ≤ M, so K < M.
Step 2. I: K % M decodes to K ≥ M. False in every case.
Step 3. II: M @ K decodes to M > K, the same as K < M. True.
Step 4. Only II follows.

5The trap

A habit trap: you memorised "@ is greater than" from one question and use it in the next, where @ is ≤.
Always rewrite the key first. Never decode from memory.
Second trap: reversing the letters. M @ K with @ as > is M > K, not K > M.
Check every decoded line once: letter order untouched, only the symbol swapped.

6Speed method and elimination

  1. Copy the key as five short rows.
  2. Decode every symbol in place, left to right.
  3. Lessons 1 and 2: flip, line up, walk.
  4. Decode the conclusions before comparing.

Drill key: @ is ›, # is ≥, $ is =, % is ≤, & is ‹. Decode out loud.
