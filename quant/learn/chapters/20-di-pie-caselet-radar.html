Data Interpretation II: pie, caselet, radar, mixed

In one line: turn every chart or paragraph into a small table, then use percent and ratio.

Lesson 1 · Pie charts: percent, degrees, and two-chart sets

In one line: a pie is a 360° circle, so 1% = 3.6°, and every slice answer is "slice × total".

1Picture this

A chai stall owner in Pune cuts a round cake into slices for 100 customers. A slice for 25 customers is a quarter of the cake, so the slice angle is a quarter turn: 90° (a full turn is 360°, and 360 ÷ 4 = 90).

Bigger share, bigger angle. Total of all shares = whole cake = 360° = 100%.

2The idea in small steps

A pie chart shows how one total is split into parts.
The whole circle is 360° and also 100% of the total.
So 1% of the total sits in 360 ÷ 100 = 3.6° of the circle.
Percent to degrees: multiply by 3.6. Degrees to percent: divide by 3.6.
To get a real count: percent of the total, or angle ÷ 360 × total. (The slice angle at the centre is also called the central angle.)
A table may give "women %" for each slice. The rest are men: men % = 100% − women %.
Two-chart sets: the pie gives the share, a table gives a second percent. Do share first, then percent of that share.

3The rule

Slice angle = (part ÷ total) × 360° = percent × 3.6°
Count = (percent ÷ 100) × total = (angle ÷ 360) × total

Sector angle for fraction Q = 360Q degrees: Wikipedia: Pie chart (sector angle = 360 × fraction).

4Worked example, one tap per step

Practice data, invented. 12,000 new pension-scheme enrolments in a month, split by state (pie below). Women are 40% of state A's enrolments. How many women in A?

StateShare of 12,000Women in that state
A30%40%
B20%55%
C15%60%
D25%20%
E10%50%
Step 1. A's share = 30% of 12,000.
Step 2. 30% = 3/10, so 12,000 ÷ 10 × 3 = 3,600 enrolments.
Step 3. Women = 40% of 3,600 = 3,600 ÷ 10 × 4 = 1,440.
Step 4. Answer: 1,440 women. Angle check: A slice = 30 × 3.6 = 108°.

5The trap

A question asks for "women as % of all 12,000" and the table shows 40, 55, 60, 20, 50.
Wrong move: average them: (40+55+60+20+50) ÷ 5 = 45%.
Right move: states have different sizes, so find each state's women first. A 1,440 (from the example). B: 20% of 12,000 = 2,400, 55% of 2,400 = 1,320. C: 15% = 1,800, 60% of 1,800 = 1,080. D: 25% = 3,000, 20% of 3,000 = 600. E: 10% = 1,200, 50% of 1,200 = 600.
Add them: 1,440+1,320+1,080+600+600 = 5,040. 5,040 ÷ 12,000 = 42%. (Ratio basics: chapter 19, lesson 1.)

Lesson 2 · Caselets: turn words into a table

In one line: a caselet is a data table written as a paragraph, so draw the table first and the questions become easy.

1Picture this

Your neighbour describes the sabzi bill in words: "I bought tomatoes and onions, onions cost twice the tomatoes..." You cannot add that in your head. You jot it down in rows. A caselet needs the same jotting.

2The idea in small steps

Read the whole paragraph once and underline every number.
Draw a table: one row per item (machine, branch, person), one column per fact.
Fill the facts. Turn "the rest" into 100% minus the others.
Turn each percent into a real count, using the right total.
Add a "check" cell: parts must add back to the total.
Only then read the questions.

3The rule

Defective = (share × total) × defect rate. Non-defective = made − defective.
"Rest" = whole − sum of the named parts.
One machine's share of all defects = its defective ÷ total defective.

Defective = faulty, not usable. Non-defective = good.

Overall rate = total defective ÷ total made (never the average of the rates).

These follow from the definition of a percentage (Wikipedia: Percentage); derived, Python-checked.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Practice data, invented. A printing unit prints 5,000 ID cards in a day on three machines. M1 prints 40% of the day's cards, M2 prints 35% and M3 the rest. Of the cards from M1, 2% are defective; from M2, 4%; from M3, 6%. How many defective cards in all?

Step 1. M3 share = 100 − 40 − 35 = 25%.
Step 2. Cards: M1 = 40% of 5,000 = 2,000. M2 = 35% = 1,750. M3 = 25% = 1,250. Check: 2,000+1,750+1,250 = 5,000.
Step 3. Defective: M1 2% of 2,000 = 40. M2 4% of 1,750 = 70. M3 6% of 1,250 = 75.
Step 4. Total = 40+70+75 = 185.

MachineMadeDefect rateDefectiveNon-defective
M12,0002%401960
M21,7504%701680
M31,2506%751175
Total5,0001854815

5The trap

Question: overall defective %. Rates are 2%, 4%, 6%.
Wrong move: average them to get 4%.
Right move: M1 made more than M3, so each rate must count for as many cards as it covers (this is "weighting"). Total defective ÷ total made: 185 ÷ 5,000 = 3.7%.

6Mental-math shortcut: percent of a round number

Split the percent into 1% and 10% blocks of the number. 1% of 1,750 is 17.5, so 4% = 70. 1% of 1,250 is 12.5, so 6% = 75.

Lesson 3 · Radar charts and mixed sets

In one line: a radar chart is a line graph bent into a circle, so read each spoke like a bar and compare the two shapes.

1Picture this

A cricket coach scores two batters on six skills and joins the dots on a spider web. A bigger shape means better overall, but each spoke is still just one number.

2The idea in small steps

A radar chart has spokes from one centre, each spoke is one category.
Spokes are equally spaced. Here 6 spokes are 60° apart.
Distance from the centre is the value. Rings show the scale.
Each series (one branch, one colour) is one outline joined across spokes.
Read the value of a point from the ring it touches.
Then it is a normal table: write both series in a row and use percent and average rules.

3The rule

Radar chart: three or more measured quantities (here, months), each on its own spoke, all spokes starting from the same centre.
Percent difference "A over B" = (A − B) ÷ B × 100.
Percent fall = (Old − New) ÷ Old × 100.
Average = sum ÷ how many values (chapter 19, lesson 2).

Radar definition: Wikipedia: Radar chart. Percent change is measured from the original (base) value: Wikipedia: Percentage.

4Worked example, one tap per step

Practice data, invented. Monthly enrolments (hundreds) at two branches, January to June.

■ Branch P   ■ Branch Q   (rings = hundreds of enrolments; each ring is 10)

BranchJanFebMarAprMayJun
P405535605045
Q304550406555

In which month is the gap between P and Q the largest?

Step 1. Read both rows off the table.
Step 2. Gaps: Jan 10, Feb 10, Mar 15, Apr 20, May 15, Jun 10.
Step 3. Largest is April (20), P above Q.

5The trap

Radar shapes look big or small, so you may judge by area.
Wrong move: "Q's shape looks a little bigger, so Q is higher in every month." In fact P and Q both total 285 (P: 40+55+35+60+50+45; Q: 30+45+50+40+65+55), and each is higher in only 3 of 6 months.
Right move: compare spoke by spoke, month by month.

6Mental-math shortcut: gap over base

Write the gap, then the base (the number after "than"), then reduce. 20 over 40 = 1/2 = 50%. 15 over 60 = 1/4 = 25%.

Speed drill

7Reading checklist

  1. Name each series and its colour.
  2. Note the scale unit (hundreds here).
  3. Copy values into a mini table.
  4. Answer from the table, not the picture.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "In how many months is A more than B?"
  • "By what percent is A above B in month X?"
  • "Find the average over six months."
  • "Find the percent fall from month X to month Y."

Radar is on the third-party topic list (Oliveboard), not official.

Wrong options are built from: dividing by the wrong base, giving the raw gap as the percent, and ratios flipped.
