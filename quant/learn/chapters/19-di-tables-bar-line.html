Data Interpretation I: Tables, Bar, Line

Three lessons, three full 5-question practice sets (Sets A, B, C). One lesson at a time, then stop.

Lesson 1: Reading tables

In one line: a table gives you exact numbers; your job is to pick the two you need, decide which one is the OLD (base), and do one small calculation.

1Picture this

You open the passbook page of a pension subscriber. Each row is one year, each column one fact: contribution, interest, balance. To answer "how much did the balance grow?" you do not read every number. You find the two cells you need and ignore the rest.

YearContribution (₹)Balance (₹)
Year 112,00012,000
Year 212,00025,000

Ask first: which row, which column, which one is the old value? Everything else is noise.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Read the title and the unit first (₹, thousands, per cent). A wrong unit ruins a right sum. (A series is one row or one coloured set of values, like "Office A" or "Product X".)
Read the question and underline the two things asked: which row(s), which column(s).
Circle only those cells. Ignore the rest of the table.
Decide the type: a share (part ÷ total), a change (new − old ÷ old), or a ratio (A : B).
For "than" and "from … to …", the base is the old / "than" value. Divide by it.
Estimate first (round to friendly numbers), then pick the option that matches. Exact long division is rarely needed.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Share % = Part ÷ Total × 100
Change % = (New − Old) ÷ Old × 100
Ratio A : B = A ÷ their HCF : B ÷ their HCF

HCF = the biggest number that divides both exactly (chapter 10). Example: HCF of 90 and 42 is 6, because 90 = 6 × 15 and 42 = 6 × 7.

Percentage change formula: Math Is Fun, "Percentage Change" (see Sources). Share is the same percentage idea with the total as base. Ratios: divide both numbers by the same value (Math Is Fun, "Ratios"); dividing by the HCF gives lowest terms (derived, Python-checked).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Office P received 250 forms in 2023 and 300 in 2024. It rejected 30 of the 2024 forms. Find the growth and the rejected share.

Practice data, invented for this guide. Not from any real exam.

Step 1. Growth: old = 250, new = 300. Change = 50.
Step 2. Divide by OLD: 50 ÷ 250 = 1/5 = 20%.
Step 3. Rejected share uses the 2024 total as base: 30 ÷ 300 = 1/10 = 10%.
Answer: 20% growth, 10% rejected. Two questions, two different bases.

5The trap

Question: "Office E: 350 forms in 2023, 420 in 2024. What is the % growth?" Change = 70.
Wrong move: divide by the NEW number. 70 ÷ 420 = 16.67%. This is exactly the option the examiner plants.
Right: divide by the OLD number. 70 ÷ 350 = 20%.
A second trap: the office with the biggest rise in numbers is not always the office with the biggest % growth. Compare like with like.

6Mental-math: friendly fractions, then compare

Turn every percentage into a fraction you know. Chunk one: 1/2, 1/4, 1/5, 1/8, 1/10.

Fraction%Fraction%
1/812.51/616.67
1/714.291/911.11
1/5201/425
Use it. 70 out of 350 is 1/5, so 20%. 72 out of 576 is 1/8, so 12.5%. Look for a small whole-number relationship before you divide.

Speed drill

Say it out loud first. Aim: under 5 seconds.

7Lab: one table, three answers

Type two cells of a table to see growth, share and ratio, with steps.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "What is the percentage increase in forms received by Office C from 2023 to 2024?"
  • "Rejected forms of Office A are what percent of its forms received in 2024?"
  • "What is the ratio of rejected forms of Office D to Office E?"
  • "Which office showed the highest percentage growth?"

Reported in memory-based exam analyses: DI was 8 questions (two sets, 5 + 3) in the 2025 Phase I and 5 questions in 2022. Table-type sets are among the formats typically listed by coaching sites (third-party list, not official).

Wrong options are built from the wrong base, the raw change read as a percent, the inverted ratio, and the wrong year. Name the base before you calculate.

Lesson 2: Bar charts

In one line: a bar chart is a table drawn with bar heights, so read the axis and unit, write the bar values into a mini-table, then calculate as before.

1Picture this

A chai stall owner draws how many cups he sold each day this week. Taller bar, more cups. He does not need numbers to see which day was best, but the exam asks "how many more", so you must read the value off the scale.

Mon 40
Tue 60
Wed 80

Wed − Mon = 40 more cups. Wed is double Mon.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A bar graph shows data with bars of different heights, so you can compare at a glance (Math Is Fun).
Read the title, then both axes (the axis is the numbered line along the side or bottom): what is measured and in which unit (units, thousands, ₹ crore; 1 crore = 1,00,00,000).
Check the legend (the small key that says which colour is which series).
Write every bar value you need into a tiny table on your rough sheet. Do this once.
Now it is a table question. Pick the formula: share, change or ratio.
Gap questions (bar A minus bar B in the same year) compare the heights directly. Do not use % unless asked.
"Absolute difference" just means the gap with the sign ignored: always big minus small.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Read the value → write a mini-table → then calculate
Average (mean) = Sum of values ÷ How many values
A is what % more than B = (A − B) ÷ B × 100

Bar graph: Math Is Fun "Bar Graphs"; Mean: Math Is Fun "How to Calculate the Mean". Good bar graphs begin the axis at zero (Math Is Fun), so check the axis before trusting bar lengths.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Using the chart below (Set B data): X sold 55 thousand in 2021 and 65 thousand in 2023. Find the % growth of X from 2021 to 2023.

Practice data, invented for this guide. Not from any real exam.

■ Product X   ■ Product Y   (units sold, in thousands)

Step 1. Read the bars: X 2021 = 55, X 2023 = 65. Unit: thousands.
Step 2. Change = 65 − 55 = 10.
Step 3. Divide by OLD (2021): 10 ÷ 55 = 2/11 (divide top and bottom by 5).
Step 4. 2 ÷ 11 = 0.1818, so 18.18%. Answer: about 18.2%. Estimate check: 55 × 1.2 = 66, just above 65, so a bit under 20%.

5The trap

The chart says "in thousands". Bar reads 60. A careless answer writes "60" for "how many units".
Right: 60 thousand = 60,000. If options are 60 and 60,000, the unit decides.
Second trap: "gap" is a subtraction, "how much % more" is a division by the base.
Year 2022: Y = 60, X = 50. Gap = 10. Y is 10 ÷ 50 = 20% more than X (base X, the "than" value).
But X is only 10 ÷ 60 = 16.67% less than Y (base Y). Different base, different answer.

6Mental-math: average by "guess and nudge"

Average of 40, 55, 50, 65, 80. Guess 60. Write each distance from 60: −20, −5, −10, +5, +20. Add them: −10. Divide by 5 values: −2. Average = 60 − 2 = 58. Check: sum 290 ÷ 5 = 58.

The median is the middle value when sorted (here 55). It is not the mean. Examiners use it as a wrong option.

Why it works. Each value = guess + distance, so the average = guess + average distance. Small numbers, no big addition.

Say it out loud first.

Lesson 3: Line charts and percent change

In one line: a line chart shows change over time; the slope between two points is the story, and every "growth" question is old-to-new percent change.

1Picture this

You track your daily steps for a week. Joining the dots shows a line going up, down, up. A line graph shows information connected in some way, usually as it changes over time (Math Is Fun). The exam asks: between which two days did it rise most, in per cent?

Day 1: 80
Day 2: 100
Day 3: 90

Up 20, then down 10. Different bases, so different percents.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Read the title, unit and legend. Note if the axis starts at zero (the axis is the numbered scale; the legend tells which line is which series). If it starts higher, small changes look bigger than they are (Math Is Fun gives this warning for bar graphs; it applies to any axis).
Read each point you need and write it in a mini-table, one row per series.
Percent change between two years: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
The steepest looking part of a line (its slope, how sharply it climbs) is the biggest raw rise, not always the biggest percent rise.
To find the biggest percent rise, test each interval with friendly fractions (1/5 = 20%, 1/4 = 25%).
A change in a percentage is measured in percentage points (the plain difference), not in percent.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Change % = (New − Old) ÷ Old × 100
Percentage points = (second percentage) − (first percentage)
Example: 40% to 44% is +4 percentage points, and a 10% rise in that quantity

Percentage point definition and the 40% to 44% example: Wikipedia "Percentage point". Line graph: Math Is Fun "Line Graphs".

4Worked examples, one tap per step

A line goes 80 → 100 → 90 (three years, invented). Find the % change in each step and overall.

Practice data, invented for this guide. Not from any real exam.

Step 1. 80 → 100: change 20, ÷ 80 = 1/4 = 25% rise.
Step 2. 100 → 90: change −10, ÷ 100 = 10% fall.
Step 3. Overall 80 → 90: change 10, ÷ 80 = 1/8 = 12.5% rise.
Check: 1.25 × 0.90 = 1.125. The two steps do not add (25 − 10 = 15 is wrong).

5The trap

Growth rate = the % growth in a year. It was 10% one year and 25% the next. "By how much did the growth rate rise?"
Wrong move: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5, so "150%" or "2.5". That is the relative change of the rates (one rate divided by the other), which is not what was asked.
Right: when the question is about percentages themselves, subtract: 25 − 10 = 15 percentage points.

6Mental-math: the growth ladder

Memorise these pairs. They turn most intervals into one glance.

Old → NewChange %
×1.1 (e.g. 200 → 220)+10%
×1.25 (e.g. 80 → 100)+25%
×0.8 (e.g. 220 → 176)−20%
×0.9 (e.g. 100 → 90)−10%
Use it. 220 → 176: 176 ÷ 220 = 0.8, so a 20% fall. And to undo +25% you need −20% (1/4 up, 1/5 down). To undo +10% you need about −9.09%, because the new value is 11 parts and you remove 1 of them: 1 ÷ 11 = 9.09%.
