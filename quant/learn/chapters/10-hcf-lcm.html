Number basics: HCF, LCM, remainders

Lesson 1 · HCF and LCM

In one line: HCF is the biggest number that fits into both, LCM is the smallest number both fit into.

1Picture this

A sweet shop in Jaipur has 12 laddoos and 18 barfis. The owner wants identical gift packets with nothing left over. How many packets, and what is the biggest packet count that works? Both 12 and 18 split evenly into 6 packets: 2 laddoos + 3 barfis each. That 6 is the HCF.

Now two buses leave a stand, one every 12 min and one every 18 min, both at 9:00. They leave together again at 9:36. That 36 is the LCM.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A factor of a number divides it exactly. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.
A multiple is what you get by multiplying: multiples of 12 are 12, 24, 36, 48 ...
HCF (also called GCD) = the greatest number that divides both.
LCM = the smallest number that both divide into.
A prime is a number divisible only by 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 ... Prime factorisation breaks a number into primes: 36 = 6 × 6 = (2×3) × (2×3) = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3.
A small raised number (a power) counts how many times to multiply: 2² = 2 × 2, so 36 = 2²×3². Likewise 48 = 6 × 8 = (2×3) × (2×2×2) = 2⁴×3.
HCF: take each common prime with its smallest power. LCM: take every prime with its biggest power.

3The rules (now that you get it)

HCF = product of common primes, each with the LOWEST power
LCM = product of all primes, each with the HIGHEST power
For TWO numbers only: HCF × LCM = first × second
So if one number is missing: other = HCF × LCM ÷ the known number

Definitions, prime-power method and HCF×LCM = a×b: Wikipedia "Greatest common divisor" and "Least common multiple".

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find the HCF and LCM of 36 and 48.

Step 1. 36 = 2²×3². 48 = 2⁴×3 (from the steps above).
Step 2. HCF: lowest powers of the common primes 2 and 3: 2²×3 = 12.
Step 3. LCM: highest powers of all primes: 2⁴×3² = 16×9 = 144.
Step 4. Check: 12 × 144 = 1728 and 36 × 48 = 1728. They match.

Find the LCM of 12, 18, 30.

Step 1. 12 = 2²×3. 18 = 2×3². 30 = 2×3×5.
Step 2. Highest powers: 2², 3², 5.
Step 3. LCM = 4 × 9 × 5 = 180. (HCF of the three is 6: lowest powers 2×3.)

5The trap

Rule HCF × LCM = product is guaranteed for two numbers only. With three numbers it usually fails.
Take 4, 6, 10. Product = 4 × 6 × 10 = 240. 4 = 2², 6 = 2×3, 10 = 2×5, so HCF = 2 and LCM = 2²×3×5 = 60. Wrong move: 2 × 60 = 120, which is not 240.
Second trap: multiplying the numbers to get a common multiple. 12 × 18 = 216 is a common multiple, but the least one is 36.
Right: for three numbers always use prime powers, never rely on the product rule.

6Mental-math shortcut

Fast HCF by Euclid (from the Wikipedia GCD page): replace (a, b) by (b, remainder of a ÷ b) until the remainder is 0. (Remainder = what is left over: 48 ÷ 18 = 2 with 12 left over. Lesson 3 below teaches it fully.) Last non-zero number is the HCF.

Example. HCF(48, 18): 48 ÷ 18 leaves 12; 18 ÷ 12 leaves 6; 12 ÷ 6 leaves 0. HCF = 6. Then LCM = 48 × 18 ÷ 6 = 144. Divide first, then multiply: 48 ÷ 6 = 8, 8 × 18 = 144.

Lesson 2 · HCF/LCM word problems

In one line: splitting things equally means HCF, waiting for things to line up again means LCM.

1Picture this

Three temple bells ring every 12, 15 and 20 minutes. They all ring together at 6:00 am. When next? You want the first time all three line up: the LCM = 60 minutes (worked out in the examples below), so 7:00 am.

Now a floor 360 cm × 240 cm needs square tiles with no cutting. The biggest tile side that fits both lengths is the HCF = 120 cm (360 ÷ 240 leaves 120; 240 ÷ 120 leaves 0, so 120). Then 360 ÷ 120 = 3 tiles along and 240 ÷ 120 = 2 across, 3 × 2 = 6 tiles.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Ask: am I splitting into equal pieces (making things smaller)? Then think HCF.
Or am I waiting for events to meet again (making things bigger)? Then think LCM.
Word clues for HCF: greatest, largest, longest, maximum, equal pieces, no leftover.
Word clues for LCM: least, smallest, together again, after how long. Careful: "least number of tiles / pieces" is still HCF (biggest piece gives fewest pieces).
Remainder = the leftover after dividing (47 ÷ 5 = 9 with 2 left; lesson 3 below teaches it). The divisor is the number you divide BY.
Remainder type: a number leaves remainder r with divisors a, b, c. Then (number − r) is a multiple of all of them: it is a multiple of the LCM.
(r itself trivially works, so exams mean the next one: LCM + r.)
"Increased by d is divisible": the number plus d is a multiple of the LCM, so the smallest number is LCM − d.
Largest divisor with given remainders: subtract the remainder from each number first, then take the HCF.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Smallest number (other than r itself) leaving the same remainder r: LCM + r
Smallest number such that adding d makes it divisible by all: LCM − d (if that is positive)
Largest divisor leaving remainders r1, r2 on the numbers x, y: HCF(x − r1, y − r2). It must be bigger than every remainder.

Built from the division theorem (dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder, 0 ≤ remainder < divisor): Wikipedia "Euclidean division". The three rules are derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find the longest rope piece: ropes of 84 m, 126 m and 210 m cut into equal pieces. How many pieces in all?

Step 1. Equal pieces, longest: HCF.
Step 2. 84 = 2²×3×7, 126 = 2×3²×7, 210 = 2×3×5×7. Common: 2×3×7 = 42.
Step 3. Pieces: 84÷42 + 126÷42 + 210÷42 = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 pieces of 42 m.

Smallest number which leaves remainder 5 when divided by 12, 15 and 20?

Step 1. LCM(12, 15, 20): 12 = 2²×3, 15 = 3×5, 20 = 2²×5. Highest powers: 2²×3×5 = 60.
Step 2. Add the remainder: 60 + 5 = 65.
Step 3. Check: 65 = 12×5+5 = 15×4+5 = 20×3+5. Apart from 5 itself (5 = 12×0+5), no smaller number works (checked by computer).

Largest number that divides 245 and 1029 leaving remainder 5 each time?

Step 1. Subtract the remainder: 245 − 5 = 240, 1029 − 5 = 1024.
Step 2. HCF(240, 1024): 240 = 2⁴×3×5, 1024 = 2¹⁰. Common: 2⁴ = 16.
Step 3. Check: 245 = 16 × 15 + 5, 1029 = 16 × 64 + 5, and 16 is bigger than 5.

Smallest number which, when increased by 7, is divisible by 12 and 16?

Step 1. LCM(12, 16): 12 = 2²×3, 16 = 2⁴. Highest powers: 2⁴×3 = 48.
Step 2. The number plus 7 must be 48 (the smallest positive multiple). So number = 48 − 7 = 41.
Step 3. Check: 41 + 7 = 48 = 12 × 4 = 16 × 3.

5The trap

Remainder question: smallest number leaving remainder 5 with 12, 15, 20.
Wrong move: stop at the LCM and answer 60. But 60 ÷ 12 leaves 0, not 5.
Second trap: largest divisor of 245 and 1029 with remainder 5. Wrong move: HCF(245, 1029) directly. 245 = 5×7², 1029 = 3×7³, HCF = 7²=49, but 245 ÷ 49 leaves 0, not 5.
Right: subtract the remainder first (240 and 1024), then HCF = 16.

6Mental-math shortcut: pick HCF or LCM from the keyword

Words in questionUse
greatest / largest / longest / equal pieces / no leftoverHCF
least / smallest / together again / after how longLCM
least number of tiles / piecesHCF (then divide)
remainder r, same for all divisorsLCM + r (ignoring r itself)
"largest number" with remaindersHCF of (number − remainder)
Time trick. Events together again: write time as a clock jump. 6:00 + 60 min = 7:00. Convert hours and minutes into one unit before taking LCM.

Lesson 3 · Remainders and units digit

In one line: a remainder is the leftover after dividing, and the units digit of a power just repeats in a short loop.

1Picture this

You have 47 laddoos for 5 friends. Each gets 9, and 2 are left. 47 = 5 × 9 + 2. That leftover 2 is the remainder.

Now the last digit of 7 × 7 × 7 × ... Only the last digit of each number matters, and the last digit (the units digit) is just the remainder when dividing by 10.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Remainder = what is left after dividing. The divisor is the number you divide BY, the quotient is how many whole times it fits. The remainder is always smaller than the divisor.
Division rule: number = divisor × quotient + remainder. 47 = 5×9 + 2.
To find the remainder of a sum or product, find each remainder first, then add or multiply them, then take the remainder again.
Units digit of a number = its remainder when divided by 10. Only the last digit of each number matters.
A power like 753 (written 7^53 in the quiz) means 7 multiplied by itself 53 times; 7 is the base and 53 the exponent. Powers repeat: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 ... last digits 2, 4, 8, 6, 2, 4 ... a loop of length 4.
So for a big power, find where you land inside the loop.

3The rules (now that you get it)

N = d × q + r, with 0 ≤ r < d
Remainder of (a × b) ÷ m = remainder of (rem a × rem b) ÷ m. Same for a + b.
Last-digit loops: 0,1,5,6 stay put · 4 and 9: loop of 2 · 2,3,7,8: loop of 4

Remainder rules (compatibility of congruence with +, ×, powers): Wikipedia "Modular arithmetic". Last-digit loops: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Remainder when 27 × 38 is divided by 7?

Step 1. 27 ÷ 7: 7 × 3 = 21, leaves 6. 38 ÷ 7: 7 × 5 = 35, leaves 3.
Step 2. Multiply remainders: 6 × 3 = 18.
Step 3. 18 ÷ 7: 7 × 2 = 14, leaves 4. Answer: 4.
Check: 27 × 38 = 1026 = 7 × 146 + 4.

Units digit of 753?

Step 1. Loop for 7: 7, 9, 3, 1 (length 4).
Step 2. 53 ÷ 4: 4 × 13 = 52, leaves 1, so we land on the 1st place of the loop.
Step 3. 1st place = 7. Answer: 7.

5The trap

Units digit of 220. Loop of 2: 2, 4, 8, 6. 20 ÷ 4 leaves 0.
Wrong move: leftover 0 means "digit 0" or 20 = 1.
Right: remainder 0 means the last place of the loop, which is 6. (24 = 16 ends in 6, and so does 220.)
Second trap: remainder of (17 + 19) ÷ 5. 17 ÷ 5 leaves 2 and 19 ÷ 5 leaves 4. Wrong: 2 + 4 = 6 as the answer. Remainders must be below 5, so reduce again: 6 ÷ 5 leaves 1.
