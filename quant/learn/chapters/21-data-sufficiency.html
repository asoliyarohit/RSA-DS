Data Sufficiency

In one line: you do not solve the question, you only decide whether the statements give enough to solve it.

LOW priority. Why this chapter, honestly. Data Sufficiency appears only on a third-party topic list (Oliveboard, not official). The official PFRDA notification lists no Quant topics. In the 2025 and 2022 question counts reported in memory-based exam analyses, Data Sufficiency had zero questions in both years. We have no evidence it is tested. Do this chapter last, only after the high-count topics (DI, quadratic equations, number series, arithmetic), as a short insurance skill.

Lesson 1 · The method: one statement at a time

1Picture this

Your friend texts: "How much did the auto cost?" Message 1: "It was double the bus fare." Message 2: "The bus fare was ₹15." Alone, neither tells you the cost. Together, 2 × 15 = ₹30. Data Sufficiency is exactly this: do you have enough to answer, from message 1, from message 2, or only from both?

Notice you never needed to be asked for the answer at the end. You only needed to know whether it could be found.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

You get a question and two statements, I and II.
Question type A (value): "What is x?" Enough means exactly one value comes out.
Question type B (yes/no): "Is N even?" Enough means the answer is always yes or always no. A definite NO is also enough. "Sometimes" is not enough.
Read the question, then cover II. Does I alone give enough? Write yes or no.
Now cover I. Does II alone give enough? Write yes or no. Do not borrow anything from the other statement.
Only if both were "no", join them. Now is it enough?
Your two yes/no notes and the joined check decide the answer.

3The rules (now that you get it)

I yes, II no: answer is "I alone". I no, II yes: "II alone".
I yes AND II yes: "Either alone is enough".
I no, II no, joined yes: "Both together". Joined no: "Even together, not enough".

Our summary (derived from the method above, not from any PFRDA paper): with two statements there are exactly these five possible verdicts. Real papers word and order the options in their own way, and some bank-exam Data Sufficiency sets use three statements (I, II, III) instead of two (seen on an Oliveboard practice page, see Sources). Read the directions carefully on the day. The quizzes here show four of the five at a time to fit four boxes.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Value question. What is the price of one pen? I. 5 pens cost ₹60. II. A pen costs ₹2 less than a pencil.

Step 1. Cover II. From I: 60 ÷ 5 = 12. One value. I alone is enough.
Step 2. Cover I. From II: pen = pencil − 2, pencil price unknown. Many values. II alone fails.
Step 3. I yes, II no. Verdict: I alone. (We stop. Joining is not needed.)

Yes/no question. Is N divisible by 3? I. N is divisible by 6. II. N is even.

Step 1. I: a multiple of 6 is 6 × something, which contains 3. So always YES. Enough.
Step 2. II: 4 is even (not divisible by 3), 6 is even (divisible by 3). Sometimes yes, sometimes no. Not enough.
Step 3. Verdict: I alone. If I had said "N is not divisible by 3" the answer would be a definite NO, still enough.

5The trap

How old is Ravi? I. Ravi is 5 years older than Meena. II. Meena is 20.
Wrong move: while checking I, you remember "Meena is 20" from II, so you say "I alone: 20 + 5 = 25". That used II's facts inside I's test.
I by itself: Ravi = Meena + 5, Meena unknown. Not enough. II by itself: Meena only, Ravi never mentioned. Not enough.
Joined: 20 + 5 = 25. Correct verdict: Both together. Wrong verdict from borrowing: "I alone".

6Mental-math shortcut: the "5 verdicts" recall

Do not compute, label. Learn the shortcut question for each statement: "Does this give ONE value (or one definite yes/no)?" Say yes or no in under 10 seconds, then move on.

Fast flags for "No". A statement fails alone if: (a) it has fewer facts than unknowns, (b) it is a square or "less than / more than" statement leaving several values, (c) it talks about a different thing from what is asked.

Lesson 2 · Algebra statements: equations, squares, yes/no

1Picture this

Two friends split a chai bill at a stall. Clue 1: "Together we paid ₹10." Clue 2: "I paid ₹2 more than you." One clue says only the total. The other says only the gap. Both give you each person's share: ₹6 and ₹4.

₹6₹4

Total 10, difference 2. Two different facts, two unknowns: enough.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Each statement is a clue about unknowns such as x and y.
Two unknowns usually need two different clues to pin both down.
"Different" matters. "x + y = 3" and "2x + 2y = 6" say the same thing. The second gives no new information.
Two clues that contradict each other (x + y = 3 and x + y = 9) have no solution at all.
Squares are sneaky: x² = 16 gives two values, 4 and −4, unless another clue removes one.
If the question asks for x + y, you may not need x and y separately. Check what is actually asked.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Unknowns to find = number of different clues needed (usually).
Clues that are multiples of each other count as ONE clue.
Square statement: expect two values (plus and minus).

Dependent equations give no new information; inconsistent ones have no solution: Math Is Fun "Systems of Linear Equations" (see Sources).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find x. I. x + y = 10. II. x − y = 2.

Step 1. I alone: x + y = 10 has many pairs (1, 9), (5, 5), (6, 4). Not enough.
Step 2. II alone: x − y = 2 has many pairs (3, 1), (6, 4). Not enough.
Step 3. Join: add the two clues: 2x = 12, so x = 6 (and y = 4).
Step 4. Verdict: Both together.

Is x > 5? I. x² = 36. II. x > 0.

Step 1. I: x = 6 (yes, bigger than 5) or x = −6 (no). Mixed. Not enough.
Step 2. II: x = 1 gives no, x = 10 gives yes. Mixed. Not enough.
Step 3. Join: x = ±6 and positive leaves x = 6. 6 > 5, always yes.
Step 4. Verdict: Both together.

5The trap

Find x. I. x + y = 3. II. 2x + 2y = 6.
Wrong move: "two statements, two unknowns, so together they must be enough". That counts statements, not different information.
Divide II by 2 and you get x + y = 3, exactly I. So the pair is one clue wearing two coats.
x = 0, y = 3 and x = 1, y = 2 both fit. Correct verdict: Even together, not enough.

6Mental-math shortcut: add or subtract the two clues

When both clues are like "x + y = a" and "x − y = b", add them: 2x = a + b. Subtract them: 2y = a − b. This is the quickest mental route.

Use it. x + y = 10 and x − y = 4: add, 2x = 14, x = 7. Then y = 3. Small numbers only: you are checking, not solving to the end.

Speed drill

Say the answer out loud before you tap Show.

7Lab: do two equations pin down x and y?

Type the numbers of two equations: a·x + b·y = c. Watch the verdict.

Try 1, 1, 3 and 2, 2, 6 (same clue). Try 1, 1, 3 and 1, 1, 9 (contradiction).

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "What is the value of x? I. x + y = 8. II. 3x + 3y = 24." (one clue twice: not enough.)
  • "Is x positive? I. x² > 0. II. x³ > 0." (II alone: a cube keeps the sign, while a square hides it.)
  • "What is the area of the rectangle? I. Perimeter is 30 cm. II. Length is 3 cm more than breadth."
  • "What is x + y? I. x − y = 1. II. 2x + 2y = 14."
How the wrong options are built. Look for the "two statements, two unknowns" bait (dependent clues), the "x² = number" bait (two roots), and the "asks for x + y but you solved x and y" detour.

Right = +1.25, wrong = −0.3125 (official 1/4 negative rule). Guessing breaks even at a 20% chance, so guess only when your chance is above that.

10Lock it in

Cover the page, say it out loud: (1) Do two clues always beat two unknowns? (2) How many values does x² = 16 give? (3) When asked for x + y, must I find x? (4) What do I do first with "x + y = a, x − y = b"?
Memorise this
1. Count DIFFERENT clues, not statements.
2. Multiples of one clue = one clue.
3. x² = k gives plus and minus.
4. Read what is asked: x + y may be easy.
5. Add and subtract paired clues.
