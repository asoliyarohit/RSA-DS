Quadratic Equations (x and y comparison)

In one line: solve two small quadratics, list the roots, and compare x with y. Reported as 5 questions in 2025 in memory-based exam analyses, so this chapter is worth the effort.

Lesson 1 · Solve one quadratic

In one line: a quadratic is solved by finding two numbers that multiply to the last term and add to the middle one, then flipping their signs to get the roots.

1Picture this

At the hostel mess a friend sets you a puzzle: “two numbers multiply to 12 and add to 7”. You think: 3 and 4. A quadratic equation is that same puzzle in disguise.

3 × 4= 12

Product 12, sum 7. Factor-pair thinking is the whole engine of this chapter.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A quadratic equation is one where the highest power of x is x² (see chapter 11 for powers). Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b, c are plain numbers and a is not zero. A term is one chunk of the equation, like ax² or bx or c.
It usually has two answers for x. They are called roots. A "real" root is an ordinary number you can put on a number line.
Look at x² − 7x + 12 = 0. Find two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to −7. Those are −3 and −4.
Write it as (x − 3)(x − 4) = 0. A product is zero only if one part is zero.
So x − 3 = 0 or x − 4 = 0. Roots: x = 3 and x = 4. Note: the roots have the OPPOSITE sign to the numbers inside the brackets.
If the x² has a number in front (like 2x²), multiply a by c first and hunt for a pair with that product (worked in example 2 below). That is the “split the middle term” method.
Untidy equation? Move everything to one side. x² = 5x − 6: subtract 5x and add 6 on both sides, giving x² − 5x + 6 = 0. With brackets, expand first: (x + 3)(x − 2) = 6 becomes x² − 2x + 3x − 6 = 6, so x² + x − 6 = 6, so x² + x − 12 = 0.
Quick check on any answer: the two roots add up to −b/a and multiply to c/a. For x² − 7x + 12 (a = 1): 3 + 4 = 7 and 3 × 4 = 12. If a is not 1, divide the whole equation by a first (3x² − 12x + 9 = 0 becomes x² − 4x + 3 = 0).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Standard form: ax² + bx + c = 0
Split the middle: find two numbers with product = a × c and sum = b. Rewrite bx using them, group, factor.
Zero-product rule: if (px + q)(rx + s) = 0, then px + q = 0 or rx + s = 0.
Sign rule for x² + bx + c (a = 1, real roots): c positive → both roots have the SAME sign, the opposite of b’s sign. c negative → roots have DIFFERENT signs; the root that is bigger (ignoring its sign) has the opposite sign of b.

Standard form, factoring (AC method) and zero-product: Math Is Fun pages in Sources. Sign rule: derived, Python-checked (all integer root pairs from −20 to 20).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Solve x² − 7x + 12 = 0.

Step 1. a = 1, b = −7, c = 12. Need product 12 and sum −7.
Step 2. Pairs of 12: (1,12), (2,6), (3,4). Signs: c is positive so both same sign; b is negative so both negative: −3 and −4 (sum −7).
Step 3. Split: x² − 3x − 4x + 12. Take x out of the first two terms and −4 out of the last two: x(x − 3) − 4(x − 3). Both leave (x − 3), so it is (x − 3)(x − 4).
Step 4. Roots: x = 3 and x = 4. Check: 3 + 4 = 7 = −b, 3 × 4 = 12 = c.

Solve 2x² + 7x + 3 = 0.

Step 1. a = 2, b = 7, c = 3. Product a × c = 6, sum must be 7.
Step 2. Pairs of 6: (1,6) adds to 7. Good.
Step 3. Split: 2x² + x + 6x + 3. Take x out of the first two terms and 3 out of the last two: x(2x + 1) + 3(2x + 1). Both leave (2x + 1), so it is (2x + 1)(x + 3).
Step 4. 2x + 1 = 0: take 1 across to get 2x = −1, so x = −1/2. x + 3 = 0 gives x = −3. Answer: −1/2 and −3.

5The trap

Equation: x² − 7x + 12 = 0. Factors: (x − 3)(x − 4).
Wrong move: copy the numbers inside the brackets with their signs and write the roots as −3 and −4.
Test x = −3: (−3)² − 7(−3) + 12 = 9 + 21 + 12 = 42, not 0. So −3 is wrong.
Right: (x − 3) = 0 means x = +3. Roots are the numbers that make a bracket zero: opposite sign to the number inside. Always substitute one root to check.

Learn the pairs of c once. Then you only look for the pair whose sum is |b| (b with its sign ignored, so |−7| = 7).

Lesson 2 · The x vs y relation grid

In one line: solve both equations, put the four roots on a number line, and compare the x roots against the y roots one pair at a time.

1Picture this

Two teams, X and Y, each have two players with heights in cm. The coach asks: “is X taller than Y?” You can only say yes if EVERY X player is taller than EVERY Y player. If the tallest of Y stands between X players, you cannot say.

x roots: 2, 3y roots: 1, 2

Here the shortest X (2) ties with the tallest Y (2). That tie is why the answer is “≥”, not “>”. (≥ means “greater than or equal to”, ≤ means “less than or equal to”.)

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Question type: two equations, one in x and one in y. Each gives two values (usually).
Step A: solve the x equation. Write the two x values, small to large.
Step B: solve the y equation. Write the two y values, small to large.
Step C: min means smallest and max means largest. Compare the SMALLEST x with the LARGEST y, and the LARGEST x with the SMALLEST y. Only these two checks decide everything.
Step D: if smallest x is above largest y, every x beats every y: x > y. If largest x is below smallest y: x < y. If both a strict and a loose statement are true (x > y and x ≥ y), give the strict one: it is the most exact.
Step E: if they only touch (equal) and nothing crosses, use ≥ or ≤. If the x values and y values overlap or interleave (some x below a y and some x above it, like x = −3, 2 and y = −1, 3), there is no relation.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Let x have roots in X, y in Y. Compare min X, max X with min Y, max Y.
x > y if min X > max Y  ·  x < y if max X < min Y
(“Strictly >” means greater and not equal.)
x ≥ y if min X ≥ max Y (and not strictly >)  ·  x ≤ y if max X ≤ min Y (and not strictly <)
Otherwise: no relation. If every root of both is the same single value, x = y.

This is a logical definition: “x > y” means true for every pair. Derived, Python-checked against the pair-by-pair definition on 20,000 random root sets.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

I. x² − 5x + 6 = 0   II. y² − 3y + 2 = 0

Step 1. x: pair with product 6, sum −5 is −2, −3. (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0. x = 2, 3.
Step 2. y: product 2, sum −3 is −1, −2. (y − 1)(y − 2) = 0. y = 1, 2.
Step 3. Smallest x = 2, largest y = 2. Equal, so not strictly greater. Largest x = 3, smallest y = 1: x not below y.
Step 4. Every x is at least every y: x ≥ y.

I. x² + x − 6 = 0   II. y² − 2y − 3 = 0

Step 1. x: product −6, sum +1: 3 and −2 give sum +1, so factors (x + 3)(x − 2). x = −3, 2.
Step 2. y: product −3, sum −2: the numbers are −3 and +1. (y − 3)(y + 1) = 0. y = 3, −1.
Step 3. Smallest x = −3 is below biggest y = 3. Biggest x = 2 is above smallest y = −1. Both directions occur.
Step 4. Answer: no relation (x = y or cannot be established).

5The trap

Equations: x² − 5x + 6 = 0 (x = 2, 3) and y² − 3y + 2 = 0 (y = 1, 2).
Wrong move: pair them in order. Biggest with biggest: 3 > 2. Smallest with smallest: 2 > 1. Conclude x > y.
That ignores the pair x = 2 and y = 2. They are equal, so “x > y” fails for that pair.
Right: check ALL four pairs (or just min X vs max Y). The answer is x ≥ y.

Lesson 3 · Discriminant and shortcuts

In one line: the number b² − 4ac tells you how many real roots there are before you solve, and sum and product of roots let you check or skip the work.

1Picture this

Before a long train journey you check the ticket status: confirmed, waitlisted, or cancelled. The discriminant does the same for a quadratic: two real answers, one repeated answer, or none. It costs 10 seconds and saves you from chasing roots that do not exist.

D > 0: twoD = 0: oneD < 0: none

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

For ax² + bx + c = 0, compute D = b² − 4ac. Call it the discriminant.
If D is positive, there are two different real roots.
If D is zero, there is exactly one real root, repeated (it equals −b/2a). In the x-vs-y question, that equation gives ONE number.
If D is negative, there are no real roots. (These are not used in the x-vs-y comparison.)
Many k-questions ask “for what k are the roots equal (repeated)?” Set D = 0 and solve for k. Example x² − 6x + k = 0: D = 36 − 4k = 0, so k = 9.
If D is a perfect square (a number whose root is whole: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 ...) and a, b, c are whole numbers, the roots are fractions or whole numbers that factor neatly.
Sum of roots = −b/a. Product of roots = c/a. Use them to check your roots in 3 seconds.
Also watch for x² = k (like x² = 49): this has TWO roots, +7 and −7.

3The rules (now that you get it)

D = b² − 4ac   (D > 0: two real roots; D = 0: one repeated; D < 0: none real)
Quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √D) ÷ 2a (± means do a plus once and a minus once: that gives the two roots)
Sum of roots = −b/a  ·  Product of roots = c/a
Build an equation from roots r and s: x² − (r + s)x + rs = 0

Discriminant cases and Vieta’s formulas: Wikipedia “Quadratic equation”; formula and discriminant: Math Is Fun. Building an equation from roots: from a(x − r)(x − s) = 0 on Wikipedia with a = 1; derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find D and the roots of 2x² − 5x + 3 = 0.

Step 1. a = 2, b = −5, c = 3.
Step 2. D = (−5)² − 4 × 2 × 3 = 25 − 24 = 1. Positive and a perfect square: two neat roots.
Step 3. −b = 5, √D = √1 = 1, 2a = 4. So x = (5 ± 1) ÷ 4: (5 + 1) ÷ 4 = 6/4 = 3/2 and (5 − 1) ÷ 4 = 4/4 = 1.
Step 4. Check: sum = 5/2 = −b/a. Product = 3/2 = c/a. Both match.

Build the equation whose roots are 3 and −5.

Step 1. Sum = 3 + (−5) = −2. Product = 3 × (−5) = −15.
Step 2. x² − (sum)x + product = x² − (−2)x + (−15).
Step 3. Answer: x² + 2x − 15 = 0. Check: it matches the Lesson 1 example.

5The trap

Equations: I. x² = 49   II. y − 7 = 0, so y = 7.
Wrong move: take the square root and write only x = 7. Then x = y.
Right: x² = 49 gives x = 7 AND x = −7. Test: (−7)² = 49.
Now x is in {−7, 7}, y = 7. Every x is at most y, but x = −7 is strictly below: answer x ≤ y, not x = y.

6Mental-math shortcut: perfect squares and Vieta, plus practice (8 items)

Squares to know for D. 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225. If D is one of these, roots are neat and factoring works. If not (with whole-number a, b, c), the roots are not whole numbers or fractions: use the formula.
Check by sum and product (called Vieta’s formulas). After finding two roots, add them: must equal −b/a. Multiply them: must equal c/a. 3 seconds, catches sign slips.
