Inequalities and Quantity Comparison

Lesson 1: Linear inequalities and the flip rule

In one line: solve it like an equation, but flip the sign whenever you multiply or divide by a negative.

1Picture this

Rohan has ₹500 in his wallet and a bus pass costs ₹60 per trip. "How many trips can he afford?" is not asking for ONE number. It is asking for a whole range: any number of trips up to 8 works.

0 to 8 trips: affordable9, 10: no

An inequality is an equation that answers with a range, not a single value.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Symbols: > means greater than, < means less than. The small end points at the smaller number.
≥ and ≤ also allow "equal to". x ≥ 3 means x can be 3 or anything bigger.
You can add or subtract the same number on both sides. The sign does not change.
You can multiply or divide both sides by a positive number. The sign does not change.
Multiply or divide by a negative number and the sign flips. Reason: 7 > 3 but −7 < −3.
Solve like an equation, step by step, and keep one eye on the sign every time you meet a minus.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Add or subtract the same number: sign stays.
Multiply or divide by a positive number: sign stays.
Multiply or divide by a negative number: sign FLIPS.
Swapping the two sides also flips: 7 < x is the same as x > 7.

Rules as stated in Math Is Fun "Solving Inequalities" and Wikipedia "Inequality (mathematics)" (see Sources).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Solve 3x − 7 > 11.

Step 1. Add 7 to both sides: 3x > 18.
Step 2. Divide both sides by 3 (positive, so no flip): x > 6.
Step 3. Check with x = 7: 3×7 − 7 = 14, and 14 > 11. True. Answer: x > 6.

Solve 5 − 2x ≤ 13.

Step 1. Subtract 5 from both sides: −2x ≤ 8.
Step 2. Divide both sides by −2. Negative, so flip: x ≥ −4.
Step 3. Check with x = 0: 5 − 0 = 5 and 5 ≤ 13. True. Check with x = −5: 5 + 10 = 15, and 15 ≤ 13 is false, correctly outside. Answer: x ≥ −4.

5The trap

Question: solve −3x < 12.
Wrong move: divide by −3 and keep the sign. That gives x < −4.
Test the wrong answer: x = −5 should work, but −3 × (−5) = 15 and 15 < 12 is false.
Right: dividing by −3 flips the sign, so x > −4. Test x = 0: 0 < 12 is true.

6Mental-math shortcut: test one number

After solving, plug in ONE number from your answer range. If it fails, you probably forgot to flip. It costs about 5 seconds.

Routine. (1) Move the plain number across by adding or subtracting. (2) Look at the number stuck to x. Is it negative? Flip. (3) Divide. (4) Test one value.

Speed drill: flip or stay?

Say the answer before you tap Show. Aim: under 5 seconds each.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Solve: 4x − 5 < 19. Which is the correct range for x?"
  • "What is the smallest integer x that satisfies 3x − 4 > 14?"
  • "What is the largest integer x such that 7 − 3x > −5?"

Word clues: "smallest integer" with > means start just ABOVE the boundary. "Largest integer" with < means stop just BELOW it.

How the wrong options are built. The examiner plants the un-flipped answer, the answer with the boundary value included, and the answer where you forgot one step. If your result appears as option A with the sign the wrong way round, check for a negative divide.

Memorise this

Lesson 2: Quantity I versus Quantity II

In one line: work out each quantity fully, then compare the two numbers; if the comparison can flip, it cannot be determined.

1Picture this

Two mobile plans. Plan I gives 20% off a ₹150 recharge. Plan II gives 30% off a ₹90 recharge. Which discount is bigger in rupees? The bigger percentage does not automatically win.

Plan I: 20% of 150 = ₹30
Plan II: 30% of 90 = ₹27

Compute both numbers first. Then compare.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A quantity comparison gives you Quantity I and Quantity II. You decide which is bigger, or whether they are equal.
Step A: find the value of Quantity I completely.
Step B: find the value of Quantity II completely.
Step C: put the two numbers side by side and compare.
If a quantity is a range or has two possible values, test the extreme cases. If the comparison flips between cases, the answer is "cannot be determined".
To compare two fractions of positive numbers, cross-multiply: a/b versus c/d becomes a×d versus b×c.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Compute I and II separately, then compare. Never compare half-finished work.
a/b < c/d exactly when a×d < b×c (all numbers positive).
If I > II in one case and I < II in another: cannot be determined.

Cross-multiplying is allowed because multiplying both sides by a positive number keeps the sign (Math Is Fun; Wikipedia, see Sources). Python checked the rule on every a, b, c, d from 1 to 12.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

I: 25% of 240. II: 3/8 of 160. Compare.

Step 1. I: 25% = 1/4, and 240 ÷ 4 = 60.
Step 2. II: 160 ÷ 8 = 20, then 20 × 3 = 60.
Step 3. 60 and 60. Answer: I = II.

I: x if 3x + 5 = 20. II: y if 2y − 1 = 11. Compare.

Step 1. I: 3x = 15, so x = 5.
Step 2. II: 2y = 12, so y = 6.
Step 3. 5 < 6. Answer: I < II.

5The trap

I: 20% of 150. II: 30% of 90.
Wrong move: "30% is bigger than 20%, so II is bigger". That ignores the base.
Real values: I = 30, II = 27. So I > II.
Rule: a percentage is only half the story. Always multiply by the actual number.

6Mental-math shortcut: cross-multiply fractions

Which is bigger, 7/9 or 5/7? Multiply "upward" across: 7×7 = 49 (for 7/9) and 5×9 = 45 (for 5/7). The side with the bigger product is the bigger fraction. So 7/9 > 5/7.

Use it. Equal products mean equal fractions: 6/8 and 9/12 give 6×12 = 72 and 9×8 = 72, so they are equal.

Speed drill

Say which is bigger before you tap Show.

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Quantity I: 12.5% of 96. Quantity II: 3/8 of 32. Compare I and II."
  • "Quantity I: x if 3x + 5 = 20. Quantity II: y if 2y − 1 = 11."
  • "Quantity I: x where x² = 49. Quantity II: 5."

Typical answer format for this type: I > II, I < II, I = II, or cannot be determined (sometimes with ≥ and ≤ choices). Read the options first: the exact wording can differ between papers.

Memorise this
1. Compute I fully, compute II fully, compare.
2. Never judge by percentage alone: multiply by the base.
3. a/b vs c/d: compare a×d with b×c (positive numbers).

Lesson 3: Linking two inequalities (x versus y)

In one line: solve each statement to a boundary, then use the boundaries to link x and y, or admit it cannot be determined.

1Picture this

Asha is taller than 150 cm. Ben is shorter than 150 cm. Who is taller? Asha, without knowing either height. But if Asha is taller than 150 and Ben is ALSO taller than 150, you cannot say.

Ben: below 150Asha: above 150

A common value in the middle is the link between two statements.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Solve each inequality alone first. You get x > something and y < something.
Put the two boundary numbers side by side.
If x is above a number and y is below the SAME or a lower number, then x is above y. The boundary links them.
If both are above (or both below) the same number, there is no link. Answer: cannot be determined.
Watch equality. x ≥ 3 and y ≤ 3 gives x ≥ y only, because both could be exactly 3.
Two inequalities pointing the same way can be added: if a < b and c < d then a + c < b + d.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Transitivity: if a ≤ b and b ≤ c then a ≤ c (strict if either step is strict).
Same-direction inequalities add: a < b and c < d give a + c < b + d. (Reason: a + c < b + c < b + d.)
For positive numbers, 0 < a ≤ b gives 1/a ≥ 1/b: reciprocals reverse.
Do not multiply or divide by a variable unless you know its sign.

Transitivity, addition and positive-number reciprocal rules: Wikipedia "Inequality (mathematics)". Variable-sign caution: Math Is Fun "Solving Inequalities". The add-two-inequalities step is derived from those two rules and checked by Python.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

2x + 1 > 9 and 3y − 2 < 10. Compare x and y.

Step 1. First: 2x > 8, so x > 4.
Step 2. Second: 3y < 12, so y < 4.
Step 3. x is above 4 and y is below 4, so x > 4 > y. Answer: x > y.

x > 4 and y > 4. Compare x and y.

Step 1. Try x = 5, y = 6: x < y.
Step 2. Try x = 6, y = 5: x > y.
Step 3. The answer flips. Cannot be determined.

5The trap

Given: x ≥ 3 and y ≤ 3.
Wrong move: "x is at least 3, y is at most 3, so x > y".
Counter-example: x = 3 and y = 3. Then x = y, so x > y is false.
Right: x ≥ y. The ≥ must survive to the answer.

Second trap: reciprocals only reverse for same-sign positives. −2 < 3 but 1/(−2) = −0.5 is still < 1/3 = 0.333.

6Mental-math shortcut: boundary check

Solve each side to "x > p" or "y < q". Then compare p and q only. If x is above p, y is below q, and p ≥ q, then x > y. If p < q, you cannot tell.

Use it. x > 6, y < 4: 6 ≥ 4, so x > y. x > 2, y < 5: 2 < 5, so cannot be determined (x = 3, y = 4 vs x = 9, y = 0).

Speed drill

Say the verdict before you tap Show.

Memorise this
1. Solve each statement to x > p, y < q first.
2. p ≥ q: x > y. Otherwise: cannot be determined.
3. Both above (or both below) one number: no link.
4. x ≥ 3 and y ≤ 3 gives x ≥ y, not x > y.
5. Same-direction inequalities add. Do not subtract or multiply them.
