Start Here: exam facts and game plan

In one line: Quant is 20 questions worth 25 marks inside a 60-minute paper shared with three other sections, so you win by picking the easy questions first and guessing only when the odds are good.

1Picture this

You enter a sweet shop with ₹100 and exactly one hour. ("Quant" = Quantitative Aptitude, the maths section.) There are four counters. Quant is one counter with 20 items. Each item you get right pays ₹1.25. Each item you get wrong costs you ₹0.3125. Skipping costs nothing.

English 25Quant 25Reasoning 25GA 25

Paper 1: four sections, 25 marks each, 100 marks in total (GA = General Awareness). Quant is one quarter of Paper 1.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

The Phase I online exam is on 15 October 2026 (a Thursday). Phase I = the first screening round. It has two papers: Paper 1 (everyone) and Paper 2 (your stream subject, 50 questions, 100 marks, 40 minutes). Quant is in Paper 1.
Paper 1 has 4 sections: English 20 Q, Quantitative Aptitude 20 Q, Reasoning 30 Q, General Awareness 20 Q. That is 90 questions.
Each section carries 25 marks, so the paper is 100 marks. You get 60 minutes for the whole paper, not per section.
So a Quant question is worth 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25 marks. A wrong answer loses one quarter of that: 0.3125.
A cut-off is the minimum score you must reach. You need a separate cut-off in each paper and also an aggregate (total) cut-off. So do not ignore a section.
Phase I marks only shortlist you for Phase II (the next written round); they do not count in the final result. Your target here is a clean, accurate Quant score.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Marks per Quant question = 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25
Penalty per wrong answer = 1.25 ÷ 4 = 0.3125
Score = 1.25 × (right) − 0.3125 × (wrong). Skipped = 0.
Break-even chance (the chance of being right at which a guess neither gains nor loses on average) = 0.3125 ÷ (1.25 + 0.3125) = 0.3125 ÷ 1.5625 = 0.2 = 20%
Check this against your call letter. The official notification says "negative marking (1/4th of marks assigned to the question)". It does not print marks per question. We worked 1.25 out from its table (25 marks ÷ 20 questions), so the penalty is 0.3125. Sources differ: some coaching pages say a flat −0.25. If it is a flat −0.25, break-even is 0.25 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 16.7%. Using 20% as your guess rule is safe under both readings.

Source: official PFRDA Officer Grade A (Assistant Manager) 2026 notification, pages 6-7 (see Sources). The break-even line is our arithmetic, checked by Python.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

You attempt 14 questions right, 4 wrong, 2 skipped. Quant score?

Step 1. Right: 14 × 1.25 = 17.5.
Step 2. Wrong: 4 × 0.3125 = 1.25.
Step 3. Skipped questions add 0.
Step 4. Score = 17.5 − 1.25 = 16.25 out of 25.

Is a pure guess worth it if a question has 4 options and you know nothing?

Step 1. Chance of luck = 1 in 4 = 25%. Chance of wrong = 75%.
Step 2. Expected marks = the average result if you made this same guess many times = (chance right × 1.25) − (chance wrong × 0.3125) = 0.25 × 1.25 − 0.75 × 0.3125 = 0.3125 − 0.234375.
Step 3. = +0.078125. Tiny gain, so a blind guess is nearly worthless.
Step 4. With 5 options the chance is 20%: 0.2 × 1.25 − 0.8 × 0.3125 = 0.25 − 0.25 = exactly 0. Cross out even one option and the odds improve.

5The trap

Wrong belief: "20 questions in a section, so I have 20 minutes each section" or "I must try all 20."
Fact: 60 minutes covers all 90 questions. Average = 3600 s ÷ 90 = 40 seconds a question.
Trying everything and rushing may give, say, 12 right and 8 wrong: 12 × 1.25 = 15, 8 × 0.3125 = 2.5, so 15 − 2.5 = 12.5. Careful 10 right, 0 wrong gives 10 × 1.25 = 12.5 too, with less stress. Accuracy is cheap marks.
