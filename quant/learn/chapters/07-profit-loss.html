Profit, Loss, Discount

Three short lessons: profit and loss on cost, marked price with discounts, and dishonest weights.

Lesson 1: Profit, loss, CP and SP

In one line: profit and loss are measured against what you PAID (CP), not what you sold for.

1Picture this

Ramesh in Jaipur buys a saree from the wholesale market for ₹500. He sells it in his shop for ₹600. He paid ₹500 (cost price). He received ₹600 (selling price). The ₹100 gap is his profit.

Cost ₹500₹100

Profit ₹100 on a cost of ₹500: that is 100 out of 500, which is 20%.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

CP (cost price) is what the seller paid. SP (selling price) is what the seller gets.
If SP is more than CP, there is a profit: Profit = SP − CP.
If SP is less than CP, there is a loss: Loss = CP − SP.
Profit% and loss% answer: how big is the gain compared with the cost?
So the base (the number you divide by) is always CP. This is the same 'divide by the OLD' habit from the Percentage chapter.
To get SP from CP: a 20% profit means SP is 120% of CP, i.e. CP × 1.20. A 20% loss means SP = CP × 0.80.
To get CP back from SP, divide by the same factor: profit 20% → CP = SP ÷ 1.20. Loss 20% → CP = SP ÷ 0.80. Never "take 20% off SP".

3The rules (now that you get it)

Profit = SP − CP   |   Loss = CP − SP
Profit% = Profit ÷ CP × 100   |   Loss% = Loss ÷ CP × 100
SP = CP × (1 + p/100) for profit p%; SP = CP × (1 − l/100) for loss l%
CP = SP ÷ (1 + p/100) for profit; CP = SP ÷ (1 − l/100) for loss

Cost price and profit as selling price less cost: Wikipedia "Cost price". Percentage over cost: Wikipedia "Markup (business)" (Markup = (Sale price − Cost) ÷ Cost). Profit% = Profit ÷ CP × 100 and Loss% = Loss ÷ CP × 100: Cuemath "Profit and Loss". Note: in accounting, Wikipedia "Cost price" gives GP% as profit ÷ selling price; exam P&L questions use CP as the base unless told otherwise. CP-from-SP rows: derived, Python-checked.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

CP = ₹250, SP = ₹300. Profit%?

Step 1. Profit = 300 − 250 = 50.
Step 2. Divide by CP: 50 ÷ 250 = 1/5.
Step 3. 1/5 = 20%. Answer: 20% profit.

An item costs ₹480. It is sold at a 12.5% profit. SP?

Step 1. 12.5% = 1/8.
Step 2. Profit = 480 ÷ 8 = 60.
Step 3. SP = 480 + 60 = ₹540.
Check with factor: 480 × 1.125 = 540.

SP = ₹1,080 at a 20% profit. CP?

Step 1. SP is 120% of CP, so SP = CP × 120/100 = CP × 6/5.
Step 2. Reverse it: CP = SP × 5/6. First 1,080 ÷ 6 = 180, then 180 × 5 = 900.
Step 3. Check: 20% of 900 = 180; 900 + 180 = 1,080. Answer: ₹900.

Two articles are each sold for ₹960: one at a 20% profit, one at a 20% loss. Overall result?

Step 1. Profit article: CP = 960 ÷ 1.20 = 800.
Step 2. Loss article: CP = 960 ÷ 0.80 = 1,200.
Step 3. Total CP = 800 + 1,200 = 2,000. Total SP = 960 + 960 = 1,920.
Step 4. Loss = 2,000 − 1,920 = 80. 80 ÷ 2,000 = 4%. Answer: 4% overall loss, not "no gain, no loss".

5The trap

CP = ₹80, SP = ₹100. Profit = ₹20.
Wrong move: divide by SP. 20 ÷ 100 = 20%.
Right: divide by CP. 20 ÷ 80 = 1/4 = 25%.
Second trap: 'sold at 20% profit for ₹120, find CP'. Wrong: take 20% off 120. 20% of 120 = 24, so 96. Right: 120 is 120% of CP, so CP = 120 ÷ 1.2 = ₹100.

6Mental-math shortcut: profit% as a ratio SP : CP

Turn the percent into a fraction (the fraction table is in the Percentage chapter). Then SP : CP is simply (1 + fraction) : 1.

Lesson 2: Marked price and discount

In one line: discount comes off the tag price (MP), profit is measured on cost (CP), and two discounts multiply, they do not add.

1Picture this

A shopkeeper in Indore buys a fan for ₹1,000. He prints a tag of ₹1,500 on it (marked price). On a "20% off" sale board he sells it for ₹1,200. His profit is ₹200, which is 20% of his cost.

Pay ₹1,200₹300 off

The 20% discount is taken from the tag (₹1,500), not from his cost (₹1,000).

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

MP (marked price) is the price on the tag. It is usually above CP.
A discount is a reduction from the marked price. Discount = MP − SP.
Discount % is always on MP. Profit % is always on CP. Two different bases.
So SP = MP × (1 − d/100). A 20% discount means you pay 80% of MP.
If an article is "marked X% above cost", then MP = CP × (1 + X/100). Marked 25% above CP 800: MP = 800 × 1.25 = 1,000.
Two discounts in a row (successive) work like successive percentage changes: multiply the factors.
20% off, then 10% off: factor 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72. You pay 72%, so the total discount is 28%, not 30%.
A single equivalent discount is the one discount that does the same job as the two: here 28%. Quick formula: d₁ + d₂ − (d₁ × d₂)/100 = 20 + 10 − 2 = 28 (d₁, d₂ = the first and second discount %).

3The rules (now that you get it)

Discount = MP − SP   |   Discount% = Discount ÷ MP × 100
SP = MP × (1 − d/100)
Successive discounts d₁ and d₂: SP = MP × (1 − d₁/100) × (1 − d₂/100)
Single equivalent discount = d₁ + d₂ − (d₁ × d₂)/100

Marked price, Discount = MP − SP, Discount% on MP: Cuemath "Profit and Loss". Discounts as price reductions: Wikipedia "Discounts and allowances". Successive-discount and single-equivalent formulas: derived, Python-checked (expand 1 − (1 − d₁/100)(1 − d₂/100)).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

MP = ₹1,500, discount 20%, CP = ₹1,000. Profit%?

Step 1. 20% off means you pay 80%: SP = 1,500 × 0.80 = 1,200.
Step 2. Profit = 1,200 − 1,000 = 200.
Step 3. Divide by CP: 200 ÷ 1,000 = 20%. Answer: 20% profit.

By what % above CP must an article be marked so that after a 10% discount the profit is 8%?

Step 1. Let CP = 100 (any CP works; 100 keeps percents easy). SP for 8% profit = 108.
Step 2. SP = 0.90 × MP, so MP = 108 ÷ 0.90 = 120.
Step 3. MP 120 versus CP 100: 20% above CP.

5The trap

MP = ₹500. Discounts of 20% and 10% one after the other.
Wrong move: add them. 30% off gives 500 × 0.70 = ₹350.
Right: 500 × 0.80 = 400, then 400 × 0.90 = ₹360. Total discount 28%.
Second trap: marked 25% above CP, then 20% off. Wrong: 'profit = 25 − 20 = 5%'. Right: 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00, so no profit, no loss.

6Mental-math shortcut: discount as a fraction you pay

Do not compute the discount. Compute what you PAY: the fraction that is left.

Lesson 3: Dishonest weights

In one line: a shopkeeper who gives less than he charges for gains the missing amount, measured against what he actually gave.

1Picture this

A kirana shop in Kanpur says "1 kg" but the metal weight on the scale is really 900 g. The customer pays for 1 kg of sugar and gets 900 g. The shopkeeper keeps the missing 100 g to sell again.

900 g given100 g kept

He charged for 1000 g but handed over goods that cost him only 900 g.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Say sugar costs ₹1 per gram to the shopkeeper. (Any price works; the ratio stays the same.)
He gives 900 g, which cost him ₹900.
He charges the customer for 1000 g, so he receives ₹1,000 (selling at cost price, only the weight is false).
Profit = 1,000 − 900 = ₹100.
Divide by what the goods cost him (CP = ₹900): 100 ÷ 900 = 11.11%.
So the gain is error ÷ weight actually given, not error ÷ the claimed weight.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Seller charges for the true weight T but gives false weight W (W < T), selling at cost:
Gain% = (T − W) ÷ W × 100
If he also adds a profit of p% on top: total factor = (1 + p/100) × (T ÷ W). Gain% = factor − 1, times 100.

Derived, Python-checked: built from Profit% = Profit ÷ CP × 100 (Lesson 1, Cuemath). Not copied from a page.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

A shopkeeper uses an 800 g weight as 1 kg and sells at cost. Gain%?

Step 1. Gives 800 g, charges for 1000 g.
Step 2. Gain in weight = 200 g, on goods worth 800 g.
Step 3. 200 ÷ 800 = 1/4 = 25%.

He uses a 900 g weight as 1 kg and ALSO marks up 10% (sells at 10% above his cost). Total gain%?

Step 1. False weight alone gives a factor of 1000 ÷ 900 = 10/9.
Step 2. The 10% profit gives a factor of 1.1 = 11/10.
Step 3. Multiply: 10/9 × 11/10 = 11/9 (the 10s cancel) = 1.2222.
Step 4. Gain = 1.2222 − 1 = 0.2222 = 22.22%, not 11.11 + 10.

5The trap

A 900 g weight sold as 1 kg, at cost. Error = 100 g.
Wrong move: divide by the claimed 1000 g. 100 ÷ 1000 = 10%.
Right: divide by the weight actually given. 100 ÷ 900 = 11.11%.
Second trap: with an extra 10% profit, adding gives 21.11%. Right is 22.22% (the two gains multiply).

6Mental-math shortcut: false weight to gain% for a 1 kg claim

Gain = error ÷ false weight. Reduce the fraction first.

Weight givenErrorGain
900 g100/900 = 1/911.11%
875 g125/875 = 1/714.29%
800 g200/800 = 1/425%
750 g250/750 = 1/333.33%
500 g500/500 = 1100%
Use it. Gain of 1/9 means he gives 9 parts and keeps 1 more part: 11.11%. Reverse: gain 1/4 means claimed ÷ given = 5/4, so given = 1000 × 4/5 (1000 ÷ 5 = 200, × 4 = 800 g).
