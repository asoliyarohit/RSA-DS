Speed Maths II: calculate fast

Three lessons. Do one, then stop. Each trick is explained with algebra and was checked by Python over a range of numbers.

Lesson 1 · Chunk it, then use the multiply shortcuts

In one line: break numbers into easy chunks, and turn × 5, × 25, × 9, × 99 and × 11 into the easy moves × 10, × 100, − n and + n.

1Picture this

You buy 478 sweets for a wedding and then 356 more. Nobody adds right to left like at school. You think "478 and 300 more is 778, and 50 more is 828, and 6 more is 834". You moved left to right, one chunk at a time.

A chunk is a small piece of a number (hundreds, tens or ones). Your head holds only a few numbers at once. Small chunks keep it from dropping the thread.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Every number is hundreds, tens and ones. 478 = 400 + 70 + 8.
To add, keep the first number whole and add the second one in chunks: first the hundreds, then the tens, then the ones.
To subtract from a round number like 1,000, use 999 instead: no borrowing is needed, because each digit is just 9 minus the digit.
Then add the 1 back. 1,000 − 467 = 999 − 467 + 1.
Multiply by 5? That is half of × 10. Multiply by 25? That is a quarter of × 100. A quarter means ÷ 4, which is just halve, then halve again: 4,600 → 2,300 → 1,150.
Multiply by 9 or 99? That is × 10 or × 100, then take away one copy of the number.
Multiply by 11? That is × 10, then add one copy of the number.

3The rules (now that you get it)

n × 5 = n × 10 ÷ 2 · n × 25 = n × 100 ÷ 4
n × 9 = n × 10 − n · n × 99 = n × 100 − n
n × 11 = n × 10 + n. For a two-digit number with digits a and b (52 has a = 5, b = 2): write a, then (a + b), then b: 5, 7, 2 = 572. If a + b is 10 or more, carry 1 to the left.

Here n stands for any number. Why they work: 5 = 10 ÷ 2, 25 = 100 ÷ 4, 9 = 10 − 1, 99 = 100 − 1, 11 = 10 + 1. For two digits, (10a + b) × 11 = 100a + 10(a + b) + b.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

478 + 356

Step 1. Keep 478. Add the hundreds of 356: 478 + 300 = 778.
Step 2. Add the tens: 778 + 50 = 828.
Step 3. Add the ones: 828 + 6 = 834.

46 × 25

Step 1. × 25 means × 100 ÷ 4. 46 × 100 = 4,600.
Step 2. 4,600 ÷ 4: halve to 2,300, halve again to 1,150.

87 × 99

Step 1. × 99 means × 100 − 87. 87 × 100 = 8,700.
Step 2. 8,700 − 87: take off 100 first, 8,700 − 100 = 8,600. That was 13 too much (100 − 87), so give 13 back: 8,600 + 13 = 8,613. (Friendly-number method, chapter 01c.)

35 × 9

Step 1. × 9 means × 10 − 35. 35 × 10 = 350.
Step 2. 350 − 35 = 315.

52 × 11

Step 1. Outer digits: 5 and 2.
Step 2. Middle: 5 + 2 = 7.
Step 3. Write 5, 7, 2 = 572. (Check: 520 + 52 = 572.)

5The trap

Question: 78 × 11.
Wrong move: write 7, then 7 + 8 = 15, then 8, side by side: 7,158.
Right: 15 has two digits. Write 5, carry 1 to the 7: 8, 5, 8 = 858.
Quick sense check: 78 × 10 is 780, plus 78 is 858. The wrong 7,158 is far too large.

Lesson 2 · Near 100, squares ending in 5, and the three identities

In one line: numbers close to 50 or 100 are easy to square or multiply if you work with the small gap instead of the big number.

1Picture this

A wholesaler sells 96 packets per box and you buy 97 boxes. Both numbers are just a little short of 100. Instead of 96 × 97 the long way, you ask: how short are they? 4 short and 3 short.

964

Work with the gaps 4 and 3. They are tiny and easy.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Take 96 × 97. Both are below 100. A shortfall (gap) is how far below 100 a number is: 100 − 96 = 4 and 100 − 97 = 3.
Cross step: take one number and subtract the OTHER shortfall. 96 − 3 = 93. (97 − 4 gives the same 93.)
Multiply the shortfalls: 4 × 3 = 12.
Write the cross result, then the product as two digits: 93 then 12 = 9,312. (That is 93 × 100 + 12 = 9,300 + 12.)
If both numbers are ABOVE 100, add instead of subtract. 103 × 106: the excesses over 100 are 3 and 6. Cross: 103 + 6 = 109. Product: 3 × 6 = 18. Join: 109 × 100 + 18 = 10,900 + 18 = 10,918.
A number ending in 5 is special. 35²: take the number in front of the 5, which is 3. The next number up is 4. Multiply 3 × 4 = 12, then write 25 after it: 1,225.
Numbers near 50 or 100 can be squared by splitting them: 51 = 50 + 1. A square of a sum has a middle part: (a + b)² = a² + 2 × a × b + b². So 51² = 50² + 2 × 50 × 1 + 1² = 2,500 + 100 + 1 = 2,601. For a number just below, (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²: 49² = 2,500 − 100 + 1 = 2,401.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Near 100: (100 + x)(100 + y) = 100 × (100 + x + y) + xy. Use a negative x or y for numbers below 100. Example 96 × 97: x = −4, y = −3, so 100 × (100 − 4 − 3) + (−4) × (−3) = 9,300 + 12. (Minus times minus gives plus.)
Ends in 5: (10a + 5)² = 100 × a × (a + 1) + 25. Here a is the number in front of the 5 (for 35, a = 3; for 125, a = 12).
(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² · (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b² · (a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²

The three identities are quoted from Math Is Fun "Special Binomial Products" (see Sources). The near-100 and ending-in-5 rules are our own algebra from them, checked by Python.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

96 × 97

Step 1. Shortfalls from 100: 4 and 3.
Step 2. Cross: 96 − 3 = 93.
Step 3. Product of shortfalls: 4 × 3 = 12.
Step 4. Join: 93 and 12 = 9,312.

85²

Step 1. Tens digit is 8. Next number up is 9.
Step 2. 8 × 9 = 72.
Step 3. Write 25 after it: 7,225.

52 × 48

Step 1. Both are 2 away from 50: (50 + 2)(50 − 2).
Step 2. Use a² − b² = 2,500 − 4.
Step 3. 2,500 − 4 = 2,496. (Why: (50 + 2)(50 − 2) = 2,500 − 100 + 100 − 4. The two middle parts cancel.)

49²

Step 1. 49 = 50 − 1, so use (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b².
Step 2. a² = 50² = 2,500. 2 × 50 × 1 = 100. b² = 1.
Step 3. 2,500 − 100 + 1 = 2,401.

5The trap

Question: 98 × 97.
Shortfalls are 2 and 3. Cross: 98 − 3 = 95. Product: 2 × 3 = 6.
Wrong move: write 95 then 6, giving 956.
Right: the product part must take TWO digits. 6 becomes 06, so the answer is 9,506.
Check: 100 × 100 = 10,000 and 98 × 97 is a bit less. 956 is nowhere near.

Lesson 3 · Estimate first, then check the last digit

In one line: before you trust an answer, ask if it is the right size and if its last digit and digit sum fit. (Digit sum and digit root are from chapter 02, Lesson 3.)

1Picture this

At a kirana shop the bill shows ₹20,096 for 398 packets at ₹52. You glance: 400 packets at about ₹50 is about ₹20,000. The size is fine. But the last digit: 8 × 2 = 16, so the bill should end in 6. It does. Is it safe? A second check, the digit sum, says no. The honest bill is ₹20,696.

Three quick checks, each only a few seconds. Together they catch many slips, but not all.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Estimate: round each number to something friendly. 398 becomes 400. This tells you the size of the answer.
Last-digit check: only the last digits of the numbers decide the last digit of the product. 7 × 8 = 56, so 347 × 28 ends in 6.
It works the same for adding and subtracting last digits. For subtracting, add 10 to the top digit if it is smaller: 23 − 8 ends in 5, because 13 − 8 = 5.
Digit-sum check (casting out nines): add the digits of a number until one digit remains. 347 → 14 → 5.
Do the same operation on the digit sums. 5 × 1 (since 28 → 10 → 1) = 5. The true answer must have digit sum 5.
If the check fails, the answer is wrong for sure. If it passes, the answer may still be wrong.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Last digit of a × b = last digit of (last digit of a × last digit of b). Same for + and −.
Digit sum (to one digit, with 9 and 0 treated alike) of a × b = digit sum of (digit sum of a × digit sum of b).
Passing a check is not proof. A failed check is proof of an error.

Why the last-digit rule works: write a = 10p + r and b = 10q + s. Then ab = 10(10pq + ps + qr) + rs, so only rs affects the last digit. Why digit sums work: 10ⁿ − 1 is always divisible by 9, so a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder when divided by 9 (Wikipedia, Casting out nines).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Check that 347 × 28 = 9,716

Step 1. Estimate: 347 is about 350. 35 × 28 = 980, so 350 × 28 = 9,800. Close to 9,716: size is fine.
Step 2. Last digit: 7 × 8 = 56, so it must end in 6. 9,716 ends in 6. Good.
Step 3. Digit sums: 347 → 3 + 4 + 7 = 14 → 5. And 28 → 10 → 1. 5 × 1 = 5.
Step 4. 9,716 → 9 + 7 + 1 + 6 = 23 → 5. Matches.
Step 5. All checks pass: 9,716 is right.

Estimate 498 × 21

Step 1. 498 is nearly 500.
Step 2. 5 × 21 = 105, then add two zeros: 500 × 21 = 10,500.
Step 3. True value: 498 × 21 = 498 × 20 + 498 = 9,960 + 498 = 10,458. The estimate is off by only 42. Pick the closest option.

5The trap

Question: 347 × 28. A tempting option reads 9,176. The true answer is 9,716.
Wrong move: "it ends in 6 and its digit sum is 23 → 5, so it passes".
Both checks do pass for 9,176. They cannot see swapped digits.
Right: when two options use the same digits, do not rely on these checks. Compare the hundreds with your estimate: 350 × 28 = 9,800 means 9,716 is closer than 9,176.

7Lab: check a claimed product

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • "Find the approximate value of 603 × 49." (estimation)
  • "Which of these is the value of 398 × 52?" with four close numbers (checks)
  • Data interpretation: answer options are often close together. Estimate first, check second.
How the wrong options are built. Typical sets include a wrong decimal place, a swapped-digit look-alike, and a slip in one place value. The estimate removes the first, the checks remove most of the third.
