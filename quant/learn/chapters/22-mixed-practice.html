Mixed Practice and Exam Technique

In one line: Quant is a 20-question game where knowing which questions to do first, which to park and when to guess is worth as much as speed.

1Picture this

You are at a Sunday market with ₹100 of time. Some stalls (quadratic equations, number series) sell you a mark in 30 seconds. Others (a heavy DI set) take four minutes. A wise buyer visits the cheap stalls first and does not stand for ten minutes haggling at one.

Do-first questions · ~7 minRest · ~5 min

Suggested plan, not official: 12 minutes for 20 questions is 36 seconds each on average. The paper is 60 minutes for four sections (official).

2The routine, one tiny step at a time

Read the question once. Within about 10 seconds, label it Do, Park or Drop. This is a suggested rule, not an official one.
Do = you know the routine (for example a quadratic pair, a number series with an obvious gap).
Park = you know the topic but it needs a long calculation. Come back after the Do pile.
Drop = you do not know the topic. Guess only if you can cross out at least one option (see the lab below).
Before exact arithmetic, estimate. Cross out options that are far from your estimate.
For a DI set: look at the table once, then answer the easiest two or three questions of that set first.
After every mock, fill the error log (card 7). The log is how you get better, not the score.

3The rules (now that you get it)

Marks: +1.25 for a right answer, −0.3125 for a wrong one, 0 for a skip (official).
Two-touch rule (suggested): if you have worked on a question twice and are still not near any option, park it.
Elimination rule (suggested): estimate first; strike every option that is more than your rounding error away.

Marking scheme: PFRDA 2026 notification (pfrda.org.in/documents/33652/212847/Recruitment+of+Officer+Grade+A+(Assistant+Manager)-2026.pdf, pp.6-7). The two rules above are our own coaching suggestions, not exam rules.

4Worked example: estimation eliminates options

38% of 1,210 is closest to: 360, 460, 560, 660?

Step 1. Round: 38% is close to 40%; 1,210 is close to 1,200.
Step 2. 10% of 1,200 = 120, so 40% = 480.
Step 3. 38% is a bit less than 40%, so the answer is a bit less than 480.
Step 4. Only 460 is a bit below 480. 360 is too low, 560 and 660 are too high. Exact check: 0.38 × 1,210 = 459.8.

Skip decision: a block of 5 questions. Suppose the average time budget is 36 s per question.

Step 1. Block budget = 5 × 36 s = 180 s = 3 minutes.
Step 2. If one question has used 4 minutes (240 s), it has eaten 240 ÷ 36 ≈ 6.7 questions' worth of time.
Step 3. Park it earlier. The marks from that one question (1.25) are the same as an easy one.

5The trap

You are on a long division and you "almost have it". You have spent 4 minutes.
Wrong move: "I have come this far, I must finish." That 4 minutes could have bought about 6 average questions (240 ÷ 36 = 6.7).
Right: write the question number in your mind as "Parked", move on, and come back only after the Do pile is empty.

Second trap: guessing blind with 5 options has expected gain exactly 0 (see the lab). It only pays once an option is crossed out.

9Quiz · two mini mocks, exam-style marks

Right = +1.25, wrong = −0.3125 (official). Each set has 20 questions in 4 blocks of 5. Do one block, then stop and rest. Try the Do / Park / Drop labels while you go. Data in the tables is invented.

Option count: most questions have 4 options (A to D). The 5 quadratic questions (Blocks A2 and A3) use the usual 5-option format: A x > y, B x < y, C x ≥ y, D x ≤ y, E no relation. Pick the strongest relation that holds for every pair: if x > y always holds, choose A, not C (C is true but weaker). Choose E only when no option holds for every pair.
