Speed Maths I: tables, squares, cubes

Three lessons: (1) tables, squares, cubes, primes, (2) divisibility rules, (3) a digit check for your answers. One lesson per sitting, then stop.

Lesson 1: Tables, squares, cubes, primes

In one line: own a small set of number facts so your brain is free for the real question.

1Picture this

You are at a Kanpur sweet shop. Laddoos come in boxes of 12 and you need 7 boxes for a wedding. If you know "7 twelves" without counting, you are done in a second. Speed maths is just that: facts you already own, so your brain is free for the actual question.

A table is not 20 sums. It is one fact repeated: add the same number again and again.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Tables, squares, cubes and primes are the raw material of every later chapter. Learn them once, in small chunks.
Tables 1 to 10 first. You probably know most. Mark only the ones that make you pause.
For 11 to 20 use a split: 17 × 6 = 10 × 6 + 7 × 6 = 60 + 42 = 102. Ten-part is free, units-part is a small table.
A square is a number times itself: 13² = 13 × 13 = 169. Learn 1 to 30 in three blocks of ten.
Neighbouring squares are linked: (n+1)² = n² + 2n + 1. So 14² = 13² + 2×13 + 1 = 169 + 27 = 196.
A cube is a number times itself three times: 5³ = 125. You only need 1 to 15.
A prime has no divisors other than 1 and itself. There are exactly 25 below 100.

3The rules (now that you get it)

a × (10 + b) = a × 10 + a × b (split the table)
(n + 1)² = n² + 2n + 1
Prime: a natural number greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself. So 1 is NOT prime, and 2 is the only even prime.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Find 17 × 13.

Step 1. Split 13 = 10 + 3.
Step 2. 17 × 10 = 170.
Step 3. 17 × 3 = 51.
Step 4. 170 + 51 = 221.

Find 14² using 13² = 169.

Step 1. 14 = 13 + 1, so 14² = 13² + 2 × 13 + 1.
Step 2. 2 × 13 = 26.
Step 3. 169 + 26 + 1 = 196.

5The trap

Question: which of 51, 57, 87, 91 are prime? They all look prime, because none is in a table you know well.
Wrong move: "they are odd and not in the 2, 5, 10 tables, so prime". That marks all four prime.
Right: 51 = 3 × 17, 57 = 3 × 19, 87 = 3 × 29, 91 = 7 × 13. None is prime. Also remember 1 is not prime.
Habit: before calling a number prime, test 2, 3, 5, 7. If the number is below 121, nothing else is needed.

6Mental-math shortcut: learn in chunks, then drill

One chunk per sitting: squares 1-10 today, 11-20 tomorrow, and so on. Then one drill. Stop after one. Spread out beats crammed.

Use it. Squares ending in 5 are quick: 25² = 625. Neighbour trick: 24² = 25² − 49 = 576 (since (n−1)² = n² − 2n + 1 gives 625 − 50 + 1).

Lesson 2: Divisibility rules

In one line: decide whether a number splits evenly by looking at a few digits, with no division.

1Picture this

You have 7,284 loose rupee coins from a temple collection and want to split them equally into 4 bags, then 6, then 9. Dividing each time is slow. Divisibility rules answer "will it split with nothing left over?" by looking at a few digits only.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A number is divisible by 4 if it splits into 4 equal parts with no remainder.
Every number is a hundreds-part plus the last two digits. The hundreds-part is a multiple of 100, and 100 is a multiple of 4, so it is always fine. Only the last two digits matter. That is the rule for 4.
Same idea for 8: 1000 is a multiple of 8, so only the last three digits matter.
For 2 and 5 only the last digit matters, because 10 is a multiple of both.
For 3 and 9: each power of 10 leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3 or 9. So a number leaves the same remainder as its digit sum.
For 11: 10 leaves remainder −1 on dividing by 11, so digits alternate +, −. Use the alternating digit sum.
6 is just 2 and 3 together. Test both.

3The rules (now that you get it)

ByTest
2last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8
3digit sum divisible by 3
4last two digits divisible by 4
5last digit is 0 or 5
6divisible by 2 and by 3
8last three digits divisible by 8
9digit sum divisible by 9
11alternating digit sum (odd places minus even places) divisible by 11, zero included

Rules and reasons (100 is divisible by 4, 1000 by 8, 10 leaves remainder 1 for 3 and 9, and −1 for 11): Wikipedia "Divisibility rule". Rules also Python-checked for every number below 100,000.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Is 7,284 divisible by 4, 3, 9, 11?

Step 1 (by 4). Last two digits 84. 84 ÷ 4 = 21, so yes.
Step 2 (by 3). Digit sum 7+2+8+4 = 21. 21 is a multiple of 3, so yes.
Step 3 (by 9). Digit sum 21 is not a multiple of 9, so no.
Step 4 (by 11). From the right: 4 − 8 + 2 − 7 = −9. Not a multiple of 11, so no.

Is 2,728 divisible by 11?

Step 1. From the right: 8 − 2 + 7 − 2 = 11.
Step 2. 11 is a multiple of 11, so yes. (2,728 = 11 × 248.)

5The trap

Question: is 116 divisible by 8?
Wrong move: use the rule for 4 (last two digits). 16 ÷ 8 = 2, so "yes". But 116 ÷ 8 = 14.5.
Right: for 8 you must look at the last three digits, here 116 itself. 116 ÷ 8 is not whole, so no.
Second trap: 12 is divisible by 4 and by 6, but not by 24. Combine rules only for numbers with no shared factor, like 2 and 3 for 6. For 8 do not test 2 and 4.

6Mental-math shortcut: test, do not divide

Order of tests: last digit first (2, 5), then digit sum (3, 9), then last two digits (4), then 11. The quickest test first.

Use it. To check a big sum is a multiple of 9, add the digits. 4,725: 4+7+2+5 = 18, so yes. No division at all.

Speed drill

Say yes or no before you tap Show. Aim: under 8 seconds each.

Lesson 3: Casting out nines

In one line: shrink every number to one digit and use those digits to catch most calculation mistakes.

1Picture this

A shopkeeper in Patna multiplies 487 × 36 on a calculator-less bill and gets 17,532. His assistant says "are you sure?". A 10-second check using only single digits can catch most slips, without redoing the sum.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Shrink any number to one digit by adding its digits again and again until one digit remains. That is its digit root (textbooks call it the "digital root"). 487 → 4+8+7 = 19 → 1+9 = 10 → 1.
A number and its digit sum leave the same remainder on division by 9. So the digit root is the remainder when you divide by 9 (with 0 written as 9 for non-zero multiples of 9).
So adding and multiplying work on the roots too. Add the roots of the inputs, shrink, and compare with the root of your answer.
If the roots differ, there is a definite mistake somewhere. If they match, the answer is probably right, but not certain.
Why not certain? Different wrong answers can share the same root, for example when two digits swap places.

3The rules (now that you get it)

root(a + b) = root( root(a) + root(b) )
root(a × b) = root( root(a) × root(b) )
Roots differ → answer is wrong. Roots match → likely right (not proof).

Method and its limits (cannot detect swapped digits): Wikipedia "Casting out nines". Both identities checked by Python on hundreds of number pairs.

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Check 487 × 36 = 17,532.

Step 1. Root of 487: 4+8+7 = 19 → 1.
Step 2. Root of 36: 3+6 = 9.
Step 3. Multiply roots: 1 × 9 = 9.
Step 4. Root of 17,532: 1+7+5+3+2 = 18 → 9. Match, so the answer passes.

Check 3,456 + 2,789 = 6,345.

Step 1. Root of 3,456: 3+4+5+6 = 18 → 9.
Step 2. Root of 2,789: 2+7+8+9 = 26 → 8.
Step 3. 9 + 8 = 17 → 8.
Step 4. Root of 6,345: 6+3+4+5 = 18 → 9. Not 8, so wrong. The true sum is 6,245 (root 8).

5The trap

Someone writes 487 × 36 = 17,352 (digits 5 and 3 swapped).
Root of 17,352: 1+7+3+5+2 = 18 → 9. Same as the root of the true answer, 9.
Wrong move: "roots match, so correct". But 17,352 is wrong; the true answer is 17,532.
Right: a match never proves correctness. Add a second check, such as the last digit or a rough estimate (487 × 36 is about 500 × 36 = 18,000).

6Mental-math shortcut: cast out the nines as you add

While adding digits you may skip any 9, and any digits that add to 9. 487: no digits pair up to 9 here, so 4+8+7 = 19 → 1. For 4,725: 4+5 = 9, skip; 7+2 = 9, skip; root is 9 (zero left, written 9).

Use it. Use the check as a safety net on long multiplications, but only when two answer options have different roots. If two options share a root, check the last digit instead.

7Lab: casting-out-nines checker

8How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. Not copied from any paper.

  • The examiner does not ask for casting out nines. It is your private checker for products, sums and approximation questions.
  • Typical options sit close together, such as 17,532 / 17,352 / 17,523 / 17,253. Roots: 9, 9, 9, 9, so the check cannot separate them. Then use the last digit and an estimate.
Use it wisely. For random errors, about 1 in 9 slip through on average (Wikipedia, "Casting out nines"), so treat a match as "probably".
