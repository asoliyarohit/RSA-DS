Head-Math Rescue

In one line: stop doing sums in your head, and write one small step at a time, so you never have to hold a number in your memory.

1Picture this

Carrying a full tray of chai across a room is hard. Carrying one cup at a time is easy. Mental maths is the full tray: you hold the answer from step 1 while doing step 2. Written steps are one cup at a time: put it down on paper, then pick up the next.

You are not broken. Working memory = the brain's "notepad" that holds a number for a few seconds while you use it. A page from Understood.org says that when working memory is weak, students "have to hold on to information, like a formula, an answer from a previous step" and can "get lost in the problem". (It describes children, so use it as general context.) The fix is to need less memory, not more effort.

2Four methods, one tap at a time

Method 1: Left-to-right addition. 348 + 735. Split 735 into 700 + 30 + 5. Write 348. Add the hundreds: 348 + 700 = 1048. Add the tens: + 30 = 1078. Add the ones: + 5 = 1083. One new number is written at each step.
Method 2: Friendly number. A friendly number is a round one, ending in 0. 621 − 49. Round 49 up to 50 (that is 1 too many). 621 − 50 = 571. You took away 1 too much, so give 1 back: 572. (A Manitoba Education guide shows the same idea: 850 − 375 = 850 − 350 − 25.)
Method 3: Complement from 1000. The complement is what you add to a number to reach 1000. 1000 − 372. Take each digit from 9: 9−3=6, 9−7=2, 9−2=7 gives 627. Add 1: 628. (This is the same as 999 − 372 + 1, and 999 never needs borrowing.) For a 2-digit number like 45, write it as 045 first: 954, then 955.
Method 4: Count up. 812 − 575. Hop 575 to 600 = 25. Hop 600 to 800 = 200. Hop 800 to 812 = 12. Add the hops: 25 + 200 + 12 = 237. Write each hop.
Estimate first (add and subtract only). Round both numbers to the nearest hundred, do the quick sum, then check your exact answer is near it. Example: 348 + 735 ≈ 300 + 700 = 1000, and 1083 is near 1000, so it looks right. We did not verify estimating for multiplication: rounding can be far off, so use it only as a size check there.

3Why this works, and the formula

a + b = a + (hundreds of b) + (tens of b) + (ones of b)
a − b = a − (b rounded up) + (the extra you rounded)
1000 − n = (999 − n) + 1

All four methods are plain place-value algebra. We checked each on 20,000 random cases in Python (research/mental_math_verify.py). Labelled: derived, Python-checked.

4The trap

Trap: doing 812 − 575 "in your head" and keeping 3 numbers alive at once.
The slip: you forget a hop (drop the 12 and say 225), or slip a ten while adding 25 + 12 in your head (say 227 or 247) instead of 237.
The fix: write the hops. Never carry more than the one number you are looking at.

6How the 12 days go

DayFocus (10 to 20 min)
1-2Method 1, then add Method 2
3-4Methods 2, 3, 4
5-7Mixed, then Estimate first; redo yesterday's misses
8-11Mixed with all four, 15 to 20 min
1210 minutes light, then rest

Routine each day (from the CDC, NHS and Australian education-research pages we fetched): same time every day, a 15 to 20 minute slot with a break, write a short plan (to-do list) first, recall yesterday's method from memory, redo yesterday's wrong ones. Spaced recall (testing yourself again after a gap of a day or more) beats re-reading. Printable drills with answer keys for all 12 days are in research/mental-math-plan.md in this repo.

Timers and body-doubling (working while another person sits with you): we found no fetched evidence either way. Treat them as personal experiments, not advice. A phone timer is simply how you keep to the 15 to 20 minute slot.

7How the examiner asks

Typical style, written by us. No exam asks "use the count-up method"; these steps hide inside bigger questions.

  • Simplification: "What will come in place of the question mark? 348 + 735 − 49 = ?"
  • Inside DI: "What is the difference between the sales in 2024 and 2023?" (a subtraction like 812 − 575).
  • Totals: "Find the total of the three values" (left-to-right addition).

Memorise this
1. Write one step at a time. Never hold two numbers.
2. Add: hundreds, then tens, then ones.
3. Subtract: round up, subtract, give back the extra.
4. 1000 − n = (999 − n) + 1.
5. Count up in hops, write each hop.
