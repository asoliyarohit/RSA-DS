Permutation, Combination, Probability

In one line: first learn to COUNT ways (multiply stages), then chance is just "ways I want ÷ all ways".

Three lessons. Do one lesson, then stop. Lesson 1: arrangements (order matters). Lesson 2: selections (order does not matter). Lesson 3: probability. Lesson 1 · Counting and arrangements (permutations)

In one line: to count ways, split the job into stages and multiply the choices at each stage.

1Picture this

You are packing for a trip from Lucknow. You own 3 shirts and 4 trousers. Every shirt can go with every trouser. How many different outfits?

Shirt 1: 4Shirt 2: 4Shirt 3: 4

Each shirt has 4 trouser partners. 4 + 4 + 4 = 12, which is 3 × 4.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

Break the job into stages. Stage 1: pick a shirt (3 ways). Stage 2: pick a trouser (4 ways).
When each stage can be done in its own number of ways, multiply the numbers. 3 × 4 = 12.
Now arrange 3 people (A, B, C) in 3 chairs. Chair 1: 3 choices. Chair 2: 2 left. Chair 3: 1 left.
So 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 arrangements. That "multiply down to 1" is called a factorial, written 3!.
A permutation is an ordered arrangement. "Gold, silver" is different from "silver, gold".
Choose and arrange only r out of n (n = how many things you pick from, r = how many places you fill): multiply just r terms, starting at n and counting down. This is written nPr, so 10P2 means "arrange 2 out of 10".

3The rules (now that you get it)

Counting principle: multiply the number of ways at each stage.
n! = n × (n−1) × … × 2 × 1, and by convention 0! = 1
nPr = n! ÷ (n−r)! (order matters, no repeats)
Repeats allowed, r places: n × n × … = nr (n multiplied by itself r times, e.g. 104 = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10)

Definitions and formulas: Math Is Fun "Combinations and Permutations" (see Sources).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

10 people run a race. In how many ways can first and second place be filled?

Step 1. Order matters (1st is not 2nd). So this is a permutation: 10P2.
Step 2. First place: 10 choices. Second place: 9 left.
Step 3. 10 × 9 = 90.
Check with the formula: 10! ÷ 8! = 10 × 9 = 90 (the 8 × 7 × … × 1 on top cancels the 8! below).

A 4-digit PIN (the secret number code for a bank card) uses digits 0 to 9. How many PINs if no digit may repeat?

Step 1. Digit 1: 10 choices. Digit 2: 9. Digit 3: 8. Digit 4: 7.
Step 2. 10 × 9 = 90, then 90 × 8 = 720, then 720 × 7 = 5,040 (that is 10P4).
Step 3. If repeats were allowed: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000.

5The trap

Question: no-repeat 4-digit PIN from digits 0 to 9.
Wrong move: write 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. That lets a digit be used again.
Right: each used digit leaves the pool, so 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5,040. Always ask: "can the same thing be used twice?"

Lesson 2 · Selections (combinations)

In one line: a combination is a selection where order does not matter, so divide the arrangements by r!.

1Picture this

Four friends (A, B, C, D; you are A) want 2 of the group to fetch chai. Choosing "A and B" is the same as choosing "B and A". The same two people go.

ABACADBCBDCD

Only 6 different pairs, not 12.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

A combination is a choice where the order does not matter. nCr means "the number of ways to choose r things out of n".
First count as if order mattered: 4P2 = 4 × 3 = 12.
But each pair got counted 2 times (AB and BA). 2 = 2!.
So divide: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 pairs. In general, nCr = nPr ÷ r!.
Choosing r to take is the same as choosing n−r to leave. So nCr = nC(n−r). Use whichever r is smaller.
Several groups with separate conditions: do each group's selection, then multiply (counting principle).

3The rules (now that you get it)

nCr = n! ÷ [r! × (n−r)!] = nPr ÷ r!
nCr = nC(n−r) (swap r and n−r in the formula and it does not change)
"At least one" = (all selections) − (selections with none)

Combination definition and formula: Math Is Fun "Combinations and Permutations". The "at least one" subtraction is the same idea as the "easier to work out the No case" trick on Math Is Fun "Conditional Probability" (see Sources).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

A committee of 3 is chosen from 5 men and 4 women, exactly 2 men and 1 woman. How many ways?

Step 1. Choose 2 men from 5: 5C2 = (5 × 4) ÷ (2 × 1) = 10.
Step 2. Choose 1 woman from 4: 4C1 = 4 (four people, any one of them).
Step 3. Both stages happen, so multiply: 10 × 4 = 40.

Same 5 men and 4 women, committee of 3 with at least one woman?

Step 1. All committees of 3 from 9 people: 9C3 = (9 × 8 × 7) ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 504 ÷ 6 = 84.
Step 2. Committees with NO woman (all men): 5C3 = 5C2 (swap rule) = 10.
Step 3. At least one woman = 84 − 10 = 74.
Check by adding cases. 1 woman: 4C1 × 5C2 = 4 × 10 = 40. 2 women: 4C2 × 5C1 = 6 × 5 = 30. 3 women: 4C3 × 5C0 = 4 × 1 = 4. Total 40 + 30 + 4 = 74.

5The trap

Question: choose a team of 3 from 9 people.
Wrong move: 9 × 8 × 7 = 504. That counts "A, B, C" and "C, B, A" as different teams.
Right: each team is counted 3! = 6 times, so 504 ÷ 6 = 84. If order does not matter, divide by r!.

Lesson 3 · Probability

In one line: probability = (ways the thing can happen) ÷ (all possible outcomes), always between 0 and 1.

1Picture this

In a game of Ludo you roll one die. Six faces: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. You need a 6 to bring a token out. One face helps you out of six.

5 faces: no6

Chance of a 6 = 1 out of 6 = 1/6.

2The idea, one tiny step at a time

List every possible result of the experiment. This list is the sample space. One die (plural: dice) has faces {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. "Fair" means every result is equally likely. P(A) is short for "the probability of event A".
An event is one or more of those results. "Even number" = {2, 4, 6}.
Probability = number of ways the event can happen ÷ total number of outcomes. Even: 3 ÷ 6 = 1/2.
It is always from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
Not: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Often it is easier to count the "No" case and subtract from 1.
And for independent events (one does not change the other, like two coin tosses): multiply. Or for events that cannot happen together (mutually exclusive): add. Drawing "without replacement" (not putting the item back) changes the next chance.
Coin example: P(head) = 1/2. Two tosses both heads: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Three tosses all heads: 1/8.

3The rules (now that you get it)

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes
P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
Independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Mutually exclusive: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Otherwise: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Math Is Fun pages on Probability, Independent Events, Mutually Exclusive Events and Conditional Probability (see Sources).

4Worked examples, one tap per step

Two fair dice are rolled. Probability that the sum is 7?

Step 1. Sample space: 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs.
Step 2. Sum 7: (1,6) (2,5) (3,4) (4,3) (5,2) (6,1). That is 6 pairs.
Step 3. 6 ÷ 36, divide top and bottom by 6: 1/6.

A bag has 4 red, 3 blue, 2 green balls. Two are drawn without putting back. Probability both are red?

Step 1. Total 9 balls. First red: 4/9.
Step 2. A red is gone: 3 red left among 8 balls. Second red: 3/8.
Step 3. Multiply: 4/9 × 3/8 = 12/72. Divide top and bottom by 12: 1/6.
Check with counting: 4C2 ÷ 9C2 = 6 ÷ 36 = 1/6.

A card is drawn from a pack of 52. Probability it is a heart or a king? (A pack has 4 suits: hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades, 13 cards each. It has 4 kings and 4 queens.)

Step 1. Hearts: 13. Kings: 4. The king of hearts is in both lists.
Step 2. Cards that are heart or king: 13 + 4 − 1 = 16.
Step 3. 16 ÷ 52, divide top and bottom by 4: 4/13.

5The trap

Question: two dice, probability the sum is 7.
Wrong move: "sums go from 2 to 12, that is 11 results, so 1/11". But the 11 sums are not equally likely.
Right: list the 36 equally likely pairs. Sum 7 appears 6 times: 6/36 = 1/6. Count outcomes that are equally likely, not labels.
